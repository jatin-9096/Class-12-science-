Definite integrals and their properties

Definite Integral – Definition

The definite integral of a continuous function $f(x)$ over $[a,b]$ is the limit of Riemann sums and is denoted by $\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx$. It represents the signed area under the curve between $x=a$ and $x=b$. The unit is "unit of $f$ × unit of $x$" (e.g., $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}$ for velocity vs. time).

Fundamental Properties

PropertyMathematical Form
Linearity$\int_{a}^{b} [\alpha f(x)+\beta g(x)]\,dx = \alpha\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx + \beta\int_{a}^{b} g(x)\,dx$
Additivity$\int_{a}^{c} f(x)\,dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx + \int_{b}^{c} f(x)\,dx$
Reversal of Limits$\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx = -\int_{b}^{a} f(x)\,dx$
ComparisonIf $f(x)\ge g(x)$ on $[a,b]$, then $\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx \ge \int_{a}^{b} g(x)\,dx$

Key Formula – Evaluation using Antiderivative

If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then $\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$ (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus).

Worked Example

Evaluate $\int_{0}^{2} (3x^{2}+2x)\,dx$.

  • Find antiderivative: $F(x)=\int (3x^{2}+2x)dx = 3\cdot\frac{x^{3}}{3}+2\cdot\frac{x^{2}}{2}=x^{3}+x^{2}$.
  • Apply limits: $\int_{0}^{2} (3x^{2}+2x)dx = F(2)-F(0)= (2^{3}+2^{2})-(0+0)=8+4=12$.

Hence the signed area equals $12$ (units of $f\times x$).

Fundamental theorem of calculus

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)

FTC connects differentiation and integration. It has two parts.

  • First Part: If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\,dt$, then $F$ is differentiable on $(a,b)$ and $F'(x)=f(x)$.
  • Second Part: If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a,b]$, then $\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx = F(b)-F(a)$.

These results allow evaluation of definite integrals by finding antiderivatives.

NotationResult
$F(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\,dt$$F'(x)=f(x)$
$\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx$$F(b)-F(a)$ where $F' = f$

Derivation (First Part)

Using the limit definition of derivative:

$$F'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\int_{a}^{x+h}f(t)\,dt-\int_{a}^{x}f(t)\,dt}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\int_{x}^{x+h}f(t)\,dt}{h}=f(x)$$

Continuity of $f$ justifies the last step.

SI Units

If $f$ has unit $U$ and $x$ has unit $L$, then $\int f(x)\,dx$ has unit $U\cdot L$ (e.g., $\text{m/s}\times\text{s}=\text{m}$).

Example

Evaluate $\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin x\,dx$.

Antiderivative $F(x) = -\cos x$. Using the second part:

$$\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin x\,dx = [-\cos x]_{0}^{\pi}=(-\cos\pi)-(-\cos0)=(-(-1))-(-1)=2.$$

Thus the area under $\sin x$ from $0$ \to $\pi$ is $2$ (unit $U\cdot L$).

Integration by substitution

Definition: Integration by substitution (also called $u$‑substitution) is a technique to evaluate integrals of the form $\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx$ by converting them into a simpler integral in the new variable $u$ where $u=g(x)$.

Formula:

Original IntegralSubstituted Form
$\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx$$\int f(u)\,du$

Derivation: Let $u=g(x)\Rightarrow du=g'(x)\,dx$. Replacing $g'(x)dx$ by $du$ transforms the integral directly.

Steps:

  • Identify a function $u=g(x)$ whose derivative $g'(x)$ appears (or can be created) in the integrand.
  • Express $du=g'(x)\,dx$ and replace $g'(x)dx$ with $du$.
  • Rewrite the entire integrand in terms of $u$ and $du$.
  • Integrate with respect to $u$.
  • Substitute back $u=g(x)$ (or adjust limits for definite integrals).

Definite Integral Rule: $\int_{a}^{b} f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du$.

SI Unit: The unit of a definite integral equals the unit of the integrand multiplied by the unit of the variable of integration (e.g., $\text{m}\cdot\text{s}$ for $\int v\,dt$).

Common Pitfall: If $g'(x)$ is not explicitly present, multiply and divide the integrand by the required factor to create $du$.

Example (Indefinite):

Evaluate $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$.

Solution: Choose $u=x^2\Rightarrow du=2x\,dx$. Then

$\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx = \int \cos(u)\,du = \sin(u)+C = \sin(x^2)+C$.

Example (Definite):

Compute $\int_{0}^{1} 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$.

With $u=x^2$, limits change: $u(0)=0$, $u(1)=1$. Hence

$\int_{0}^{1} 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx = \int_{0}^{1} \cos(u)\,du = \sin(u)\big|_{0}^{1}=\sin 1-\sin 0=\sin 1$.

Integration by parts

Definition: Integration by parts is a technique derived from the product rule of differentiation, used to evaluate integrals of the form $\int f(x)g'(x)dx$ where the integrand is a product of two functions.

Let $u=f(x)$ and $dv=g'(x)dx$. Then $du=f'(x)dx$ and $v=\int g'(x)dx$. The fundamental formula is

$$\\int u\\,dv = uv - \\int v\\,du$$

Key points:

  • Choose $u$ such that $du$ simplifies the integral.
  • Choose $dv$ whose antiderivative $v$ is easily obtainable.
  • Repeated application may be required for higher powers.
  • If the resulting integral is more complicated, revert \to another method.

SI‑unit note: If $x$ has unit $U_x$ and $f(x)$ has unit $U_f$, then $\int f(x)dx$ carries unit $U_f\times U_x$.

Example: Evaluate $\int x e^{x}dx$.

Choose $u=x \;\Rightarrow\; du=dx$ and $dv=e^{x}dx \;\Rightarrow\; v=e^{x}$.

Apply the formula:

$$\\int x e^{x}dx = x e^{x} - \\int e^{x}dx$$

The remaining integral is $\int e^{x}dx = e^{x}$, so

$$\\int x e^{x}dx = x e^{x} - e^{x} + C$$

or $\boxed{e^{x}(x-1)+C}$.

Improper integrals

Improper Integrals

An integral is called improper when either the interval of integration is unbounded or the integrand becomes unbounded at some point in the interval.

Two Types

  • Infinite limits: $\\int_{a}^{\\infty} f(x)\\,dx$ or $\\int_{-\\infty}^{b} f(x)\\,dx$.
  • Integrand singularity: $\\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\\,dx$ where $f(x)$ has a vertical asymptote at $c\\in(a,b)$.

Convergence Criteria

  • Limit definition: $\\int_{a}^{\\infty} f(x)\\,dx = \\lim_{t\\to\\infty} \\int_{a}^{t} f(x)\\,dx$; converges if the limit exists finite.
  • p‑test: $\\int_{1}^{\\infty} \\frac{1}{x^{p}}\\,dx$ converges iff $p>1$; $\\int_{0}^{1} \\frac{1}{x^{p}}\\,dx$ converges iff $p<1$.
  • Comparison test: If $0\\le f(x)\\le g(x)$ and $\\int g$ converges, then $\\int f$ converges.

Key Formulas

IntegralResult (Convergent)
$\\int_{a}^{\\infty} e^{-k x}\\,dx$$\\frac{e^{-k a}}{k}$, $k>0$
$\\int_{0}^{\\infty} \\frac{dx}{(x^2+1)}$$\\frac{\\pi}{2}$

Example

Evaluate $\\int_{1}^{\\infty} \\frac{1}{x^{2}}\\,dx$.

Solution:

  1. Write as limit: $\\displaystyle \\lim_{t\\to\\infty} \\int_{1}^{t} x^{-2}\\,dx$.
  2. Integrate: $\\int x^{-2}\\,dx = -x^{-1}$.
  3. Apply limits: $\\displaystyle \\lim_{t\\to\\infty} \left[-\\frac{1}{x}\right]_{1}^{t}= \\lim_{t\\to\\infty} \left(-\\frac{1}{t}+1\right)=1$.

The integral converges to $1$, showing that a $p$‑value of $2>1$ satisfies the p‑test.

Application of definite integrals – area under a curve and volume of solids of revolution

Definition: The definite integral $\\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\\,dx$ represents the signed area between the curve $y=f(x)$ and the $x$‑axis from $x=a$ to $x=b$. When $f(x)\\ge0$, it equals the geometric area (SI unit $m^{2}$).

Key Formulas

QuantityFormula
Area under a single curve$\\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\\,dx$
Area between two curves $y=f(x)$ and $y=g(x)$$\\int_{a}^{b} [f(x)-g(x)]\\,dx$
Volume (Disc/Washer) about $x$‑axis$\\pi \\int_{a}^{b} [R(x)]^{2}\\,dx$
Volume (Shell) about $y$‑axis$2\\pi \\int_{a}^{b} r(x)h(x)\\,dx$

Derivation (Disc Method) Rotating a thin strip of width $dx$ at position $x$ gives a disc of radius $R(x)$. Its volume $dV=\\pi [R(x)]^{2}dx$. Summing from $a$ to $b$ yields $V=\\pi\\int_{a}^{b}[R(x)]^{2}dx$.

SI Units: Area – $m^{2}$; Volume – $m^{3}$.

Example (Area between curves)

Find the area enclosed by $y=2x$ and $y=x^{2}$.

  • Intersection: $x^{2}=2x \\Rightarrow x=0,2$.
  • Upper curve $y=2x$, lower $y=x^{2}$ on $[0,2]$.
  • Area $=\\int_{0}^{2} (2x-x^{2})\\,dx = \\left[x^{2}-\\frac{x^{3}}{3}\\right]_{0}^{2}=4-\\frac{8}{3}=\\frac{4}{3}$ $\\text{units}^{2}$.

Numerical integration – trapezoidal rule and Simpson’s rule

Definition: The trapezoidal rule approximates $\int_{a}^{b}f(x)dx$ by the area of a trapezoid formed with the end‑point ordinates $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. It is useful when $f(x)$ is continuous and reasonably linear over $[a,b]$.

Single‑interval formula: $I_{T}=\frac{b-a}{2}\left[f(a)+f(b)\right]$ (area in square units, e.g., $m^{2}$ if $x$ is in metres).

Composite trapezoidal rule (n equal sub‑intervals, $h=\frac{b-a}{n}$): $$I_{T}=\frac{h}{2}\left[f(x_{0})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f(x_{i})+f(x_{n})\right]$$

Simpson’s 1/3 rule (single pair of sub‑intervals, $h=\frac{b-a}{2}$): $I_{S}=\frac{h}{3}\left[f(a)+4f\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)+f(b)\right]$.

Composite Simpson’s 1/3 rule (n even): $$I_{S}=\frac{h}{3}\left[f(x_{0})+4\sum_{i=1,\,odd}^{n-1}f(x_{i})+2\sum_{i=2,\,even}^{n-2}f(x_{i})+f(x_{n})\right]$$

Error terms:

  • Trapezoidal: $E_{T}=-\frac{(b-a)^{3}}{12n^{2}}\,f''(\xi)$, $\xi\in(a,b)$.
  • Simpson’s: $E_{S}=-\frac{(b-a)^{5}}{180n^{4}}\,f^{(4)}(\xi)$, $\xi\in(a,b)$.

Steps for numerical integration:

  1. Choose $n$ (even for Simpson’s).
  2. Compute step size $h$.
  3. Tabulate $x_{i}=a+ih$ and $f(x_{i})$.
  4. Apply the appropriate formula.
  5. Estimate error using the above expressions.

Example (Composite Simpson’s, $n=4$): Evaluate $\int_{0}^{2}x^{3}dx$.

Here $h=\frac{2-0}{4}=0.5$, $x_{i}=0,0.5,1,1.5,2$ and $f(x_{i})=0,0.125,1,3.375,8$.

$$I_{S}=\frac{0.5}{3}\left[0+4(0.125+3.375)+2(1)+8\right]=\frac{0.5}{3}\times24=4$$

Exact value $=\left[\frac{x^{4}}{4}\right]_{0}^{2}=4$, confirming the high accuracy of Simpson’s rule for polynomial integrands.

Evaluation of integrals using reduction formulas

Definition: A reduction formula expresses an integral $I_n$ in terms of another integral with a lower (or higher) integer parameter, typically $I_{n-2}$ or $I_{n-1}$. It is derived by integration by parts or trigonometric identities and is indispensable for evaluating $\int x^n e^{ax}\,dx$, $\int \sin^n x\,dx$, $\int \cos^n x\,dx$, etc.

Key reduction formulas (most frequently used):

IntegralReduction formula
$I_n=\int \sin^n x\,dx$$I_n=-\frac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\,I_{n-2}$
$J_n=\int \cos^n x\,dx$$J_n=\frac{\cos^{n-1}x\sin x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\,J_{n-2}$
$K_n=\int x^n e^{ax}\,dx$$K_n=\frac{x^n e^{ax}}{a}-\frac{n}{a}\,K_{n-1}$

Derivation sketch (for $I_n=\int \sin^n x\,dx$):

  • Write $\sin^n x=\sin^{n-1}x\cdot\sin x$.
  • Set $u=\sin^{n-1}x$, $dv=\sin x\,dx$; then $du=(n-1)\sin^{n-2}x\cos x\,dx$, $v=-\cos x$.
  • Apply integration by parts: $I_n=uv-\int v\,du$ leading to the stated recurrence.

SI unit: Since the integrand is dimensionless in trigonometric cases, the result is in radians (unit of angle). For $\int x^n e^{ax}\,dx$, the unit is the same as $x^{n+1}$ (e.g., $\text{m}^{n+1}$ if $x$ is in metres).

Example: Evaluate $\int \sin^5 x\,dx$.

Using the reduction formula for $I_n$:

$I_5=-\frac{\sin^4 x\cos x}{5}+\frac{4}{5}\,I_3$

Now apply the formula again for $I_3$:

$I_3=-\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3}+\frac{2}{3}\,I_1$ and $I_1= -\cos x$.

Substituting back:

$I_3=-\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3}-\frac{2}{3}\cos x$

$I_5=-\frac{\sin^4 x\cos x}{5}+\frac{4}{5}\left(-\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3}-\frac{2}{3}\cos x\right)+C$

Simplify:

$I_5= -\frac{\sin^4 x\cos x}{5}-\frac{4\sin^2 x\cos x}{15}-\frac{8\cos x}{15}+C$

This compact result is obtained without performing the integration from scratch, illustrating the power of reduction formulas for CBSE Class 12 exams.
