Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Mathematics
Definition of a Relation

A relation is a rule that associates each element of a set $A$ (called the first set) with one or more elements of another set $B$ (called the second set). Formally, a relation $R$ from $A$ to $B$ is a subset of the Cartesian product $A\times B$, i.e., $$R\subseteq A\times B=\{(a,b)\mid a\in A,\;b\in B\}.$$ The ordered pair $(a,b)$ belongs to $R$ if and only if $a$ is related to $b$ under the rule $R$, and we write $aRb$ or $(a,b)\in R$. The concept of a relation generalises the idea of “pairing” and is the foundation for many structures in mathematics, including functions, equivalence relations, and orderings.

Key points to remember:

  • The domain of a relation $R$ is the set of all first components: $\{a\in A\mid \exists b\in B,\;(a,b)\in R\}$.
  • The range (or image) of $R$ is the set of all second components: $\{b\in B\mid \exists a\in A,\;(a,b)\in R\}$.
  • A relation may associate a single element of $A$ with several elements of $B$, or none at all.
Understanding relations as subsets of $A\times B$ allows us to apply set‑theoretic operations (union, intersection, complement) directly to relations, which is useful in later topics such as composition of relations and equivalence classes.

Mathematics
Types of Relations

Relations can be classified according to various properties that they may satisfy. The most important classifications for Class‑12 are:

1. Reflexive Relation: A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is reflexive if every element is related to itself, i.e., $\forall a\in A,\;(a,a)\in R$. Example: the “is equal to’’ relation on $\mathbb{R}$.

2. Symmetric Relation: $R$ is symmetric if $aRb\Rightarrow bRa$ for all $a,b\in A$. Example: “has the same birthday as’’ on the set of people.

3. Antisymmetric Relation: $R$ is antisymmetric if $aRb$ and $bRa$ together imply $a=b$. Example: “≤’’ on $\mathbb{R}$.

4. Transitive Relation: $R$ is transitive if $aRb$ and $bRc$ together imply $aRc$. Example: “is an ancestor of’’ on the set of all persons.

When a relation on $A$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive simultaneously, it is called an equivalence relation. Equivalence relations partition the set $A$ into disjoint equivalence classes, a concept that underlies modular arithmetic and many algebraic structures.

A relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive is called a partial order. If, in addition, any two distinct elements are comparable (i.e., for any $a,b\in A$, either $aRb$ or $bRa$), the relation becomes a total order. The familiar “≤’’ on $\mathbb{R}$ is a total order.

Recognising these properties helps in simplifying problems, especially when dealing with matrices of relations, where the presence of $1$’s along the diagonal indicates reflexivity, symmetry appears as a mirror image about the main diagonal, and transitivity can be checked using matrix multiplication.

Mathematics
Functions: A Special Kind of Relation

A function $f$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is a relation with the additional restriction that each element of $A$ is associated with **exactly one** element of $B$. Formally, $$f:A\to B\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall a\in A,\;\exists!\;b\in B\;(a,b)\in f.$$ The set $A$ is called the domain of $f$, $B$ the codomain, and the set of actual outputs $\{f(a)\mid a\in A\}$ is the range (or image) of $f$.

Notation: $f(a)=b$ or $b=f(a)$. When the rule is given explicitly, we write $f(x)=\text{expression in }x$.

Important classifications:

  • One‑one (Injective): $f(a_1)=f(a_2)\Rightarrow a_1=a_2$. No two distinct elements of the domain share the same image.
  • Onto (Surjective): Every element of the codomain is hit by at least one element of the domain, i.e., $\forall b\in B,\;\exists a\in A$ such that $f(a)=b$.
  • Bijective: Both one‑one and onto; such functions have inverses that are also functions.

Domain and Codomain Determination: In many CBSE problems, the domain is inferred from the context (e.g., square‑root requires non‑negative arguments), while the codomain is often taken as the set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ unless otherwise specified.

Composite Functions: If $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to C$, the composition $g\circ f:A\to C$ is defined by $(g\circ f)(a)=g\bigl(f(a)\bigr)$. The composition is associative: $h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f$ whenever the domains match.

Understanding these definitions is crucial for solving problems involving function graphs, inverse functions, and for proving properties such as monotonicity or boundedness.

Mathematics
Inverse Functions and Their Existence

The inverse of a function $f:A\to B$, denoted $f^{-1}$, is a relation that swaps the roles of domain and codomain: $f^{-1}:B\to A$ with $f^{-1}(b)=a$ iff $f(a)=b$. For $f^{-1}$ to be a **function**, $f$ must be bijective.

Procedure to find $f^{-1}$ analytically:

  1. Replace $f(x)$ by $y$ and write $y = \text{expression in }x$.
  2. Interchange $x$ and $y$ to obtain $x = \text{expression in }y$.
  3. Solve for $y$; the resulting expression is $f^{-1}(x)$.

Example: Find the inverse of $f(x)=\dfrac{2x-3}{5}$.

$$\begin{aligned} y &= \frac{2x-3}{5}\\ 5y &= 2x-3\\ 2x &= 5y+3\\ x &= \frac{5y+3}{2} \end{aligned}$$
Interchanging $x$ and $y$ gives $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{5x+3}{2}$. Since the original function is linear with non‑zero slope, it is both one‑one and onto $\mathbb{R}$, confirming that the inverse exists for all real $x$.

Graphical Insight: The graph of $f^{-1}$ is the reflection of the graph of $f$ about the line $y=x$. This visual test quickly tells whether a relation is invertible: if any vertical line cuts the graph more than once, the original relation fails the one‑to‑one test, and thus its inverse cannot be a function.

Common Pitfalls:
  • Assuming every function has an inverse; forgetting the bijectivity requirement.
  • Neglecting domain restrictions after inversion; e.g., $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ has domain $[0,\infty)$, and its inverse $f^{-1}(x)=x^{2}$ must be restricted to $[0,\infty)$ to remain a true inverse.
Mastery of inverse functions is essential for solving equations of the form $f(x)=c$, for transforming coordinates, and for understanding the symmetry of curves.

Problem Statement: Let $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be defined by $f(x)=3x+4$. Define $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ by $g(x)=2x-5$. Find the expression for the composite function $(g\circ f)(x)$ and verify whether $(g\circ f)^{-1}$ equals $f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}$.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

1. Principal Values and Domains of Inverse Trigonometric Functions

The inverse trigonometric functions are defined as the multivalued inverses of the elementary trigonometric ratios. In order to make them single‑valued, a principal branch is chosen. The principal values are listed below together with their domains and ranges. All angles are expressed in radians unless otherwise stated.

  • Inverse Sine – $\displaystyle y=\sin^{-1}x$
    Domain: $-1\le x\le 1$
    Range (Principal Value): $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\dfrac{\pi}{2}$
  • Inverse Cosine – $\displaystyle y=\cos^{-1}x$
    Domain: $-1\le x\le 1$
    Range (Principal Value): $0\le y\le\pi$
  • Inverse Tangent – $\displaystyle y=\tan^{-1}x$
    Domain: $-\infty Range (Principal Value): $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}< y<\dfrac{\pi}{2}$
  • Inverse Cotangent – $\displaystyle y=\cot^{-1}x$
    Domain: $-\infty Range (Principal Value): $0< y<\pi$
  • Inverse Secant – $\displaystyle y=\sec^{-1}x$
    Domain: $|x|\ge 1$
    Range (Principal Value): $0\le y<\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ or $\pi< y\le\dfrac{3\pi}{2}$
  • Inverse Cosecant – $\displaystyle y=\csc^{-1}x$
    Domain: $|x|\ge 1$
    Range (Principal Value): $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\le y<0$ or $0< y\le\dfrac{\pi}{2}$

These restrictions guarantee that each inverse function is one‑to‑one, allowing us to differentiate and integrate them in a consistent manner.

2. Derivation of the Derivative of $\sin^{-1}x$

Let $y=\sin^{-1}x$, which implies $\sin y = x$ with $-\dfrac{\pi}{2}\le y\le\dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ gives

$$\cos y\;\frac{dy}{dx}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{\cos y}.$$

Using the Pythagorean identity $\sin^{2}y+\cos^{2}y=1$ and substituting $\sin y = x$, we obtain

$$\cos y=\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}y}=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.$$

Since $y$ lies in the first or fourth quadrant, $\cos y$ is non‑negative, and therefore

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sin^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}},\qquad -1This result is fundamental for evaluating integrals that involve $\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$ in the denominator.

3. Derivative of $\cos^{-1}x$ and Its Relationship with $\sin^{-1}x$

Starting from $y=\cos^{-1}x$, we have $\cos y = x$ with $0\le y\le\pi$. Differentiating,

$$-\sin y\;\frac{dy}{dx}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{1}{\sin y}.$$

From $\sin^{2}y=1-\cos^{2}y=1-x^{2}$ and noting that $\sin y\ge0$ for $0\le y\le\pi$, we get $\sin y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}$. Hence

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cos^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}},\qquad -1Comparing with the derivative of $\sin^{-1}x$, we observe the simple identity $$\cos^{-1}x=\frac{\pi}{2}-\sin^{-1}x,$$

which differentiates to the same result, confirming the internal consistency of the inverse trigonometric system.

4. Derivative of $\tan^{-1}x$ – A Detailed Derivation

Let $y=\tan^{-1}x$, so $\tan y = x$ with $-\dfrac{\pi}{2} $$\sec^{2}y\;\frac{dy}{dx}=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{\sec^{2}y}.$$

Since $\sec^{2}y=1+\tan^{2}y$, and $\tan y = x$, we have

$$\sec^{2}y=1+x^{2}.$$

Thus the derivative becomes

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\tan^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}},\qquad -\inftyThis expression is especially useful in integration techniques such as partial fractions and in solving differential equations involving rational functions.

5. Derivatives of $\cot^{-1}x$, $\sec^{-1}x$, and $\csc^{-1}x$

Using the same implicit differentiation strategy, we obtain the following results:

  • Cotangent Inverse – $y=\cot^{-1}x$, $\cot y = x$, $0 $$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\cot^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}.$$
  • Secant Inverse – $y=\sec^{-1}x$, $\sec y = x$, $|x|\ge1$
    $$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\sec^{-1}x\bigr]=\frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}.$$
  • Cosecant Inverse – $y=\csc^{-1}x$, $\csc y = x$, $|x|\ge1$
    $$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\csc^{-1}x\bigr]=-\frac{1}{|x|\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}.$$

Note the absolute value $|x|$ in the denominators of the secant and cosecant derivatives; it ensures the expression remains positive for the admissible domain $|x|\ge1$.

6. Integral Formulas Involving Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Integrals that contain rational functions of $x$ often lead to inverse trigonometric results. The following standard forms are derived directly from the differentiation formulas above.

  • $$\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}=\sin^{-1}x+C,\qquad |x|<1.$$
  • $$\int\frac{dx}{1+x^{2}}=\tan^{-1}x+C,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.$$
  • $$\int\frac{dx}{x\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}=\sec^{-1}|x|+C,\qquad |x|>1.$$
  • $$\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}}=\ln\bigl|x+\sqrt{x^{2}+a^{2}}\bigr|+C,\qquad a>0,$$ which can be expressed as $\sinh^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{x}{a}\right)+C$ if hyperbolic functions are introduced.

These integrals are frequently asked in CBSE board examinations and form the backbone of the “Application of Integrals” chapter.
