Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

1. Comprehensive Chapter Overview & Core Concepts

This chapter is a cornerstone of electrostatics, extending the understanding of electric fields and forces to the scalar concepts of potential and energy. It lays the groundwork for understanding how charge is stored and released in devices like capacitors, which are ubiquitous in electronic circuits.

1.1 Electrostatic Potential (V)

  • Definition: The electrostatic potential at any point in an electric field is the work done by an external agency in bringing a unit positive test charge from infinity to that point without acceleration. It is a scalar quantity, measured in Volts (V).
  • Potential Difference: The potential difference between two points A and B ($V_B - V_A$) is the work done per unit positive test charge ($q_0$) in moving it from point A to point B: $V_B - V_A = \frac{W_{AB}}{q_0}$.
  • Relation with Electric Field: Electric potential is intimately linked to the electric field. In one dimension, $E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$. Generally, $\vec{E} = -\nabla V$, meaning the electric field points in the direction of decreasing potential.
  • Potential due to a Point Charge: For a point charge $Q$ at a distance $r$, the potential is $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}$. Note that potential is positive for positive charges and negative for negative charges.
  • Potential due to an Electric Dipole: At a point $(r, \theta)$ from the center of a dipole with dipole moment $\vec{p}$, the potential is $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p \cos\theta}{r^2}$.
    • On the axial line ($\theta=0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$): $V = \pm \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2}$
    • On the equatorial plane ($\theta=90^\circ$): $V=0$
  • Equipotential Surfaces: These are surfaces in an electric field where the electric potential is constant at every point.
    • No work is done in moving a charge from one point to another on an equipotential surface.
    • Electric field lines are always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.
    • They are closer together in regions of strong electric fields and farther apart in regions of weak fields.
    • Two equipotential surfaces can never intersect.

1.2 Electrostatic Potential Energy (U)

  • Definition: The electrostatic potential energy of a system of charges is the work done by an external agency in assembling the charges from infinity to their current configuration.
  • Potential Energy of a System of Two Point Charges: For two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by a distance $r$, $U = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$.
  • Potential Energy of a Dipole in an External Electric Field: When an electric dipole with moment $\vec{p}$ is placed in a uniform external electric field $\vec{E}$, its potential energy is $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos\theta$.
    • Minimum energy (stable equilibrium) when $\theta=0^\circ$ (dipole aligned with E).
    • Maximum energy (unstable equilibrium) when $\theta=180^\circ$ (dipole anti-aligned with E).

1.3 Conductors and Dielectrics in Electric Fields

  • Conductors: Materials with a large number of free charge carriers.
    • Net electrostatic field inside a conductor is zero.
    • Net charge in the interior of a conductor is zero; any excess charge resides on its surface.
    • Electric potential is constant throughout the volume of the conductor and is equal to its value on the surface.
    • Electric field at the surface of a charged conductor is always perpendicular to the surface.
    • Electrostatic shielding: The phenomenon of protecting a region from external electric fields by enclosing it within a conductor (Faraday cage).
  • Dielectrics: Insulating materials that get polarized when placed in an external electric field but do not conduct electricity.
    • Polar Dielectrics: Molecules have a permanent electric dipole moment (e.g., H₂O, HCl).
    • Non-Polar Dielectrics: Molecules do not have a permanent electric dipole moment (e.g., O₂, N₂).
    • Polarization: The alignment or induction of dipole moments in a dielectric due to an external electric field. This creates an internal electric field that opposes the external field, reducing the net electric field inside the dielectric.
    • Dielectric Constant (K): It is the ratio of the external electric field ($E_0$) to the reduced electric field ($E$) inside the dielectric: $K = E_0/E$. For vacuum, $K=1$. For conductors, $K=\infty$.

1.4 Capacitance (C)

  • Definition: The capacitance of a conductor is its ability to store electric charge. It is defined as the ratio of the charge ($Q$) stored on the conductor to the potential difference ($V$) developed across it: $C = \frac{Q}{V}$. The SI unit is Farad (F), where 1 F = 1 C/V.
  • Parallel Plate Capacitor: A common capacitor design consisting of two large parallel conducting plates separated by a small distance.
    • Capacitance in vacuum: $C_0 = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$, where $A$ is the area of each plate and $d$ is the separation between them. $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.
    • Capacitance with Dielectric: If a dielectric medium of dielectric constant $K$ fills the space between the plates, the capacitance increases: $C = \frac{K\epsilon_0 A}{d} = K C_0$.
  • Combination of Capacitors:
    • Series Combination: When capacitors are connected end-to-end. The charge on each capacitor is the same, but the potential difference divides. The reciprocal of the equivalent capacitance is the sum of the reciprocals of individual capacitances: $\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots$
    • Parallel Combination: When capacitors are connected such that the potential difference across each is the same. The total charge divides. The equivalent capacitance is the sum of individual capacitances: $C_{eq} = C_1 + C_2 + \dots$
  • Energy Stored in a Capacitor: The energy stored is the work done in charging the capacitor. This energy is stored in the electric field between the plates. $$U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV$$
  • Energy Density: The energy stored per unit volume in the electric field: $u = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 E^2$.
  • Loss of Energy on Sharing Charges: When two charged capacitors are connected, charge redistributes until a common potential is reached. In this process, there is always a loss of energy, usually dissipated as heat, unless an inductor is used to conserve energy in oscillations.

2. Key Theorems / Core Formulas

Electrostatic Potential:

  • Potential difference between points A and B: $V_B - V_A = \frac{W_{AB}}{q_0}$
  • Potential due to a point charge Q at distance r: $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}$$
  • Potential due to an electric dipole (p) at $(r, \theta)$: $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p \cos\theta}{r^2}$$
  • Relation between Electric Field and Potential (in 1D): $E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$

Electrostatic Potential Energy:

  • Potential energy of two point charges ($q_1, q_2$) separated by r: $$U = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r}$$
  • Potential energy of an electric dipole ($\vec{p}$) in a uniform electric field ($\vec{E}$): $$U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos\theta$$

Capacitance:

  • Definition of Capacitance: $$C = \frac{Q}{V}$$
  • Capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor (vacuum): $$C_0 = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$$
  • Capacitance of a parallel plate capacitor (with dielectric K): $$C = \frac{K\epsilon_0 A}{d} = K C_0$$
  • Capacitors in Series: $$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{C_i}$$
  • Capacitors in Parallel: $$C_{eq} = \sum_{i} C_i$$

Energy Stored in a Capacitor:

  • Energy stored: $$U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV$$
  • Energy density of electric field: $$u = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 E^2$$

3. Solved High-Yield CBSE Board PYQs

Question 1 (CBSE 2018) - 3 Marks

Define an equipotential surface. Draw equipotential surfaces for a uniform electric field in the z-direction. Why are equipotential surfaces perpendicular to the electric field lines?

Solution:

  1. Definition: An equipotential surface is a surface in an electric field over which the electrostatic potential is constant at every point. (1 Mark)
  2. Drawing: For a uniform electric field in the z-direction, the electric field lines are straight, parallel, and equally spaced, pointing along the z-axis. The equipotential surfaces would be planes perpendicular to the z-axis (i.e., xy-planes) and parallel to each other. 
                            
                            ^ E-field (z-direction)
                            |
                            |------------------- Equipotential Surface 1 (V₁)
                            |                         (Plane at z₁)
                            |------------------- Equipotential Surface 2 (V₂)
                            |                         (Plane at z₂)
                            |------------------- Equipotential Surface 3 (V₃)
                            |                         (Plane at z₃)
                            O-----------------------------------------> X-axis
                            |
                            V Y-axis
    (1 Mark for correct drawing/description)
  3. Reason for Perpendicularity: The work done ($W$) in moving a test charge 'q' between any two points on an equipotential surface is zero, because the potential difference ($\Delta V$) between any two points on such a surface is zero ($W = q \Delta V = q \times 0 = 0$). Also, work done is given by $W = \int \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}$. Since $W=0$ for any arbitrary displacement $d\vec{l}$ along the surface, it implies that $\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0$. This means the electric field vector $\vec{E}$ must be perpendicular to the displacement vector $d\vec{l}$. As $d\vec{l}$ can be in any direction on the equipotential surface, the electric field $\vec{E}$ must be perpendicular to the entire equipotential surface at every point. (1 Mark)

Question 2 (CBSE 2020) - 3 Marks

Three capacitors of capacitances $C_1 = 3 \mu F$, $C_2 = 6 \mu F$ and $C_3 = 12 \mu F$ are connected in series. Find the equivalent capacitance of the combination. If the combination is connected to a 120V supply, what is the charge on each capacitor and the total energy stored in the combination?

Solution:

Given: $C_1 = 3 \mu F$, $C_2 = 6 \mu F$, $C_3 = 12 \mu F$. Voltage supply $V = 120 V$.

  1. Equivalent Capacitance ($C_{eq}$): For capacitors connected in series, the reciprocal of the equivalent capacitance is the sum of the reciprocals of individual capacitances: $$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3}$$ $$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{3 \mu F} + \frac{1}{6 \mu F} + \frac{1}{12 \mu F}$$ To sum these fractions, find a common denominator, which is $12 \mu F$: $$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{4}{12 \mu F} + \frac{2}{12 \mu F} + \frac{1}{12 \mu F} = \frac{4 + 2 + 1}{12 \mu F} = \frac{7}{12 \mu F}$$ $$C_{eq} = \frac{12}{7} \mu F \approx 1.71 \mu F$$ (1 Mark for formula and calculation of $C_{eq}$)
  2. Charge on each capacitor (Q): In a series combination, the charge stored on each capacitor is the same as the total charge stored by the equivalent capacitance. $$Q = C_{eq} V$$ $$Q = \left(\frac{12}{7} \times 10^{-6} F\right) \times (120 V)$$ $$Q = \frac{1440}{7} \times 10^{-6} C \approx 205.71 \times 10^{-6} C \approx 205.71 \mu C$$ Therefore, the charge on each capacitor is $Q_1 = Q_2 = Q_3 = \frac{1440}{7} \mu C$. (1 Mark for concept and calculation of Q)
  3. Total Energy Stored (U): The total energy stored in the combination can be calculated using the equivalent capacitance and the total voltage: $$U = \frac{1}{2} C_{eq} V^2$$ $$U = \frac{1}{2} \times \left(\frac{12}{7} \times 10^{-6} F\right) \times (120 V)^2$$ $$U = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{12}{7} \times 10^{-6} \times 14400$$ $$U = \frac{6 \times 14400}{7} \times 10^{-6} J = \frac{86400}{7} \times 10^{-6} J \approx 12342.86 \times 10^{-6} J \approx 12.34 mJ$$ (1 Mark for formula and calculation of U)

Question 3 (CBSE 2019) - 3 Marks

Two point charges $Q_1 = +5 \mu C$ and $Q_2 = -3 \mu C$ are located 16 cm apart. At what point(s) on the line joining the two charges is the electric potential zero? Take the potential at infinity to be zero.

Solution:

Let the two charges be placed along the x-axis. Let $Q_1$ be at the origin (x=0) and $Q_2$ at x = 16 cm = 0.16 m. Let P be a point on the line joining the two charges where the electric potential is zero. The total potential at P is the algebraic sum of potentials due to $Q_1$ and $Q_2$: $V = V_1 + V_2 = 0$. $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q_1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q_2}{r_2} = 0$$ Since $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \ne 0$, we must have $\frac{Q_1}{r_1} + \frac{Q_2}{r_2} = 0$, which implies $\frac{Q_1}{r_1} = -\frac{Q_2}{r_2}$.

  1. Case 1: Point P is between the charges ($0 < x < 0.16$ m). Let P be at a distance x from $Q_1$. Then $r_1 = x$ and $r_2 = (0.16 - x)$. $$\frac{+5 \times 10^{-6} C}{x} = -\frac{(-3 \times 10^{-6} C)}{0.16 - x}$$ $$\frac{5}{x} = \frac{3}{0.16 - x}$$ Cross-multiplying: $$5(0.16 - x) = 3x$$ $$0.8 - 5x = 3x$$ $$0.8 = 8x \implies x = \frac{0.8}{8} = 0.1 \text{ m or } 10 \text{ cm}$$ This point is located 10 cm from $Q_1$ (and 6 cm from $Q_2$) on the line segment joining them. (1.5 Marks for correct setup and solution of Case 1)
  2. Case 2: Point P is outside the charges. Since $Q_1$ is positive and $Q_2$ is negative, for the potential to be zero outside, the point must be closer to the charge with smaller magnitude to effectively cancel the potentials. Here, $|Q_2| < |Q_1|$, so the point P must be to the right of $Q_2$ (at $x > 0.16$ m). Let P be at a distance x from $Q_1$. Then $r_1 = x$ and $r_2 = x - 0.16$. $$\frac{+5 \times 10^{-6} C}{x} = -\frac{(-3 \times 10^{-6} C)}{x - 0.16}$$ $$\frac{5}{x} = \frac{3}{x - 0.16}$$ Cross-multiplying: $$5(x - 0.16) = 3x$$ $$5x - 0.8 = 3x$$ $$2x = 0.8 \implies x = \frac{0.8}{2} = 0.4 \text{ m or } 40 \text{ cm}$$ This point is located 40 cm from $Q_1$ (and 24 cm from $Q_2$) on the line extending beyond $Q_2$. (1.5 Marks for correct setup and solution of Case 2)

Thus, there are two points on the line joining the two charges where the electric potential is zero: one at 10 cm from $Q_1$ between the charges, and another at 40 cm from $Q_1$ on the line extending beyond $Q_2$ (i.e., 24 cm to the right of $Q_2$).

4. Quick Revision Summary Box

  • Electrostatic Potential (V): Scalar quantity. Work per unit charge ($W/q_0$). $V = kQ/r$. Measured in Volts.
  • Potential Difference: Work done per unit charge moving between two points ($V_B - V_A = W_{AB}/q_0$).
  • Equipotential Surfaces: Surfaces of constant potential. Electric field lines are always perpendicular to them. No work done moving charge on these surfaces.
  • Electrostatic Potential Energy (U): Energy of a system of charges due to their configuration. $U = kq_1q_2/r$ for two charges. $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E}$ for a dipole in E-field.
  • Conductors: Electric field inside is zero. Potential is constant throughout. Excess charge resides on surface.
  • Dielectrics: Insulators that polarize in an E-field. Reduces the net E-field. Characterized by dielectric constant $K = E_0/E$.
  • Capacitance (C): Measure of a conductor's ability to store charge. $C = Q/V$. SI unit is Farad (F).
  • Parallel Plate Capacitor: $C = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$ (vacuum). $C = \frac{K\epsilon_0 A}{d}$ (with dielectric).
  • Capacitor Combinations:
    • Series: $\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \sum \frac{1}{C_i}$ (Charge is same across each capacitor).
    • Parallel: $C_{eq} = \sum C_i$ (Potential difference is same across each capacitor).
  • Energy Stored in Capacitor: $U = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV$. Energy density $u = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 E^2$.
  • Energy Loss: Occurs when two charged capacitors are connected due to charge redistribution.
