Concept Core

1. Theoretical Principles & Definitions - Electric Charges & Fields

§
Fundamental Overview of Electrostatics

Electrostatics is the branch of physics that deals with the study of electric charges at rest. It explores the forces, fields, and potentials associated with stationary charges.

Electric Charge (q): An intrinsic property of elementary particles (like electrons and protons) that gives rise to electric forces between them. It is a scalar quantity.

  • Types: Positive (protons) and Negative (electrons).
  • Units: SI unit is Coulomb (C).
  • Quantization of Charge: Electric charge is always an integral multiple of the basic elementary charge ($e$), i.e., $q = \pm ne$, where $n$ is an integer and $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.
  • Conservation of Charge: For an isolated system, the total electric charge remains constant. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed, only transferred.
  • Additivity of Charge: The total charge of a system is the algebraic sum of all individual charges present in the system.

§
Coulomb's Law & Electric Field

Coulomb's Law: States that the force of attraction or repulsion between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force acts along the line joining the two charges.
Mathematically, $F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$, where $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9 \, \text{N m}^2 \text{ C}^{-2}$ is Coulomb's constant, and $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space.

Electric Field ($\vec{E}$): The region around an electric charge or a system of charges in which any other test charge experiences an electrostatic force. It is a vector quantity.
The electric field at a point is defined as the electrostatic force per unit positive test charge ($q_0$) placed at that point: $\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}$.
For a point charge $Q$, the magnitude of the electric field at a distance $r$ is $E = k \frac{|Q|}{r^2}$.

Electric Field Lines: Imaginary lines used to visualize the electric field.

  • Originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges.
  • Never intersect each other.
  • The tangent at any point on an electric field line gives the direction of the electric field at that point.
  • The density of field lines indicates the strength of the electric field.

§
Electric Dipole & Dipole Moment

Electric Dipole: A pair of equal and opposite point charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by a small fixed distance ($2a$).

Electric Dipole Moment ($\vec{p}$): A vector quantity that measures the strength of an electric dipole. Its magnitude is the product of the magnitude of either charge and the distance between them, i.e., $p = q \times 2a$. Its direction is from the negative charge to the positive charge.

Torque on a Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field: When an electric dipole is placed in a uniform electric field ($\vec{E}$), it experiences a torque ($\vec{\tau}$) given by $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$. The torque tends to align the dipole with the electric field.

Potential Energy of a Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field: $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{E}$.

§
Gauss's Law

Electric Flux ($\Phi_E$): The measure of the number of electric field lines passing through a given surface. It is a scalar quantity.
For a uniform electric field, $\Phi_E = E A \cos\theta = \vec{E} \cdot \vec{A}$, where $\vec{A}$ is the area vector. For a non-uniform field, $\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$.

Gauss's Law: States that the total electric flux through any closed surface (called a Gaussian surface) is equal to $\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}$ times the net electric charge ($Q_{enc}$) enclosed within that surface.
Mathematically, $\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$.

Applications of Gauss's Law: Gauss's law is a powerful tool for calculating electric fields for charge distributions with high symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, planar).

  • Electric Field due to an Infinitely Long Straight Uniformly Charged Wire: $E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r}$, where $\lambda$ is the linear charge density.
  • Electric Field due to a Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet: $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$, where $\sigma$ is the surface charge density.
  • Electric Field due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell:
    • Outside the shell ($r > R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}$
    • On the surface ($r = R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{R^2}$
    • Inside the shell ($r < R$): $E = 0$

2. Essential Equations & Derivations - Electric Charges & Fields

Coulomb's Law & Principle of Superposition

Magnitude of force between two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r$:
$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$ Where $\epsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \, \text{C}^2 \text{ N}^{-1} \text{ m}^{-2}$ (permittivity of free space).

Vector form of Coulomb's Law: If $\vec{r}_{12}$ is the position vector from $q_1$ to $q_2$, then force on $q_2$ due to $q_1$ is:
$$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_{21}|^3} \vec{r}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_{21}|^2} \hat{r}_{21}$$ Principle of Superposition: The total force on a given charge due to an array of other charges is the vector sum of all individual forces acting on that charge. $$\vec{F}_{total} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \dots + \vec{F}_n$$

Electric Field & Electric Dipole

Electric Field due to a point charge $Q$ at distance $r$:
$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{|Q|}{r^2}$$ Electric Field in vector form:
$$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^3} \vec{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$ Electric Dipole Moment ($p$):
$$\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$$ Direction is from $-q$ to $+q$.

Electric Field on Axial Line of a dipole (at distance $r$ from center):
$$E_{axial} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}$$ For short dipole ($r \gg a$): $E_{axial} \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3}$

Electric Field on Equatorial Line of a dipole (at distance $r$ from center):
$$E_{equatorial} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}$$ For short dipole ($r \gg a$): $E_{equatorial} \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3}$ (Direction opposite to $\vec{p}$)

Torque on a Dipole in Uniform Electric Field:
$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$$ Magnitude: $\tau = pE \sin\theta$

Potential Energy of a Dipole in Uniform Electric Field:
$$U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos\theta$$

Electric Flux & Gauss's Law Applications

Electric Flux ($\Phi_E$):
For uniform field through a plane area: $\Phi_E = \vec{E} \cdot \vec{A} = EA \cos\theta$
For non-uniform field through a closed surface (Gaussian surface):
$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A}$$ Gauss's Law:
$$\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$$ Electric Field due to Infinitely Long Straight Charged Wire (Linear charge density $\lambda$):
$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 r}$$ Electric Field due to Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet (Surface charge density $\sigma$):
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$$ Electric Field due to Uniformly Charged Spherical Shell (Total charge $Q$, Radius $R$):
Outside ($r > R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}$
On surface ($r = R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{R^2}$
Inside ($r < R$): $E = 0$

Board Examination Questions

3. Solved High-Yield CBSE PYQs - Electric Charges & Fields

Q1

Coulomb's Law and Equilibrium

Problem: Two point charges $q_1 = +5\mu C$ and $q_2 = +10\mu C$ are separated by a distance of $0.5$ m in air. Find the force between them. Where should a third charge $q_3$ be placed on the line joining $q_1$ and $q_2$ such that it experiences no net force?

Step-by-step Solution: Part 1: Force between $q_1$ and $q_2$ 1. Identify given values: $q_1 = 5 \times 10^{-6}$ C, $q_2 = 10 \times 10^{-6}$ C, $r = 0.5$ m, $k = 9 \times 10^9$ N m$^2$ C$^{-2}$. 2. Apply Coulomb's Law: $F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$. 3. Calculate: $F = (9 \times 10^9) \frac{(5 \times 10^{-6})(10 \times 10^{-6})}{(0.5)^2} = (9 \times 10^9) \frac{50 \times 10^{-12}}{0.25} = (9 \times 10^9) (200 \times 10^{-12}) = 1.8$ N. 4. Since both charges are positive, the force is repulsive.

Part 2: Position of $q_3$ for no net force 1. Let $q_3$ be placed at a distance $x$ from $q_1$ towards $q_2$. Then its distance from $q_2$ is $(0.5 - x)$. 2. For $q_3$ to experience no net force, the force due to $q_1$ ($F_{31}$) must be equal and opposite to the force due to $q_2$ ($F_{32}$). Since $q_1$ and $q_2$ are both positive, $q_3$ must be placed between them. 3. Set magnitudes of forces equal: $F_{31} = F_{32}$ 4. $k \frac{|q_1 q_3|}{x^2} = k \frac{|q_2 q_3|}{(0.5 - x)^2}$ 5. $\frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(0.5 - x)^2}$ 6. $\frac{5 \times 10^{-6}}{x^2} = \frac{10 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.5 - x)^2}$ 7. $\frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{2}{(0.5 - x)^2}$ 8. $(0.5 - x)^2 = 2x^2$ 9. Taking square root on both sides: $0.5 - x = \pm \sqrt{2} x$ 10. Case 1: $0.5 - x = \sqrt{2} x \Rightarrow 0.5 = (1 + \sqrt{2})x \Rightarrow x = \frac{0.5}{1 + 1.414} = \frac{0.5}{2.414} \approx 0.207$ m. 11. Case 2: $0.5 - x = -\sqrt{2} x \Rightarrow 0.5 = (1 - \sqrt{2})x = (1 - 1.414)x = -0.414x \Rightarrow x = -\frac{0.5}{0.414} \approx -1.207$ m. This solution is outside the line segment between $q_1$ and $q_2$, meaning $q_3$ would not be in equilibrium if placed there with repulsive forces. Hence, reject this. 12. The third charge $q_3$ should be placed approximately $0.207$ m from $q_1$ (and $0.5 - 0.207 = 0.293$ m from $q_2$) on the line segment joining them.

Q2

Electric Field due to an Electric Dipole

Problem: An electric dipole consists of two point charges $+1\mu C$ and $-1\mu C$ separated by a distance of $2$ cm. Calculate the electric field at a point on the axial line $60$ cm away from the center of the dipole. (CBSE 2018)

Step-by-step Solution: 1. Identify given values: $q = 1 \mu C = 1 \times 10^{-6}$ C $2a = 2$ cm $\Rightarrow a = 1$ cm $= 0.01$ m $r = 60$ cm $= 0.60$ m $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^9$ N m$^2$ C$^{-2}$ 2. Calculate the electric dipole moment $p$: $p = q \times (2a) = (1 \times 10^{-6} \text{ C}) \times (0.02 \text{ m}) = 2 \times 10^{-8}$ C m. 3. Since $r = 0.60$ m and $a = 0.01$ m, we have $r \gg a$. Thus, we can use the approximation for the electric field on the axial line of a short dipole: $E_{axial} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3}$ 4. Substitute the values: $E_{axial} = (9 \times 10^9) \frac{2 \times (2 \times 10^{-8})}{(0.60)^3}$ $E_{axial} = (9 \times 10^9) \frac{4 \times 10^{-8}}{0.216}$ $E_{axial} = \frac{36 \times 10^1}{0.216} = \frac{360}{0.216} \approx 1666.67$ N/C. 5. The direction of the electric field on the axial line is along the direction of the dipole moment (from $-q$ to $+q$).

Fast Revision

4. Quick Mind-Map & Summary - Electric Charges & Fields

Core Rule 1 (Charge Properties)Charge is quantized ($q=ne$), conserved, and additive. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.
Core Rule 2 (Coulomb's Law)$F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$. It's a central force, acts along the line joining charges.
Core Rule 3 (Electric Field)$\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q_0$. Vector quantity, direction same as force on a positive test charge. Field lines originate from +ve, terminate on -ve, never cross.
Core Rule 4 (Electric Dipole)Pair of equal & opposite charges separated by $2a$. Dipole moment $\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$, directed from $-q$ to $+q$. Torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$.
Core Rule 5 (Gauss's Law)$\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\epsilon_0}$. Useful for symmetric charge distributions. Relates flux through a closed surface to enclosed charge.
Board Tip (Vector Nature)Remember that force and electric field are vector quantities. Always use vector addition (or components) when multiple charges/fields are involved. Direction is crucial!
Board Tip (Sign Convention)When using Coulomb's law magnitude, use $|q_1 q_2|$. The sign of charges determines attraction/repulsion. For electric field calculations, consider the direction for a positive test charge.
