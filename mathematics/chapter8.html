Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Definition & Scope
What is “Application of Derivatives”?

The chapter “Application of Derivatives” deals with the utilisation of the differential calculus concepts introduced in the preceding chapters. A derivative, denoted as $f'(x)$ or $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function $y=f(x)$ with respect to the independent variable $x$. In this chapter the focus shifts from merely computing $f'(x)$ to interpreting its geometric and physical significance. The central themes include: (i) monotonicity – determining intervals where a function is increasing or decreasing, (ii) curvature – analysing concavity and points of inflection, (iii) optimisation – locating maxima and minima under given constraints, (iv) related rates – establishing connections between the rates of change of two or more quantities that vary with time, and (v) linear approximation – estimating function values using the tangent line. Each of these themes is supported by rigorous theorems such as the First Derivative Test, the Second Derivative Test, the Mean Value Theorem, and the Lagrange’s Multipliers (in a simplified form suitable for CBSE). The chapter also introduces the concept of “critical points” – points where $f'(x)=0$ or $f'(x)$ does not exist – which serve as candidates for extremum values. Understanding these ideas equips students to model real‑world phenomena ranging from the motion of particles to the design of economical structures.

Fundamental Laws & Theorems
First and Second Derivative Tests

The First Derivative Test provides a systematic way to classify critical points. Let $c$ be a critical point of a differentiable function $f$. Examine the sign of $f'(x)$ on intervals immediately to the left and right of $c$: $$ \begin{cases} f'(x)>0 \text{ for } xc \;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ has a local maximum at } c,\\[4pt] f'(x)<0 \text{ for } x0 \text{ for } x>c \;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ has a local minimum at } c,\\[4pt] \text{sign of }f'(x) \text{ does not change} \;\Rightarrow\; c \text{ is not an extremum.} \end{cases} $$ If the sign analysis is cumbersome, the Second Derivative Test offers a shortcut. Compute $f''(c)$: $$ \begin{cases} f''(c)>0 \;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ is concave upward near } c \text{ and } c \text{ is a local minimum},\\[4pt] f''(c)<0 \;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ is concave downward near } c \text{ and } c \text{ is a local maximum},\\[4pt] f''(c)=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{the test is inconclusive; revert to the First Derivative Test.} \end{cases} $$ Both tests rest on the Mean Value Theorem, which guarantees that for a continuous function on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$ there exists some $\xi\in(a,b)$ such that $f'(\xi)=\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. This theorem underpins the logical transition from average to instantaneous rates, a cornerstone of all applications discussed in the chapter.

Intuition & Geometric Insight
Monotonicity, Concavity and the Tangent Line

Geometrically, $f'(x)$ is the slope of the tangent to the curve $y=f(x)$ at the point $(x,f(x))$. If $f'(x)>0$ on an interval, the tangent slopes are positive, and the curve rises as we move from left to right – the function is increasing. Conversely, $f'(x)<0$ signals a decreasing function. The second derivative $f''(x)$ measures how the slope itself changes. When $f''(x)>0$, the slope is increasing, producing a curve that bends upward – we say the function is concave upward. In this situation, any tangent line lies below the graph, a fact exploited in linear approximation: $$ f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a),\qquad \text{for }x\text{ near }a. $$ If $f''(x)<0$, the curve is concave downward and tangents lie above the graph. Points where $f''(x)=0$ (provided $f'''(x)\neq0$) are potential points of inflection – locations where the curvature changes sign. Visualising these concepts on a graph helps students predict the behaviour of a function without performing exhaustive algebraic calculations.

Exceptions & Special Cases
When Standard Tests Fail

The Second Derivative Test fails when $f''(c)=0$ or $f''(c)$ does not exist. In such cases the nature of $c$ must be examined by higher‑order derivatives or by reverting to the First Derivative Test. For example, consider $f(x)=x^4$. Here $f'(0)=0$ and $f''(0)=0$, yet $f'''(0)=0$ and $f^{(4)}(0)=24>0$. The function is still increasing on both sides of $0$, and $0$ is a point of minimum, not a maximum. Another subtlety arises with cusp points where $f'(c)$ does not exist (e.g., $f(x)=|x|$ at $x=0$). Such points are critical in the sense that the derivative fails to exist, but they are not classified by the usual derivative tests. The chapter therefore stresses checking the domain of definition, continuity, and differentiability before applying any test.

Worked Example
Optimising the Area of a Rectangle Inscribed in a Parabola

**Problem Statement** – A rectangle is to be inscribed in the parabola $y=12-3x^{2}$ with one side on the $x$‑axis. Find the dimensions of the rectangle that give the maximum possible area.

**Solution** – Let the rectangle have vertices at $( -x,0)$, $(x,0)$, $(x, y)$ and $(-x, y)$, where $y$ lies on the parabola. Hence $y=12-3x^{2}$ and the area $A$ as a function of $x$ is $$ A(x)=2x\cdot y=2x\bigl(12-3x^{2}\bigr)=24x-6x^{3}. $$ The domain is $x\ge0$ and $y\ge0$, which gives $12-3x^{2}\ge0\Rightarrow x\le2$. Differentiate: $$ A'(x)=24-18x^{2}. $$ Set $A'(x)=0$ to locate critical points: $$ 24-18x^{2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;x^{2}=\frac{4}{3}\;\Rightarrow\;x=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}. $$ Since $x$ is positive, $x=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\approx1.155$ lies in the admissible interval $[0,2]$. Compute the second derivative: $$ A''(x)=-36x. $$ At $x=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$, $A''\bigl(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\bigr)=-36\cdot\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}<0$, indicating a local maximum. The corresponding $y$‑value is $$ y=12-3\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{2}=12-3\cdot\frac{4}{3}=8. $$ Thus the rectangle of maximum area has width $2x= \dfrac{4}{\sqrt{3}}$ units and height $y=8$ units. The maximal area is $$ A_{\max}=24\cdot\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}-6\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^{3}= \frac{64}{\sqrt{3}}\;\text{square units}. $$ **Key Takeaways** – The example illustrates the standard optimisation workflow: (i) express the quantity to be optimised as a single‑variable function, (ii) determine the feasible domain, (iii) locate critical points via $f'(x)=0$, (iv) use the Second Derivative Test (or sign chart) to confirm the nature of the critical point, and (v) substitute back to obtain the required dimensions. This pattern recurs throughout the chapter for problems involving cost minimisation, profit maximisation, and geometric optimisation.

2. Master Equation Bank

Formulation of a Linear Programming Problem (LPP)

Consider a real‑world situation where a manufacturer produces two products, $X$ and $Y$. Let $x$ and $y$ denote the number of units of $X$ and $Y$ respectively. The objective is to maximise the total profit $Z$, where the profit per unit of $X$ is $p_1$ and that of $Y$ is $p_2$. The profit function is therefore

$$Z = p_1x + p_2y$$

Subject to resource constraints, each expressed as a linear inequality. If the first resource is available in quantity $R_1$ and each unit of $X$ consumes $a_{11}$ units while each unit of $Y$ consumes $a_{12}$ units, the first constraint becomes

$$a_{11}x + a_{12}y \le R_1$$

Similarly, for a second resource with availability $R_2$, consumption coefficients $a_{21}$ and $a_{22}$, we have

$$a_{21}x + a_{22}y \le R_2$$

Non‑negativity conditions are mandatory in CBSE problems:

$$x \ge 0,\qquad y \ge 0$$

Thus the standard form of a two‑variable LPP is

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Maximise } & Z = p_1x + p_2y \\ \text{subject to } & a_{11}x + a_{12}y \le R_1,\\ & a_{21}x + a_{22}y \le R_2,\\ & x,\;y \ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

When the problem requires minimisation (e.g., minimising cost), the objective function is written as $Z = c_1x + c_2y$ and the same constraints apply.

Graphical Solution Method – Step by Step

The graphical method is applicable only when the number of decision variables is two. The steps are as follows:

  1. Convert each inequality into an equality. Replace “$\le$” or “$\ge$” by “$=$” to obtain the boundary lines. For example, $a_{11}x + a_{12}y = R_1$.
  2. Plot the boundary lines on the $xy$‑plane. Choose two convenient points for each line by setting $x=0$ and $y=0$ alternately. For $a_{11}x + a_{12}y = R_1$, the intercepts are $(\frac{R_1}{a_{11}},0)$ and $(0,\frac{R_1}{a_{12}})$.
  3. Identify the feasible region. Test a point (commonly the origin $(0,0)$) in each inequality. If the inequality holds, shade the side containing the origin; otherwise shade the opposite side. The intersection of all shaded regions gives the feasible polygon (or unbounded region).
  4. Locate the corner (extreme) points. These are the vertices of the feasible polygon, obtained either by intersecting two boundary lines or by intersecting a line with an axis. Solve the simultaneous equations of the intersecting lines to obtain the coordinates.
  5. Evaluate the objective function at each corner point. Substitute the coordinates $(x_i,y_i)$ into $Z = p_1x + p_2y$ (or the minimisation counterpart) to compute $Z_i$.
  6. Select the optimal solution. For maximisation, choose the corner point with the largest $Z_i$; for minimisation, choose the smallest.

Mathematically, the optimal value $Z^*$ satisfies

$$Z^* = \max_{(x,y)\in \mathcal{F}} \{p_1x + p_2y\}$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the feasible set.

Corner‑Point (Algebraic) Method – Derivation

When the feasible region is a convex polygon, the fundamental theorem of linear programming states that an optimal solution, if it exists, occurs at a vertex of the polygon. To prove this, consider any feasible point $P$ inside the polygon. Let $V_1$ and $V_2$ be two adjacent vertices such that $P$ lies on the line segment $\overline{V_1V_2}$. Any linear function $L(x,y)=p_1x+p_2y$ satisfies

$$L(P)=\lambda L(V_1)+(1-\lambda)L(V_2)$$

for some $\lambda\in[0,1]$. Since $L(P)$ is a convex combination of $L(V_1)$ and $L(V_2)$, it cannot exceed the larger of the two endpoint values. Hence the maximum (or minimum) is attained at a vertex.

Algorithmically, the corner‑point method proceeds as follows:

  1. Write all constraints in the form $a_{i1}x + a_{i2}y \le R_i$.
  2. Convert each inequality to an equality by introducing slack variables $s_i\ge0$: $a_{i1}x + a_{i2}y + s_i = R_i$.
  3. Form the augmented matrix of the system and apply the simplex tableau (though for two variables a direct algebraic solution is simpler).
  4. Identify all possible basic feasible solutions (BFS) by selecting two equations (including slack equations) at a time and solving for $x$ and $y$.
  5. Discard any BFS that yields negative values for $x$, $y$, or any slack variable.
  6. Compute $Z$ for each admissible BFS and pick the optimal one.

For illustration, consider the system

$$\begin{aligned} 2x + y &\le 100,\\ x + 3y &\le 120,\\ x,\,y &\ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

Introducing slacks $s_1$ and $s_2$:

$$\begin{aligned} 2x + y + s_1 &= 100,\\ x + 3y + s_2 &= 120,\\ x,\,y,\,s_1,\,s_2 &\ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

Choosing the pair $(2x + y + s_1 = 100,\; x + 3y + s_2 = 120)$ and solving for $x$ and $y$ (setting $s_1=s_2=0$) gives the intersection point $(x,y) = (30,40)$. Repeating this for other pairs yields the complete set of corner points: $(0,0)$, $(0,40)$, $(30,40)$, and $(50,0)$. Evaluating $Z$ at each point determines the optimum.

Duality Theorem – Derivation and Interpretation

Every primal maximisation problem has an associated dual minimisation problem. For the primal LPP

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Maximise } & Z = \mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x} \\ \text{subject to } & A\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{b},\\ & \mathbf{x}\ge\mathbf{0}, \end{aligned}$$

the dual is

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Minimise } & W = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{y} \\ \text{subject to } & A^\top\mathbf{y} \ge \mathbf{c},\\ & \mathbf{y}\ge\mathbf{0}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\mathbf{y}$ are the dual variables (often interpreted as shadow prices). The proof of the duality theorem relies on the weak duality inequality:

$$\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{y}$$

for any feasible $\mathbf{x}$ of the primal and feasible $\mathbf{y}$ of the dual. If both problems have feasible solutions, the optimal values satisfy

$$Z^* = W^*.$$

Derivation sketch:

  1. Start from the primal constraints $A\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{b}$ and multiply each by the corresponding dual variable $y_i\ge0$, obtaining $y_i(A\mathbf{x})_i \le y_i b_i$.
  2. Summing over all $i$ yields $\mathbf{y}^\top A\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{b}$.
  3. Since $A^\top\mathbf{y} \ge \mathbf{c}$, we have $\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}^\top A\mathbf{x}$.
  4. Combining the two inequalities gives $\mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{b}$, establishing weak duality.
  5. When both primal and dual attain feasible solutions that satisfy complementary slackness, equality holds, proving strong duality.

Complementary slackness conditions are:

$$y_i\,(b_i - (A\mathbf{x})_i)=0,\qquad x_j\,\big((A^\top\mathbf{y})_j - c_j\big)=0,$$

for all $i$ and $j$. These equations are pivotal in sensitivity analysis.
