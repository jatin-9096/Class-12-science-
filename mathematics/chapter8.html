Area under a curve and between curves

Definition: The area under a curve $y=f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ (where $f(x)\ge0$) is $$\\int_a^b f(x)\\,dx$$ measured \in $\\text{units}^2$ (SI: $\\text{m}^2$).

Area between two curves: If $f(x)$ lies above $g(x)$ on $[a,b]$, the required area is $$\\int_a^b \bigl[f(x)-g(x)\bigr]\\,dx$$.

  • Identify points of intersection by solving $f(x)=g(x)$.
  • Split the interval if the relative position of curves changes.
  • Integrate the positive difference only.

Key formulas

SituationFormula
Single curve$$\\int_a^b f(x)\\,dx$$
Between curves$$\\int_a^b \bigl[f(x)-g(x)\bigr]\\,dx$$

Example: Find the area bounded by $y=x^2$ and $y=4x-x^2$.

Intersection: $x^2=4x-x^2 \Rightarrow 2x^2-4x=0 \Rightarrow x=0,2$.

Since $4x-x^2$ is above $x^2$ on $[0,2]$,

$$\\text{Area}=\\int_0^2 \bigl[(4x-x^2)-x^2\bigr]\\,dx=\\int_0^2 (4x-2x^2)\\,dx$$

$$=\Bigl[2x^2-\\frac{2}{3}x^3\Bigr]_0^2=8-\\frac{16}{3}=\\frac{8}{3}\\,\\text{units}^2$$

Area of a region bounded by a curve and the coordinate axes

Definition: The area of a region bounded by a curve and the coordinate axes is the planar region enclosed by the curve $y=f(x)$ (or $x=g(y)$) together with the relevant portions of the $x$‑axis and $y$‑axis. The area is measured in square units, e.g., $\text{m}^2$.

Key Formulae:

  • If the curve is expressed as $y=f(x)$ and lies above the $x$‑axis on $[a,b]$, then
    $$A=\int_{a}^{b} f(x)\,dx$$
  • If the curve is expressed as $x=g(y)$ and lies \to the \right of the $y$‑axis on $[c,d]$, then
    $$A=\int_{c}^{d} g(y)\,dy$$
  • When the curve crosses an axis, split the interval at the points of intersection and \sum the absolute integrals.

Derivation (for $y=f(x)$): The infinitesimal strip of width $dx$ has height $f(x)$, giving an elemental area $dA=f(x)\,dx$. Summing (integrating) from $a$ \to $b$ yields the total area.

Units: If $x$ and $y$ are measured \in metres, the area is \in $\text{m}^2$ (or generically $\text{unit}^2$).

Example: Find the area bounded by $y=x^{2}$, the $x$‑axis, and the lines $x=0$ and $x=2$.

  1. Identify limits: $a=0$, $b=2$; $y=x^{2}\ge0$ on this interval.
  2. Set up the integral:
    $$A=\int_{0}^{2} x^{2}\,dx$$
  3. Integrate:
    $$\int x^{2}\,dx=\frac{x^{3}}{3}$$
  4. Evaluate at the limits:
    $$A=\left[\frac{x^{3}}{3}\right]_{0}^{2}=\frac{2^{3}}{3}-0=\frac{8}{3}$$
  5. Hence the required area is $\displaystyle \frac{8}{3}\,\text{units}^{2}$.

Takeaway: Always express the region in terms of a single variable, choose correct limits, and apply the definite integral. This method directly follows the CBSE Class 12 syllabus for “Application of Integrals”.

Volume of solids of revolution – disc/washer method

Definition: The volume of a solid obtained by rotating a plane region about an axis is found by integrating the areas of infinitesimal cross‑sections perpendicular to the axis.

Disc/Washer Method

  • If the region is bounded by $y=f(x)$ and the axis of rotation is the $x$‑axis, a typical disc of thickness $dx$ has radius $R=f(x)$. Its volume $dV=\pi R^{2}\,dx$.
  • When a hole of radius $r=g(x)$ is present (washer), $dV=\pi\big(R^{2}-r^{2}\big)\,dx$.
  • For rotation about the $y$‑axis, use $dV=\pi\big(R^{2}-r^{2}\big)\,dy$ with radii expressed as functions of $y$.

General Formula

$$V=\int_{a}^{b}\pi\big[\,R(x)^{2}-r(x)^{2}\,\big]\,dx$$ or $$V=\int_{c}^{d}\pi\big[\,R(y)^{2}-r(y)^{2}\,\big]\,dy$$

Derivation (Sketch)

StepExplanation
1Slice the solid by a plane perpendicular \to the axis → thin disc/washer of thickness $\Delta x$.
2Area of the cross‑section $A=\pi(R^{2}-r^{2})$.
3Volume of slice $\Delta V=A\,\Delta x$.
4Sum all slices and let $\Delta x\to0$ → integral.

SI Unit: Cubic metre ($\text{m}^{3}$) or cubic centimetre ($\text{cm}^{3}$) as per data.

Example

Find the volume generated by rotating the region bounded by $y=\sqrt{x}$, $y=0$, $x=4$ about the $x$‑axis.

Here $R(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $r(x)=0$, limits $0\le x\le4$.

$$V=\int_{0}^{4}\pi\big(\sqrt{x}\big)^{2}\,dx=\pi\int_{0}^{4}x\,dx=\pi\left[\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right]_{0}^{4}=\pi\cdot\frac{16}{2}=8\pi\ \text{units}^{3}$$

Volume of solids of revolution – cylindrical shells method

Definition: The method of cylindrical shells is used to find the volume of a solid obtained by rotating a region about an axis that is parallel to the axis of the region.

When a vertical strip of width $dx$ at a distance $x$ from the axis of rotation is revolved about a vertical axis, it generates a cylindrical shell of radius $r$, height $h$, and thickness $dx$. The volume $dV$ of the shell is

$$dV = 2\pi r h \,dx$$

Hence the total volume is

$$V = 2\pi \int_{a}^{b} r(x)\,h(x)\,dx$$

Similarly, for horizontal strips (integration w.r.t. $dy$) the formula becomes

$$V = 2\pi \int_{c}^{d} r(y)\,h(y)\,dy$$

Key points

  • Radius $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the strip \to the axis of rotation.
  • Height $h$ is the length of the strip measured parallel \to the axis of rotation.
  • SI unit of volume: $m^{3}$.

Derivation Sketch

StepExplanation
1Consider a thin strip of thickness $dx$.
2Revolve \to get a shell of circumference $2\pi r$.
3Shell volume $=$ (circumference)$\times$(height)$\times$(thickness) $=2\pi r h\,dx$.
4Integrate over the interval $[a,b]$.

Example

Find the volume generated by rotating the region bounded by $y = x^{2}$, $y = 4$ about the $y$‑axis.

Solution: Solve $x = \sqrt{y}$, limits $y:0\to4$. Using shells (vertical strips): radius $r = x$, height $h = 4 - x^{2}$, $dx$.

$$V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{2} x\,(4 - x^{2})\,dx = 2\pi\left[2x^{2} - \frac{x^{4}}{4}\right]_{0}^{2}=2\pi\left(8 - 4\right)=8\pi \ \text{m}^{3}$$

Surface area of solids of revolution

Definition: Surface area of a solid obtained by rotating a smooth curve about an axis.

General formulas

AxisFormula
About $x$‑axis$S = 2\pi\int_{a}^{b} y\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^{2}}\,dx$
About $y$‑axis$S = 2\pi\int_{c}^{d} x\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dx}{dy}\right)^{2}}\,dy$

Derivation sketch: For a small element $ds=\sqrt{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}$, its revolution about the $x$‑axis sweeps a frustum of radius $y$ and slant $ds$, giving $dS=2\pi y\,ds$. Substituting $ds=\sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^{2}}dx$ yields the formula.

SI unit: square metre ($\text{m}^{2}$).

Example

Find the surface area generated by rotating $y=\sqrt{x}$, $0\le x\le4$, about the $x$‑axis.

  • $\displaystyle \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$
  • Integrand $=2\pi y\sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^{2}}=2\pi\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}}=\pi\sqrt{4x+1}$
  • $S=\pi\int_{0}^{4}\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx$; let $u=4x+1$, $dx=\frac{du}{4}$, limits $1\to17$
  • $S=\frac{\pi}{4}\int_{1}^{17}u^{1/2}du=\frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\bigl[u^{3/2}\bigr]_{1}^{17}=\frac{\pi}{6}\bigl(17^{3/2}-1\bigr)$
  • Numerically $S\approx\frac{\pi}{6}(70.09-1)=\frac{\pi}{6}\times69.09\approx36.2\ \text{m}^{2}$

Length of a curve

Definition: The length of a smooth curve $y=f(x)$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ is the limit of the sum of straight‑line segments joining successive points on the curve as the number of points tends to infinity.

Derivation (Cartesian form): For an infinitesimal segment $ds$, the Pythagorean theorem gives $ds=\sqrt{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}=\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^{2}}\,dx$. Summing and taking the limit leads to the integral

$$L=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^{2}}\,dx$$

Other forms:

FormLength Formula
Cartesian$L=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^{2}}\,dx$
Parametric ($x=\phi(t),\;y=\psi(t)$)$L=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2}}\,dt$
Polar ($r=r(\theta)$)$L=\int_{\theta_{1}}^{\theta_{2}}\sqrt{r^{2}+\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^{2}}\,d\theta$

SI Unit: metre (m).

Example: Find the length of the curve $y=\frac{x^{3}}{3}$ from $x=0$ \to $x=2$.

  • Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx}=x^{2}$.
  • Substitute \in the Cartesian formula:

$$L=\int_{0}^{2}\sqrt{1+(x^{2})^{2}}\,dx=\int_{0}^{2}\sqrt{1+x^{4}}\,dx$$

Using a standard integral or numerical evaluation, $\int_{0}^{2}\sqrt{1+x^{4}}\,dx\approx4.844$. Hence the curve length is $\boxed{4.844\ \text{m}}$.

Centroid and centre of mass of a plane lamina

Definition: The centroid (geometric centre) of a plane lamina is the point $\\bar{P}(\\bar{x},\\bar{y})$ at which the first moments of area about the $x$‑ and $y$‑axes vanish.

Centroid formulas (uniform density):

CoordinateFormula
$\\bar{x}$$\\displaystyle \\bar{x}=\\frac{1}{A}\\int\\! x\\,dA$
$\\bar{y}$$\\displaystyle \\bar{y}=\\frac{1}{A}\\int\\! y\\,dA$

Centre of mass (variable density $\\rho(x,y)$):

  • Total mass $M=\\int\\! \\rho\\,dA$ (SI unit: kg).
  • $\\displaystyle \\bar{x}=\\frac{1}{M}\\int\\! x\\,\\rho\\,dA$, $\\displaystyle \\bar{y}=\\frac{1}{M}\\int\\! y\\,\\rho\\,dA$ (coordinates in metres).

Derivation sketch: The moment about the $y$‑axis is $M_y=\\int x\\,dA$. Dividing by total area $A$ gives the average $x$‑coordinate, i.e. $\\bar{x}=M_y/A$. Similar reasoning yields $\\bar{y}$.

Example (Uniform triangular lamina):

Vertices: $(0,0),\\;(4,0),\\;(0,3)$. Area $A=\\frac{1}{2}\\times4\\times3=6\\,\\text{m}^2$.

Equation of the hypotenuse: $y=-\\frac{3}{4}x+3$. Then

$$\\bar{x}=\\frac{1}{A}\\int_{0}^{4}\\int_{0}^{-\\frac{3}{4}x+3} x\\,dy\\,dx=\\frac{1}{6}\\int_{0}^{4} x\\left(-\\frac{3}{4}x+3\\right)dx=\\frac{4}{3}\\,\\text{m}$$

$$\\bar{y}=\\frac{1}{A}\\int_{0}^{4}\\int_{0}^{-\\frac{3}{4}x+3} y\\,dy\\,dx=\\frac{1}{6}\\int_{0}^{4}\\frac{\\left(-\\frac{3}{4}x+3\\right)^2}{2}dx=1\\,\\text{m}$$

Thus the centroid (and centre of mass) is at $(\\frac{4}{3},1)$ metres.

Applications in probability and physics (probability density, expectation, work done)

Probability density function (pdf): A non‑negative function $f(x)$ such that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)dx = 1$. $f(x)\ge0$ for all $x$.

Expectation of a continuous r.v. $X$: $E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)dx$. For any function $g$, $E[g(X)]=\int g(x) f(x)dx$.

Work done by a variable force: If $F(x)$ (in newton, N) acts along $x$, the work from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is $W=\int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)dx$ (SI unit joule, J = N·m).

  • Key formulas
SymbolMeaningUnit
$f(x)$pdf1/m
$E[X]$meanm
$W$workJ

Example: Let $f(x)=\frac{2}{a^2}x$, $0\le x\le a$, and $F(x)=kx$.

1. Verify pdf: $\int_0^a \frac{2}{a^2}x dx = \frac{2}{a^2}\cdot \frac{a^2}{2}=1$.

2. Expectation: $E[X]=\int_0^a x\cdot \frac{2}{a^2}x dx = \frac{2}{a^2}\int_0^a x^2 dx = \frac{2}{a^2}\cdot \frac{a^3}{3}=\frac{2a}{3}$.

3. Work done moving from $0$ to $a$: $W=\int_0^a kx dx = \frac{k a^2}{2}$ J.

Result: $E[X]=\frac{2a}{3}$ and $W=\frac{k a^2}{2}$ J.
