1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Set-Theoretic Foundations & The Cartesian Architecture

The architecture of modern mathematics, as codified in the Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice (ZFC), constructs the entirety of relation and function theory upon the foundational concept of the set. In the pedagogical framework of Senior Secondary Mathematics (CBSE Class 12), we establish relations and functions not as isolated operational recipes, but as structured, subset-based associations between well-defined mathematical universes.

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets. The Cartesian Product of $A$ and $B$, denoted by $A \times B$, is formally defined as the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate $a$ belongs to $A$ and the second coordinate $b$ belongs to $B$: $$A \times B = \{ (a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B \}$$ The ordered pair $(a, b)$ is rigorously distinguished from the unordered set $\{a, b\}$ via the Kuratowski definition: $$(a, b) \equiv \{ \{a\}, \{a, b\} \}$$ From this set-theoretic definition, the fundamental criterion of equality for ordered pairs naturally emerges: $$(a_1, b_1) = (a_2, b_2) \iff (a_1 = a_2 \land b_1 = b_2)$$ If set $A$ contains $m$ distinct elements ($|A| = m$) and set $B$ contains $n$ distinct elements ($|B| = n$), the cardinality of their Cartesian product is given by the multiplicative rule: $$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n$$ The Cartesian product is strictly non-commutative; that is, $A \times B \neq B \times A$ unless $A = B$, or at least one of the sets is empty ($\emptyset$). The Cartesian product $A \times A$, representing the self-product of set $A$, is denoted by $A^2$. Any mathematical relation formulated "on a set $A$" is structurally an internal binary relation inhabiting the power set of $A^2$, denoted $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$.

2. Mathematical Theory of Binary Relations

A binary relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$: $$R \subseteq A \times B$$ If $(a, b) \in R$, we denote this association by the infix notation $a \, R \, b$, which reads "$a$ is related to $b$ under relation $R$." Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, we write $a \!\not R\, b$. When $B = A$, $R \subseteq A \times A$ is called a binary relation on $A$.

Because $R$ is defined as an element of the power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$, and the cardinality of the power set of a finite set with $k$ elements is $2^k$, the total number of distinct relations that can be defined from set $A$ (with $|A| = m$) to set $B$ (with $|B| = n$) is precisely: $$N_{\text{relations}} = |\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{|A \times B|} = 2^{mn}$$

2.1 Trivial and Universal Relations

Within the Boolean lattice of subsets of $A \times A$, two extreme boundary relations exist:

  • The Empty (Void or Null) Relation ($\emptyset$): Since the empty set is a subset of every set, $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $\emptyset$ contains no ordered pairs. Formally, $\forall a, b \in A$, $(a, b) \notin \emptyset$.
  • The Universal Relation ($U = A \times A$): The maximal subset of $A \times A$ is $A \times A$ itself. Here, every element of $A$ is related to every other element of $A$. Formally, $\forall a, b \in A$, $(a, b) \in U$.

Both the empty relation and the universal relation on a set $A$ are categorized as trivial relations.

2.2 The Identity Relation vs. The Reflexive Relation

A common point of confusion in senior-level examinations lies in the distinction between the identity relation and a reflexive relation.

The Identity Relation on a set $A$, denoted by $I_A$, is defined strictly as: $$I_A = \{ (a, a) : a \in A \}$$ In $I_A$, every element of $A$ must be related to itself, and only to itself. If any element is paired with an element distinct from itself, the relation ceases to be an identity relation.

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is defined to be Reflexive if and only if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$ In terms of set inclusions, $R$ is reflexive if and only if $I_A \subseteq R$. Hence, while $I_A$ allows no extraneous pairs of the form $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$, a reflexive relation $R$ requires all diagonal pairs $(a, a)$ to be present, but imposes no restriction on the presence of non-diagonal pairs. Consequently, every identity relation is reflexive, but not every reflexive relation is an identity relation: $$I_A \text{ is reflexive} \quad \text{and} \quad I_A \subseteq R_{\text{reflexive}}$$

3. Complete Axiomatic Taxonomy: Reflexivity, Symmetry, and Transitivity

To evaluate binary relations on a set $A$, we apply three fundamental structural properties. A relation satisfying all three properties constitutes an Equivalence Relation.

3.1 Reflexivity

Formal Definition: A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. $$\forall x \in A \implies (x, x) \in R$$ Negation Criterion: To prove that a relation $R$ is not reflexive, it is sufficient to demonstrate the existence of a single counterexample element $x_0 \in A$ such that $(x_0, x_0) \notin R$: $$\exists x_0 \in A \text{ such that } (x_0, x_0) \notin R \implies R \text{ is not reflexive.}$$

3.2 Symmetry

Formal Definition: A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is symmetric if, whenever an ordered pair belongs to $R$, its inverted coordinate pair also belongs to $R$. $$\forall x, y \in A, \quad (x, y) \in R \implies (y, x) \in R$$ Set-Theoretic Inversion: Defining the inverse relation $R^{-1} = \{(y, x) : (x, y) \in R\}$, symmetry can be compactly stated as: $$R = R^{-1}$$ Logical Implication Note: Symmetry is conditioned on the premise $(x, y) \in R$. If no such pair exists, the implication $(x, y) \in R \implies (y, x) \in R$ is vacuously true. Thus, the empty relation $\emptyset$ on any set $A$ is vacuously symmetric.
Negation Criterion: A relation is non-symmetric if there exist elements $x_0, y_0 \in A$ such that: $$(x_0, y_0) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (y_0, x_0) \notin R$$

3.3 Transitivity

Formal Definition: A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is transitive if, whenever an element $x$ is related to $y$, and $y$ is related to $z$, it must follow that $x$ is related to $z$. $$\forall x, y, z \in A, \quad ((x, y) \in R \land (y, z) \in R) \implies (x, z) \in R$$ Critical Analytical Trap (Vacuous Transitivity): Transitivity is formulated as an implication where the antecedent is the conjunction of two conditions: $[(x, y) \in R \text{ and } (y, z) \in R]$. If there are no pairs in $R$ satisfying this antecedent chain, the implication is vacuously true.

Examiner Counterexample: Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $R = \{(1, 2)\}$. Here, $(1, 2) \in R$, but there exists no pair of the form $(2, z)$ in $R$. The antecedent condition never materializes. Therefore, the implication is never violated. Hence, $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ is transitive.
Negation Criterion: To prove non-transitivity, one must locate an explicit triplet $x_0, y_0, z_0 \in A$ such that: $$(x_0, y_0) \in R \quad \land \quad (y_0, z_0) \in R \quad \land \quad (x_0, z_0) \notin R$$

3.4 Antisymmetry (The Order Axiom)

While not required for an equivalence relation, antisymmetry is essential for partial orders. A relation $R$ on $A$ is antisymmetric if: $$\forall x, y \in A, \quad ((x, y) \in R \land (y, x) \in R) \implies x = y$$ Equivalently, if $x \neq y$, then $(x, y)$ and $(y, x)$ cannot simultaneously belong to $R$. A relation satisfying reflexivity, antisymmetry, and transitivity is defined as a Partial Order Relation, and the pair $(A, R)$ is called a partially ordered set or poset.

4. Equivalence Relations, Partitions, and Fundamental Decomposition Theorems

A relation $R$ defined on a set $A$ is called an Equivalence Relation if and only if it satisfies three core conditions:

  1. $R$ is reflexive: $\forall a \in A, \, (a, a) \in R$.
  2. $R$ is symmetric: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$.
  3. $R$ is transitive: $\forall a, b, c \in A, \, ((a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R) \implies (a, c) \in R$.

4.1 Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any element $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$, or $cl(a)$, or $\bar{a}$, is the subset of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$: $$[a] = \{ x \in A : (x, a) \in R \}$$ Due to the symmetry of $R$, this is identically equal to $\{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$. The element $a$ is referred to as a representative of the equivalence class $[a]$.

4.2 Fundamental Properties and the Partition Theorem

The profound significance of equivalence relations in algebra and analysis lies in their ability to slice an arbitrary set into mutually disjoint subsets.

Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations): Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Then:

  1. Non-emptiness: Every equivalence class is non-empty. For every $a \in A$, since $(a, a) \in R$ by reflexivity, we have $a \in [a]$.
  2. Equality of Classes: For any $a, b \in A$, $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
    Proof ($\implies$): Assume $[a] = [b]$. Since $a \in [a]$, we have $a \in [b]$. By definition of $[b]$, $(a, b) \in R$.
    Proof ($\impliedby$): Assume $(a, b) \in R$. Let $x \in [a]$. Then $(x, a) \in R$. Since $(x, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$, by transitivity, $(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$. Thus, $[a] \subseteq [b]$. By symmetry, $(b, a) \in R$, and repeating the argument yields $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Therefore, $[a] = [b]$.
  3. Disjointness or Identity: Any two equivalence classes are either strictly disjoint or completely identical: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad [a] \cap [b] = \emptyset \quad \lor \quad [a] = [b]$$ Proof: Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists an element $c \in [a] \cap [b]$. Thus, $c \in [a] \implies (c, a) \in R$, which by symmetry gives $(a, c) \in R$. Also, $c \in [b] \implies (c, b) \in R$. By transitivity, $(a, c) \in R$ and $(c, b) \in R$ imply $(a, b) \in R$. By property 2 above, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$. Hence, if the intersection is non-empty, the classes are identical.
  4. Exhaustive Union: The union of all equivalence classes reconstructs the original set $A$: $$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$ Proof: Since each $[a] \subseteq A$, it follows that $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A$. Conversely, let $x \in A$. Since $x \in [x]$, we have $x \in \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$. Thus, $A \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$, establishing equality.

A Partition $\mathcal{P}$ of a set $A$ is defined as a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ whose union is $A$: $$A_i \neq \emptyset, \quad A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \text{ for } i \neq j, \quad \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = A$$ Thus, every equivalence relation on $A$ induces a unique partition of $A$ into equivalence classes. Conversely, any partition of $A$ induces an equivalence relation $R$ defined by $(x, y) \in R \iff x \text{ and } y \text{ belong to the same cell of the partition}$.

The set of all equivalence classes is called the Quotient Set of $A$ by $R$, denoted by: $$A / R = \{ [a] : a \in A \}$$

5. Combinatorics of Relations on Finite Sets

Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $n^2$ elements. A relation $R \subseteq A \times A$ can be represented by an $n \times n$ adjacency matrix $M_R = [m_{ij}]$, where $m_{ij} = 1$ if $(a_i, a_j) \in R$ and $0$ otherwise.

5.1 Total Number of Binary Relations

Since each of the $n^2$ entries in the matrix can be chosen independently as either $0$ or $1$: $$N_{\text{total}} = 2^{n^2}$$

5.2 Number of Reflexive Relations

For a relation to be reflexive, all $n$ diagonal elements $(a_i, a_i)$ must be present ($m_{ii} = 1$). The remaining $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal elements can be either present or absent. Thus: $$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

5.3 Number of Symmetric Relations

In a symmetric relation, the diagonal elements $m_{ii}$ can be chosen freely in $2^n$ ways. For the off-diagonal elements, symmetry requires that $m_{ji} = m_{ij}$. The number of pairs of off-diagonal entries is: $$\frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$$ Each pair can be assigned values $(0, 0)$ or $(1, 1)$, offering $2$ choices per pair. By the fundamental counting principle: $$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

5.4 Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

For a relation to be simultaneously reflexive and symmetric, all $n$ diagonal elements are fixed to $1$ ($2^0 = 1$ choice). The remaining $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ off-diagonal symmetric pairs can be chosen freely: $$N_{\text{ref} \land \text{sym}} = 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$$

6. The Theoretical Architecture of Functions (Mappings)

A Function (or mapping) $f$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, is a special type of relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ satisfying two foundational criteria:

  1. Totality (Universal Domain Coverage): Every element of the domain must be associated with an element in the codomain: $$\forall x \in A, \quad \exists y \in B \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Single-Valuedness): No element of the domain can be mapped to more than one distinct element in the codomain: $$\forall x \in A, \quad ((x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f) \implies y_1 = y_2$$

When $(x, y) \in f$, we write $y = f(x)$, where $y$ is the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is a pre-image of $y$.

Domain, Codomain, and Range:
- Domain: $\text{dom}(f) = A$.
- Codomain: $\text{codom}(f) = B$.
- Range (or Image Set): The set of all actual outputs produced by $f$: $$\text{Range}(f) = f(A) = \{ f(x) : x \in A \} = \{ y \in B : \exists x \in A, f(x) = y \}$$ By definition, $\text{Range}(f) \subseteq \text{Codomain}(B)$. Equality between range and codomain is not required in general; when equality does occur, it defines surjectivity.

7. Comprehensive Classification: Injective, Surjective, and Bijective Mappings

7.1 Injective Functions (One-to-One Mappings)

Formal Definition: A function $f: A \to B$ is called Injective (or one-to-one) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct images in the codomain. $$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$ Standard Algebraic Working Method (Contrapositive Statement): In board examination proofs, we almost universally employ the logically equivalent contrapositive statement: $$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Analytical & Calculus-Based Criteria for Real Functions:
For functions $f: I \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on an interval $I$:

  • Monotonicity Criterion: If $f$ is differentiable on an open interval $(a, b)$, and either $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$ (strictly increasing) or $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$ (strictly decreasing), then $f$ is strictly monotonic and therefore strictly injective on $(a, b)$.
  • Horizontal Line Test: A continuous real function $y = f(x)$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = k$ (where $k \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the graph of $f$ at at most one point. If any horizontal line intersects the curve at two or more points, the function is Many-One.

7.2 Surjective Functions (Onto Mappings)

Formal Definition: A function $f: A \to B$ is called Surjective (or onto) if every element of the codomain possesses at least one pre-image in the domain under $f$. $$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$ Set-Theoretic Criterion: $$f: A \to B \text{ is surjective} \iff \text{Range}(f) = \text{Codomain}(B)$$ If $\text{Range}(f) \subsetneq B$, the function is termed Into.

Rigorous Proof Strategy for Surjectivity: To demonstrate that an analytically defined function $f: A \to B$ is surjective:

  1. Let $y$ be an arbitrary element selected from the codomain $B$.
  2. Set up the equation $y = f(x)$ and solve algebraically for the independent variable $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
  3. Prove rigorously that for every $y \in B$, the computed value $x = g(y)$ is guaranteed to lie within the stated domain $A$.
  4. Substitute $g(y)$ back into the original function to verify that $f(g(y)) = y$.

7.3 Bijective Functions (One-to-One and Onto Mappings)

Formal Definition: A function $f: A \to B$ is called Bijective (or an invertible map) if and only if it is simultaneously injective and surjective: $$f \text{ is bijective} \iff (f \text{ is one-to-one}) \land (f \text{ is onto})$$ Cardinality Implications: For finite sets $A$ and $B$:

  • If an injection $f: A \to B$ exists, then $|A| \le |B|$.
  • If a surjection $f: A \to B$ exists, then $|A| \ge |B|$.
  • If a bijection $f: A \to B$ exists, then $|A| = |B|$.

8. Combinatorial Formulas for Functions on Finite Sets

Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

8.1 Total Number of Functions

Each of the $m$ distinct elements in $A$ can be mapped independently to any of the $n$ elements in $B$. By the fundamental multiplication principle of counting: $$N_{\text{total functions}} = n^m = |B|^{|A|}$$

8.2 Total Number of Injective Functions

To construct an injective function: - The first element of $A$ can choose any of $n$ available elements in $B$. - The second element of $A$ can choose any of the remaining $(n - 1)$ elements in $B$. - The $m$-th element of $A$ can choose from $[n - (m - 1)]$ elements.

Therefore, if $m \le n$: $$N_{\text{injections}} = ^n P_m = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!} = n(n - 1)(n - 2)\dots(n - m + 1)$$ If $m > n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two distinct domain elements must share an image. Thus: $$N_{\text{injections}} = 0 \quad (\text{for } m > n)$$

8.3 Total Number of Surjective Functions

When $m < n$, no function can cover all elements of the codomain, so $N_{\text{surjections}} = 0$.
When $m \ge n$, the number of surjective functions can be derived via the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE). Let $U$ be the set of all $n^m$ functions from $A$ to $B$. For each element $b_i \in B$, let $P_i$ denote the property that $b_i$ is excluded from the range of the function. Then: $$N_{\text{surjections}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$ Expanding this summation yields: $$N_{\text{surjections}} = n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n - 1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n - 2)^m - \binom{n}{3}(n - 3)^m + \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}(1)^m$$ Equivalently, using Stirling numbers of the second kind $S(m, n)$, which count the partitions of an $m$-element set into $n$ non-empty subsets: $$N_{\text{surjections}} = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$

8.4 Total Number of Bijective Functions

A bijection requires that $m = n$. Setting $m = n$ in the permutation formula: $$N_{\text{bijections}} = n!$$ If $m \neq n$, then $N_{\text{bijections}} = 0$.

9. Composition of Functions

Formal Definition: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions. The composition of $g$ and $f$, denoted by $g \circ f$ (read as "$g$ circle $f$" or "$g$ composed with $f$"), is a function mapping $A$ directly into $C$: $$g \circ f: A \to C$$ defined pointwise by: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$

9.1 Precise Domain Compatibility Condition

For the composition $g \circ f$ to exist, the output of $f$ must be an acceptable input for $g$. Thus, the Range of $f$ must be a subset of the Domain of $g$: $$\text{Range}(f) \subseteq \text{Domain}(g)$$ If this condition is violated, the expression $g(f(x))$ is undefined for those $x \in A$ whose images fall outside the domain of $g$. When this condition fails, the natural domain of the composition must be restricted to: $$\text{Domain}(g \circ f) = \{ x \in \text{Domain}(f) : f(x) \in \text{Domain}(g) \}$$

9.2 Fundamental Theorems on Composition

Theorem 1 (Non-Commutativity): In general, function composition is non-commutative: $$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$ Indeed, $f \circ g$ may not even be defined when $g \circ f$ is defined, unless $A = C$. Even when $f, g: A \to A$, composition is typically non-commutative.

Concrete Algebraic Demonstration: Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be $f(x) = 2x + 1$, and $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be $g(x) = x^2$. Then: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$$ $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^2) = 2x^2 + 1$$ Clearly, $4x^2 + 4x + 1 \neq 2x^2 + 1$ for almost all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Thus, $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$.

Theorem 2 (Associativity): Function composition is strictly associative. Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$. Then: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$ Proof: Let $x \in A$. Applying the definition pointwise: $$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ $$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Since $(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = ((h \circ g) \circ f)(x)$ for every $x \in A$, and both functions share the same domain $A$ and codomain $D$, the mappings are identical.

Theorem 3 (Preservation of Injectivity and Surjectivity):

  • If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
    Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$. Assume $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. This implies $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$. Since $g$ is injective, $g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$; setting $u = f(x_1)$ and $v = f(x_2)$, we get $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Since $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is injective.
  • If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
    Proof: Let $z \in C$. Since $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists a $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Since $f: A \to B$ is surjective, for this $y \in B$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Substituting $y = f(x)$ gives $g(f(x)) = z \implies (g \circ f)(x) = z$. Hence, every $z \in C$ has a pre-image $x \in A$, so $g \circ f$ is surjective.

Theorem 4 (Deductions from the Composite Function):

  • If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ must be injective. (Note: $g$ need not be injective).
    Proof: Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$ to both sides: $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f$ is injective, this implies $x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ is injective.
  • If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ must be surjective. (Note: $f$ need not be surjective).
    Proof: Let $z \in C$. Since $g \circ f$ is surjective, there exists $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z$. Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have found a $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Thus, $g$ is surjective.

10. Invertible Functions and Inverse Mappings

Formal Definition: A function $f: A \to B$ is defined to be Invertible if there exists another function $g: B \to A$ such that: $$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$ where $I_A: A \to A$ ($I_A(x) = x, \forall x \in A$) and $I_B: B \to B$ ($I_B(y) = y, \forall y \in B$) are the identity functions on sets $A$ and $B$, respectively.
If such a function $g$ exists, it is called the Inverse of $f$ and is uniquely denoted by $f^{-1}$.

10.1 The Fundamental Equivalence Theorem of Invertibility

Theorem: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection (both one-to-one and onto).

Proof Part 1 ($\implies$): Invertibility implies Bijectivity.
Suppose $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.
- Since $I_A$ is an identity map, it is injective. By Theorem 4, since $g \circ f = I_A$ is injective, the inner function $f$ must be injective.
- Since $I_B$ is an identity map, it is surjective. By Theorem 4, since $f \circ g = I_B$ is surjective, the outer function $f$ must be surjective.
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Proof Part 2 ($\impliedby$): Bijectivity implies Invertibility.
Suppose $f: A \to B$ is bijective.
- Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
- Since $f$ is injective, this element $x \in A$ is strictly unique.
We can therefore define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element such that $f(x) = y$.
Now check the compositions: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x), \quad \forall x \in A \implies g \circ f = I_A$$ $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y), \quad \forall y \in B \implies f \circ g = I_B$$ Hence, $g$ satisfies both conditions of an inverse. Therefore, $f$ is invertible, and $g = f^{-1}$.

10.2 Properties of Inverse Functions

  • Uniqueness of Inverse: If a function is invertible, its inverse is unique.
    Proof: Suppose $g_1$ and $g_2$ are both inverses of $f: A \to B$. Then: $$g_1 = g_1 \circ I_B = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_A \circ g_2 = g_2$$
  • Involution Property: If $f: A \to B$ is invertible, then $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is also invertible, and: $$(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$$
  • Reversal Rule for Composition: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are invertible functions, then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and its inverse is given by: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ Proof: We test the composition of $(g \circ f)$ with $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$: $$((g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$ $$((f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ By the uniqueness of the inverse, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

11. Binary Operations: Algebraic Structures

Formal Definition: A Binary Operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function that maps every ordered pair of elements of $A \times A$ to a unique element of $A$: $$*: A \times A \to A$$ We use the infix notation $a * b$ instead of $*(a, b)$. The condition that the output must remain within set $A$ is referred to as the Closure Property.

11.1 Structural Properties of Binary Operations

  • Commutativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is commutative if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$
  • Associativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is associative if: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$
  • Identity Element: An element $e \in A$ is an identity element for $*$ if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a$$ Theorem (Uniqueness of Identity): If an identity element exists for an operation $*$, it is unique.
    Proof: Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identity elements for $*$ on $A$. Since $e_1$ is an identity, $e_2 * e_1 = e_2$. Since $e_2$ is an identity, $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. By commutativity of the evaluation $e_1 * e_2 = e_2 * e_1$, it follows that $e_1 = e_2$.
  • Invertible Element: Given an operation $*$ on $A$ with an identity element $e$, an element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that: $$a * b = b * a = e$$ The element $b$ is called an inverse of $a$ and is typically denoted by $a^{-1}$.
    Theorem (Uniqueness of Inverse): In an associative binary operation with identity $e$, if an element $a \in A$ has an inverse, that inverse is unique.
    Proof: Suppose $b_1$ and $b_2$ are both inverses of $a$. Then: $$b_1 = b_1 * e = b_1 * (a * b_2) = (b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$

12. Senior Examiner Blueprints: Canonical Proof Models & Pitfalls

12.1 The Classic Congruence Modulo Relation

Problem Statement: Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Prove that the relation $R$ on the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ defined by: $$R = \{ (a, b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} : n \text{ divides } (a - b) \}$$ is an equivalence relation, and describe its equivalence classes.

Step 1: Reflexivity.
Let $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot n$. Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n$ divides $(a - a)$. Thus, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Hence, $R$ is reflexive.

Step 2: Symmetry.
Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and assume $(a, b) \in R$. By definition, $n$ divides $(a - b)$, which means there exists an integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that: $$a - b = k \cdot n$$ Multiplying by $-1$ gives: $$b - a = -(a - b) = -k \cdot n = (-k) \cdot n$$ Since $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, $-k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Therefore, $n$ divides $(b - a)$, which means $(b, a) \in R$. Hence, $R$ is symmetric.

Step 3: Transitivity.
Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ and assume $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. Then there exist integers $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that: $$a - b = k_1 n \quad \text{and} \quad b - c = k_2 n$$ Adding the two equations yields: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 n + k_2 n \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)n$$ Since the integers are closed under addition, $(k_1 + k_2) \in \mathbb{Z}$. Thus, $n$ divides $(a - c)$, so $(a, c) \in R$. Hence, $R$ is transitive.

Conclusion: Since $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$.

Equivalence Classes: By the Division Algorithm, for any $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, dividing by $n$ yields a unique remainder $r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n - 1\}$. This produces exactly $n$ distinct equivalence classes: $$[r] = \{ x \in \mathbb{Z} : x \equiv r \pmod n \} = \{ qn + r : q \in \mathbb{Z} \}$$ The quotient set is the ring of integers modulo $n$: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \{ [0], [1], [2], \dots, [n - 1] \}$.

12.2 The Rational Function Bijection Proof

Problem Statement: Let $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. Consider the function $f: A \to B$ defined by: $$f(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$ Prove that $f$ is bijective, and find $f^{-1}$.

Step 1: Proof of Injectivity (One-to-One).
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$. Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$: $$\frac{x_1 - 2}{x_1 - 3} = \frac{x_2 - 2}{x_2 - 3}$$ Cross-multiplying (justified since $x_1, x_2 \neq 3$): $$(x_1 - 2)(x_2 - 3) = (x_2 - 2)(x_1 - 3)$$ $$x_1 x_2 - 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 3x_2 - 2x_1 + 6$$ Subtracting $x_1 x_2 + 6$ from both sides: $$-3x_1 - 2x_2 = -3x_2 - 2x_1$$ Adding $2x_1 + 3x_2$ to both sides: $$-x_1 = -x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Therefore, $f$ is injective.

Step 2: Proof of Surjectivity (Onto).
Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. We test whether there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$: $$y = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3} \implies y(x - 3) = x - 2 \implies yx - 3y = x - 2$$ $$yx - x = 3y - 2 \implies x(y - 1) = 3y - 2$$ Since $y \in B$, $y \neq 1$, so $y - 1 \neq 0$. We can safely divide: $$x = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$ We must verify that this candidate $x$ belongs to the domain $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$. Suppose $x = 3$: $$\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} = 3 \implies 3y - 2 = 3(y - 1) = 3y - 3 \implies -2 = -3$$ This is a contradiction. Hence, $x \neq 3$, meaning $x \in A$ for every $y \in B$.
Finally, verify the image of $x$: $$f(x) = f\left(\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}\right) = \frac{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 2}{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 3} = \frac{\frac{3y - 2 - 2y + 2}{y - 1}}{\frac{3y - 2 - 3y + 3}{y - 1}} = \frac{\frac{y}{y - 1}}{\frac{1}{y - 1}} = y$$ Hence, every $y \in B$ has a pre-image in $A$. Thus, $f$ is surjective.

Conclusion: Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective, and its inverse $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is given by: $$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$

Derivation 1: Combinatorial Formulas for Binary Relations on a Finite Set

Objective: Let $A$ be a set with $|A| = n$. Derive expressions for the total number of (i) relations, (ii) reflexive relations, and (iii) symmetric relations on $A$.

Step 1: Total Binary Relations

A binary relation $R$ on $A$ is defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$:

$$R \subseteq A \times A$$

The cardinality of the Cartesian product is:

$$|A \times A| = |A| \cdot |A| = n \cdot n = n^2$$

Since any subset of $A \times A$ represents a valid relation, the total number of relations corresponds to the cardinality of the power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$:

$$N_R = |\mathcal{P}(A \times A)| = 2^{|A \times A|} = 2^{n^2}$$

Step 2: Number of Reflexive Relations

A relation $R$ on $A$ is reflexive if and only if:

$$\forall a_i \in A, \quad (a_i, a_i) \in R$$

The elements of $A \times A$ can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets:

  • Diagonal elements ($D$): $D = \{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\}$, with $|D| = n$.
  • Non-diagonal elements ($D^c$): $D^c = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j\}$, with $|D^c| = n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$.

For $R$ to be reflexive, every element of $D$ must be included in $R$. Thus, each element of $D$ has exactly $1$ choice (must be present). Each element of $D^c$ may independently be either present or absent (2 choices):

$$N_{\text{ref}} = \left(\prod_{k=1}^{n} 1\right) \times \left(\prod_{k=1}^{n(n-1)} 2\right) = 1^n \cdot 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

Step 3: Number of Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ is symmetric if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$. We consider the matrix representation of $A \times A$:

  • The $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ can independently be included or excluded: $2^n$ choices.
  • The $n^2 - n$ off-diagonal pairs group into symmetric pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ for $i < j$.
  • The number of unordered pairs $\{a_i, a_j\}$ with $i \neq j$ is: $$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$$

For each symmetric pair, there are 2 choices: either both $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ belong to $R$, or neither does. Thus, the total number of symmetric choices is:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Derivation 2: Number of Surjective (Onto) Functions via Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Objective: Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ with $m \ge n \ge 1$. Derive the explicit formula for the total number of surjective mappings $f: A \to B$.

Step 1: Set definition and Universal Set

Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n\}$. Let $\mathcal{U}$ denote the set of all possible functions from $A$ to $B$:

$$|\mathcal{U}| = n^m$$

For each element $b_i \in B$, let $P_i$ denote the property that $b_i$ is not in the range of $f$. Define the subset $A_i \subset \mathcal{U}$ as:

$$A_i = \{f \in \mathcal{U} : b_i \notin f(A)\}$$

A function $f$ is surjective if and only if its range equals $B$, meaning $f$ possesses none of the properties $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \left| \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i^c \right| = |\mathcal{U}| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right|$$

Step 2: Intersection of Deficient Sets

Consider the intersection of any $k$ subsets, $A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}$. A function belonging to this intersection excludes $k$ specific elements of $B$ from its range. Hence, the range is restricted to the remaining $n - k$ elements:

$$|A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = (n - k)^m$$

The number of ways to choose $k$ excluded elements from $n$ elements is $\binom{n}{k}$. Therefore:

$$S_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n} |A_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = \binom{n}{k}(n - k)^m$$

Step 3: Application of PIE

By the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion:

$$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} S_k = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k}(n - k)^m$$

Subtracting this value from $|\mathcal{U}| = n^m = \binom{n}{0}(n - 0)^m$ yields:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = n^m - \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k}(n - k)^m = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k}(n - k)^m$$

Substituting index transformation $r = n - k$ (where $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k} = \binom{n}{r}$):

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n - r} \binom{n}{r} r^m$$

Derivation 3: Associativity of Function Composition

Objective: Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$ be functions. Prove that composition of functions is associative: $h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$.

Step 1: Domain and Codomain Equality

Verify that both combined mappings share identical domains and codomains:

  • $g \circ f: A \to C \implies h \circ (g \circ f): A \to D$
  • $h \circ g: B \to D \implies (h \circ g) \circ f: A \to D$

Both expressions represent valid maps from set $A$ into set $D$.

Step 2: Pointwise Evaluation

Let $x \in A$ be an arbitrary element. By the formal operational definition of composition $(u \circ v)(t) = u(v(t))$:

Evaluate the left-hand side:

$$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$

Evaluate the right-hand side:

$$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$

Step 3: Conclusion

Since $(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = ((h \circ g) \circ f)(x)$ for all $x \in A$, and their domains and codomains are identical:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f \quad \blacksquare$$

Derivation 4: The Reversal Law of Invertible Composition

Objective: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be bijective functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f$ is invertible and its inverse satisfies $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

Step 1: Invertibility Criteria

A function $\phi: X \to Y$ is invertible if and only if there exists a unique function $\psi: Y \to X$ satisfying:

$$\psi \circ \phi = I_X \quad \text{and} \quad \phi \circ \psi = I_Y$$

Given $f$ and $g$ are bijections, their inverses $f^{-1}: B \to A$ and $g^{-1}: C \to B$ exist such that:

$$f^{-1} \circ f = I_A, \quad f \circ f^{-1} = I_B, \quad g^{-1} \circ g = I_B, \quad g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

Step 2: Left-Inverse Verification

Let candidate inverse $\psi = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}: C \to A$. Compose $\psi$ on the left of $(g \circ f): A \to C$:

$$\psi \circ (g \circ f) = (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)$$

Using the associative property of function composition:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)) = f^{-1} \circ ((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f)$$

Substitute $g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$:

$$= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$

Step 3: Right-Inverse Verification

Now compose $\psi$ on the right of $(g \circ f)$:

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})) = g \circ ((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1})$$

Substitute $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$:

$$= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

Step 4: Conclusion

Since $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$ acts as both left- and right-inverse to $(g \circ f)$, by the uniqueness of function inverses:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1} \quad \blacksquare$$

Derivation 5: Fundamental Partition Theorem of Equivalence Classes

Objective: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Prove that for any two elements $a, b \in A$, their equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either identical or disjoint: $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$, and $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset \iff (a, b) \notin R$.

Step 1: Prove $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$

Assume $(a, b) \in R$. By definition, the equivalence class is $[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$.

Let $x \in [a]$. Then:

$$(x, a) \in R$$

Since $(a, b) \in R$ and $R$ is transitive ($(x, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (x, b) \in R$):

$$(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$

Thus, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.

Conversely, because $R$ is symmetric, $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$. Let $y \in [b]$. Then $(y, b) \in R$. By transitivity:

$$(y, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R \implies (y, a) \in R \implies y \in [a]$$

Thus, $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Combining both subset relations:

$$[a] \subseteq [b] \quad \text{and} \quad [b] \subseteq [a] \implies [a] = [b]$$

Step 2: Prove $[a] = [b] \implies (a, b) \in R$

Assume $[a] = [b]$. By the reflexive property of $R$, $(a, a) \in R$, which implies:

$$a \in [a]$$

Since $[a] = [b]$, it follows that $a \in [b]$. By the definition of $[b]$:

$$(a, b) \in R$$

Step 3: Prove Dichotomy ($[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ or $[a] = [b]$)

Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $c \in [a] \cap [b]$.

$$c \in [a] \implies (c, a) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R \quad (\text{by symmetry})$$ $$c \in [b] \implies (c, b) \in R$$

By transitivity:

$$(a, c) \in R \land (c, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$$

From Step 1, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$. Therefore, if the classes share any element, they are identical; otherwise, they are strictly disjoint:

$$[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset \quad \lor \quad [a] = [b] \quad \blacksquare$$
