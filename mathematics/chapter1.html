1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1.1 Axiomatic Foundations of Set Theory and Cartesian Products

The architecture of modern mathematics, as formalized by the Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with the Axiom of Choice (ZFC), positions the concept of an aggregated collection of well-defined, distinct objects—termed a Set—as its foundational bedrock. In the pedagogical continuum of Class 12 Senior Secondary Mathematics governed by the Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE), Chapter 1 establishes the critical transition from intuitive collection theory to deterministic mappings. Every relational calculus and functional analysis begins with the construct of the Cartesian product of two arbitrary non-empty sets.

Let $A$ and $B$ denote two arbitrary non-empty sets defined over a universal domain $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian Product, denoted mathematically by $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate $a$ belongs to the antecedent set $A$, and the second coordinate $b$ belongs to the consequent set $B$. Formally, this is stated in set-builder notation via first-order predicate logic as:

$$A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$

The order inherent to the pair $(a, b)$ is essential: $(a, b) = (c, d) \iff a = c \land b = d$. By Kuratowski's set-theoretic definition of an ordered pair, $(a, b) = \{\{a\}, \{a, b\}\}$, which fundamentally preserves asymmetry whenever $a \neq b$. Consequently, the Cartesian product is strictly non-commutative:

$$A \times B \neq B \times A \quad (\text{provided } A \neq B \text{ and } A, B \neq \emptyset)$$

If the cardinalities of the sets are finite, denoted by $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$, the cardinality of their Cartesian product is governed by the fundamental principle of counting:

$$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n$$

The Cartesian product can be generalized to $n$-tuples over a family of sets $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$:

$$\prod_{i=1}^n A_i = A_1 \times A_2 \times \dots \times A_n = \{(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) : x_i \in A_i, \, \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\}$$

When $A = B = \mathbb{R}$ (the field of real numbers), the product $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the two-dimensional Euclidean plane $\mathbb{E}^2$, providing the coordinate geometry foundation for visualizing relations and mappings through locus tracing and orthogonal projections.

1.2 Formal Definition and Anatomy of a Relation

A binary relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is fundamentally defined as an arbitrary subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. Symbolically:

$$R \subseteq A \times B \iff R \in \mathcal{P}(A \times B)$$

where $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ denotes the power set of $A \times B$. If an ordered pair $(a, b) \in R$, we denote this relational binding by $a \, R \, b$, read as "$a$ is related to $b$ under relation $R$." Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, we write $a \!\not R\, b$. When the underlying sets coincide such that $B = A$, $R$ is termed a relation on the set $A$ (i.e., $R \subseteq A \times A$).

Combinatorial Measure of Relations: Since the power set of any finite set $S$ possessing cardinality $|S| = k$ contains exactly $2^k$ distinct subsets, the total number of distinct relations that can be constructed from a set $A$ (with $|A| = m$) to a set $B$ (with $|B| = n$) is precisely:

$$N_{\text{relations}} = |\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{|A \times B|} = 2^{m \cdot n}$$

For a relation on a single set $A$ where $|A| = n$, the total number of distinct relations is $2^{n^2}$.

Structural Components of a Relation:

  • Domain of $R$ ($\operatorname{Dom}(R)$): The set of all initial coordinates extracted from the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. Formally: $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a \in A : \exists b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq A$$
  • Range of $R$ ($\operatorname{Ran}(R)$): The set of all terminal coordinates extracted from the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. Formally: $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{b \in B : \exists a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq B$$
  • Codomain of $R$: The entire designated target set $B$, regardless of whether every element $b \in B$ participates in the relation. Hence, $\operatorname{Ran}(R) \subseteq \operatorname{Codom}(R) = B$.

1.3 Rigorous Taxonomy of Relations on a Set

Let $A$ be a non-empty set. Relations on $A$ exhibit varied structural symmetries, algebraic behaviors, and structural properties. In the Class 12 CBSE curriculum, relations are classified into fundamental and higher-order categories:

1.3.1 Trivial Relations: Empty and Universal Relations

  • Empty (or Void) Relation: The empty set $\emptyset$ is a subset of every set, hence $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $R = \emptyset$ is the empty relation on $A$. It contains zero ordered pairs: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \notin R$.
    Illustrative Example: Let $A$ be the set of all students in a boys' school. Let $R = \{(a, b) \in A \times A : a \text{ is the sister of } b\}$. Because all elements are male, no such pair exists; hence $R = \emptyset$.
  • Universal Relation: The complete Cartesian product $A \times A$ is trivially a subset of itself. The relation $R = A \times A$ is the universal relation on $A$. Under this relation, every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R$.
    Illustrative Example: Let $A$ be the set of all human beings. Let $R = \{(a, b) \in A \times A : |h(a) - h(b)| \ge 0\}$, where $h(x)$ represents the height of individual $x$ in centimeters. Since the absolute difference between any two real numbers is always non-negative, $(a, b) \in R$ for all $a, b \in A$; thus, $R = A \times A$.

Both the empty relation and the universal relation are often referred to as the trivial (or extreme) relations on $A$.

1.3.2 The Identity Relation

The identity relation on a set $A$, conventionally symbolized by $I_A$ or $\Delta_A$ (the diagonal relation), is defined as:

$$I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$$

In the identity relation, every element of $A$ is related strictly and exclusively to itself. If $a \neq b$, the pair $(a, b)$ can never belong to $I_A$. This distinction is vital when contrasting the identity relation with the broader concept of a reflexive relation.

1.3.3 Core Structural Properties: Reflexivity, Symmetry, and Transitivity

(A) Reflexive Relations:

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is called reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. Formally:

$$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$

Critical Nuance (Reflexive vs. Identity): For $R$ to be reflexive, it is necessary that $I_A \subseteq R$. While an identity relation forbids any pair $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$, a reflexive relation allows additional non-diagonal elements, provided the diagonal elements $\{(a, a) : a \in A\}$ are entirely present. Thus, every identity relation is reflexive, but not every reflexive relation is an identity relation.

Combinatorial Derivation of Reflexive Relations: Let $|A| = n$. The total number of elements in $A \times A$ is $n^2$. These $n^2$ pairs can be partitioned into $n$ diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_i)$ and $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \neq j$. For a relation to be reflexive, all $n$ diagonal pairs must be included (1 choice each). Each of the remaining $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal pairs may either be included or excluded independently (2 choices each). Applying the multiplication rule:

$$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \times 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

(B) Symmetric Relations:

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is called symmetric if the presence of any directed relation implies the presence of its reverse. Formally:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$$

In terms of relational inversion, defining $R^{-1} = \{(b, a) : (a, b) \in R\}$, a relation is symmetric if and only if $R = R^{-1}$.

Combinatorial Derivation of Symmetric Relations: Consider the $n \times n$ matrix representation of a relation on a set of cardinality $n$. There are $n$ diagonal elements and $\frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$ unordered pairs of distinct elements $\{a_i, a_j\}$. For symmetry:

  • Each of the $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ has 2 choices (present or absent).
  • For each distinct pair $\{a_i, a_j\}$, the pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ must either both be present or both absent. This yields 2 choices per pair.

The total number of symmetric relations is therefore:

$$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

(C) Transitive Relations:

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is called transitive if whenever an element $a$ is related to $b$ and $b$ is related to $c$, then $a$ is also related to $c$. Formally:

$$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad [(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$$

Crucial Analytical Warning for Examinations: The conditional implication $P \implies Q$ is vacuously true whenever the premise $P$ is false. Thus, if there exist no pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ with a shared intermediate element $b$, the condition for transitivity is satisfied automatically. For instance, in the set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, the relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ is transitive because the hypothesis $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R$ cannot be formed with distinct or matching pairs, so the condition holds vacuously.

1.3.4 Anti-Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed anti-symmetric if:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad [(a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R] \implies a = b$$

Alternatively stated, if $a \neq b$, then $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ cannot both belong to $R$. The partial order relation $\le$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is a canonical example of an anti-symmetric relation: $x \le y \land y \le x \implies x = y$. Anti-symmetry is not the opposite of symmetry; a relation can be both symmetric and anti-symmetric (e.g., $I_A$), or neither.

1.4 Equivalence Relations and Partitions

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is defined as an Equivalence Relation if and only if it simultaneously satisfies three core properties:

  1. Reflexivity: $(a, a) \in R, \quad \forall a \in A$
  2. Symmetry: $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R, \quad \forall a, b \in A$
  3. Transitivity: $[(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R, \quad \forall a, b, c \in A$

1.4.1 Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any element $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$ or $\operatorname{cl}(a)$, is the set of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:

$$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$

Because $R$ is symmetric, $(x, a) \in R \iff (a, x) \in R$, so $[a] = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

Fundamental Properties of Equivalence Classes:

  • Non-emptiness: Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in A$. Therefore, $a \in [a]$, proving that no equivalence class is empty: $[a] \neq \emptyset$.
  • Identical or Disjoint Property: For any two elements $a, b \in A$, their equivalence classes are either strictly identical or completely disjoint: $$[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad [a] \cap [b] = \emptyset \iff (a, b) \notin R$$ Proof of Property: Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$. This means $(x, a) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$. By symmetry, $(a, x) \in R$. By transitivity applied to $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, we deduce $(a, b) \in R$. Now take any arbitrary $y \in [a] \implies (y, a) \in R$. Using $(a, b) \in R$ and transitivity, $(y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$, showing $[a] \subseteq [b]$. By symmetric reasoning, $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Thus, $[a] = [b]$.
  • Exhaustive Union (Partitioning): The union of all equivalence classes reconstructs the original set $A$: $$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$

1.4.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Theorem Statement: An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ partitions the set $A$ into mutually disjoint, non-empty subsets (the equivalence classes) whose union is $A$. Conversely, any partition $\mathcal{P} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ uniquely defines an equivalence relation $R$ on $A$ under which the sets $A_i$ serve as the equivalence classes.

A partition of $A$ is a family of subsets $\{A_1, A_2, \dots\}$ such that:

  1. $A_i \neq \emptyset$ for all $i$.
  2. $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ whenever $i \neq j$ (pairwise disjoint).
  3. $\bigcup_i A_i = A$.

The number of distinct equivalence relations on a finite set of cardinality $n$ equals the number of partitions of that set, given by the Bell Number $B_n$, which satisfies the recurrence relation:

$$B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} B_k, \quad \text{with } B_0 = 1, B_1 = 1, B_2 = 2, B_3 = 5, B_4 = 15, B_5 = 52$$

1.4.3 Classical Benchmark Examples of Equivalence Relations

Example 1: Congruence Modulo $m$ on the Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)

Let $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by:

$$a \, R \, b \iff a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b) \iff a - b = k \cdot m \quad (\text{for some } k \in \mathbb{Z})$$

Rigor Check:

  • Reflexive: For all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot m$. Thus $m \mid (a - a)$, so $(a, a) \in R$.
  • Symmetric: If $(a, b) \in R$, then $a - b = km$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiplying by $-1$ gives $b - a = (-k)m$. Since $-k \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid (b - a)$, so $(b, a) \in R$.
  • Transitive: If $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, then $a - b = k_1 m$ and $b - c = k_2 m$ with $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding these yields: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 m + k_2 m \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)m$$ Since $k_1 + k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid (a - c)$, confirming $(a, c) \in R$.

This equivalence relation yields precisely $m$ disjoint equivalence classes, termed the residue classes modulo $m$:

$$[0] = \{\dots, -2m, -m, 0, m, 2m, \dots\}, \quad [1] = \{\dots, 1-m, 1, 1+m, 1+2m, \dots\}, \quad \dots, \quad [m-1]$$

Example 2: Cross-Product Relation on Ordered Pairs ($\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$)

Define a relation $R$ on the Cartesian product set $A = \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ (where $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$) by:

$$(a, b) \, R \, (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$$

Rigor Check:

  • Reflexivity: For any $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$, we have $a + b = b + a$ by commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{N}$. Hence, $(a, b) \, R \, (a, b)$.
  • Symmetry: Let $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d) \implies a + d = b + c$. By commutativity and equality symmetry, $c + b = d + a \implies c + b = a + d \implies c + b = d + a$. Thus $(c, d) \, R \, (a, b)$.
  • Transitivity: Let $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \, R \, (e, f)$. Then: $$a + d = b + c \quad \text{and} \quad c + f = d + e$$ Adding the equations: $$(a + d) + (c + f) = (b + c) + (d + e) \implies a + f + (c + d) = b + e + (c + d)$$ By the additive cancellation property of integers: $$a + f = b + e \implies (a, b) \, R \, (e, f)$$

Thus, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. Each equivalence class corresponds to an integer via the mapping $[(a, b)] \mapsto a - b$, modeling the formal construction of $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ via Grothendieck group completion.

1.5 Advanced Theory of Functions (Mappings)

A function (or mapping) $f$ from a set $X$ to a set $Y$, denoted by $f: X \to Y$, is a specialized relation $f \subseteq X \times Y$ satisfying two fundamental axioms:

  1. Totality (Universal Existence): Every element of the domain must be mapped: $$\forall x \in X, \quad \exists y \in Y \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Well-Definedness): No element of the domain can map to more than one element in the codomain: $$\forall x \in X \text{ and } \forall y_1, y_2 \in Y, \quad [(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f] \implies y_1 = y_2$$

When $(x, y) \in f$, we write $y = f(x)$, where $y$ is called the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is called the pre-image of $y$ under $f$.

Cardinality of Function Spaces: If $|X| = m$ and $|Y| = n$, the total number of distinct functions that can be constructed from $X$ to $Y$ is:

$$N_{\text{functions}} = |Y|^{|X|} = n^m$$

This follows because each of the $m$ distinct domain elements can be independently mapped to any of the $n$ available codomain elements.

1.6 Classification of Functions: Injectivity, Surjectivity, and Bijectivity

1.6.1 Injective (One-to-One) Functions

A function $f: X \to Y$ is said to be injective (or one-to-one) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the codomain.

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in X, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$

Taking the logically equivalent contrapositive statement, which serves as the primary algebraic proof technique:

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in X, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

A function that fails this injectivity criterion is termed many-to-one:

$$\exists x_1, x_2 \in X \text{ such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$

Analytical and Graphical Characterizations of Injectivity:

  • Horizontal Line Test: Let $f: D \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. The function $f$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = c$ (where $c \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the Cartesian plot of $y = f(x)$ at at most one point. If any horizontal line intersects the curve at two or more distinct points, those intersection points share the same $y$-value but possess distinct $x$-coordinates, violating injectivity.
  • Monotonicity Criterion (Calculus Connection): If $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then: $$\left(\forall x \in (a, b), f'(x) > 0\right) \lor \left(\forall x \in (a, b), f'(x) < 0\right) \implies f \text{ is strictly monotonic} \implies f \text{ is injective}$$ If the derivative $f'(x)$ changes sign over the interval, there exist local extrema, which implies the existence of points where a horizontal line intersects the function multiple times, making it many-to-one.

Combinatorial Count of Injective Functions:

Let $|X| = m$ and $|Y| = n$.

  • If $m > n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two domain elements must map to the same codomain element. Thus, no injective function can exist: $N_{\text{injective}} = 0$.
  • If $m \le n$, the first domain element has $n$ choices, the second has $n-1$ choices, ..., and the $m$-th element has $n - m + 1$ choices. Hence, the number of injective functions is given by permutations: $$N_{\text{injective}} = {}^n P_m = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}$$

1.6.2 Surjective (Onto) Functions

A function $f: X \to Y$ is called surjective (or onto) if every element of the codomain $Y$ is the image of at least one element of the domain $X$. Formally:

$$\forall y \in Y, \quad \exists x \in X \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$

In set-theoretic terms, a function is surjective if and only if its range equals its codomain:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = f(X) = Y$$

A function that fails to be surjective is called an into function, meaning that $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subsetneq Y$ (i.e., there exists at least one $y \in Y$ with no pre-image in $X$).

Combinatorial Count of Surjective Functions:

Let $|X| = m$ and $|Y| = n$.

  • If $m < n$, by cardinality constraints, the range can contain at most $m$ distinct elements, so it can never span all $n$ elements of $Y$. Thus: $N_{\text{surjective}} = 0$.
  • If $m \ge n$, the number of surjective functions from $X$ to $Y$ is calculated using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE) or via Stirling numbers of the second kind $\left\{ \begin{matrix} m \\ n \end{matrix} \right\}$: $$N_{\text{surjective}} = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = n! \left\{ \begin{matrix} m \\ n \end{matrix} \right\}$$

1.6.3 Bijective (One-to-One and Onto) Functions

A function $f: X \to Y$ is termed bijective (or a one-to-one correspondence) if it is both injective and surjective. Bijective mappings establish an exact, reversible pair matching between the elements of $X$ and $Y$.

Cardinality Invariance: If $X$ and $Y$ are finite sets, a bijection between them can exist if and only if:

$$|X| = |Y| = n$$

In that case, the total number of bijective functions is given by:

$$N_{\text{bijective}} = n!$$

If $|X| \neq |Y|$, no bijective function can be constructed between them.

1.7 Composition of Functions

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions. The composition of $g$ and $f$, denoted by $g \circ f$ (read as "$g$ circle $f$" or "$g$ composed with $f$"), is a function mapping $A$ directly into $C$:

$$g \circ f: A \to C, \quad \text{defined by} \quad (g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$

The Fundamental Domain-Range Compatibility Criterion: The composite function $g \circ f$ is well-defined if and only if the range of the inner function is a subset of the domain of the outer function:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$$

If $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \not\subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$, then $g(f(x))$ is undefined for any $x \in A$ where $f(x) \notin \operatorname{Dom}(g)$. In such situations, the domain of the composite function must be restricted to:

$$\operatorname{Dom}(g \circ f) = \{x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f) : f(x) \in \operatorname{Dom}(g)\}$$

1.7.1 Structural Properties of Function Composition

  • Non-Commutativity: In general, composition is non-commutative: $$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$ Even when both compositions exist (such as when $f: A \to A$ and $g: A \to A$), the outputs are generally distinct.
    Analytical Counterexample: Let $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$ over $\mathbb{R}$. $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$$ $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(x^2) = 2(x^2) + 1 = 2x^2 + 1$$ Clearly, $4x^2 + 4x + 1 \neq 2x^2 + 1$ for most $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Associativity: Function composition is strictly associative whenever the compositions are well-defined. Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$. Then: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$ Direct Proof: For every $x \in A$: $$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ $$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ The images match for every $x \in A$, establishing identity between the two composite mappings.
  • Identity Preservation: Let $f: A \to B$. If $I_A: A \to A$ and $I_B: B \to B$ represent the identity functions on sets $A$ and $B$ respectively ($I_A(x) = x, \, \forall x \in A$), then: $$f \circ I_A = f \quad \text{and} \quad I_B \circ f = f$$

1.7.2 Inheritance Theorems for Injective and Surjective Compositions

Theorem 1: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both injective, then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also injective.

Formal Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$. Assume $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. $$\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ Since $g$ is injective, $g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$. Applying this with $u = f(x_1)$ and $v = f(x_2)$ yields: $$f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$ Next, because $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, proving that $g \circ f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both surjective, then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also surjective.

Formal Proof: Let $z$ be an arbitrary element of $C$. Because $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists an element $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Because $f: A \to B$ is surjective, for this $y \in B$, there exists an element $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Substituting $y$ into the first expression: $$g(f(x)) = z \implies (g \circ f)(x) = z$$ Since for every $z \in C$ there exists an $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$, $g \circ f$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3 (Reverse Implications):

  • If $g \circ f$ is injective, then the inner function $f$ must be injective. (However, the outer function $g$ may not be injective).
    Proof sketch: If $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$, then $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence $f$ is injective.
  • If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then the outer function $g$ must be surjective. (However, the inner function $f$ may not be surjective).
    Proof sketch: For any $z \in C$, surjectivity of $g \circ f$ yields an $x \in A$ such that $g(f(x)) = z$. Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have $g(y) = z$. Thus, every $z \in C$ has a pre-image $y \in B$, confirming $g$ is surjective.

1.8 Invertible Functions and Inverse Mappings

A function $f: X \to Y$ is said to be invertible if there exists another function $g: Y \to X$ such that:

$$g \circ f = I_X \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_Y$$

where $I_X$ and $I_Y$ are the identity functions on sets $X$ and $Y$, respectively. The function $g$ is uniquely determined and is called the inverse of $f$, denoted by $f^{-1}$.

1.8.1 The Fundamental Equivalence Theorem

A function $f: X \to Y$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection (both injective and surjective).

Rigorous Two-Way Proof:

Part 1 ($\implies$): If $f$ is invertible, it is bijective.

Assume $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: Y \to X$ satisfying $g \circ f = I_X$ and $f \circ g = I_Y$.

  • Injectivity: Suppose $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$ to both sides: $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies I_X(x_1) = I_X(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Thus, $f$ is injective.
  • Surjectivity: Let $y \in Y$ be arbitrary. Consider $x = g(y) \in X$. Applying $f$: $$f(x) = f(g(y)) = (f \circ g)(y) = I_Y(y) = y$$ Thus, every $y \in Y$ has a pre-image in $X$ under $f$, proving $f$ is surjective.

Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective.

Part 2 ($\impliedby$): If $f$ is bijective, it is invertible.

Assume $f: X \to Y$ is bijective.

  • Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in Y$, there exists an $x \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is injective, this element $x$ is unique.

We define a rule $g: Y \to X$ by assigning to each $y \in Y$ this unique element $x \in X$, so $g(y) = x$.

  • For any $x \in X$, let $y = f(x)$. By definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. Thus: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_X(x) \implies g \circ f = I_X$$
  • For any $y \in Y$, let $x = g(y)$. By definition, $f(x) = y$. Thus: $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_Y(y) \implies f \circ g = I_Y$$

Both conditions hold, confirming that $g = f^{-1}$ and $f$ is invertible. $\blacksquare$

1.8.2 Practical Algorithm for Determining the Inverse Function

  1. Set $y = f(x)$, treating $y$ as a variable in the codomain and $x$ in the domain.
  2. Rearrange this equation algebraically to solve for the input variable $x$ explicitly in terms of the output variable $y$, yielding an expression of the form $x = \psi(y)$.
  3. Verify that for every $y \in \operatorname{Codom}(f)$, the computed $x$ lies within $\operatorname{Dom}(f)$.
  4. Replace $x$ with $f^{-1}(y)$, giving $f^{-1}(y) = \psi(y)$, or rewrite with independent variable $x$ as $f^{-1}(x) = \psi(x)$.
  5. Confirm via algebraic composition that $(f \circ f^{-1})(x) = x$ and $(f^{-1} \circ f)(x) = x$.

1.8.3 Geometric Properties of Inverse Functions

Because the inverse interchanges inputs and outputs, $(a, b) \in f \iff (b, a) \in f^{-1}$. Geometrically, the point $(b, a)$ is the orthogonal reflection of $(a, b)$ across the line $y = x$. Therefore:

The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of the graph of $y = f(x)$ across the line $y = x$.

Any point where the function intersects its own inverse must lie along the diagonal line $y = x$ (for monotonically increasing functions), meaning $f(x) = x$.

1.9 Binary Operations and Algebraic Structures

A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function that maps every ordered pair in $A \times A$ to an element in $A$. Formally:

$$*: A \times A \to A, \quad \text{where } *(a, b) \text{ is denoted by } a * b$$

This definition encapsulates the closure property: for all $a, b \in A$, the result $a * b$ must also belong to $A$.

Total Number of Binary Operations: The number of binary operations on a set $A$ with $|A| = n$ is the total number of functions from $A \times A$ to $A$:

$$N_{\text{binary operations}} = |A|^{|A \times A|} = n^{(n^2)}$$

1.9.1 Core Properties of Binary Operations

  1. Commutativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is commutative if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$ Counting Commutative Operations: On a finite set of size $n$, the operation table (Cayley table) is an $n \times n$ matrix. For commutativity, entries must be symmetric across the main diagonal: $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$. The number of independent entries on and above the main diagonal is $\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$. Each entry can be chosen in $n$ ways, giving: $$N_{\text{commutative}} = n^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$
  2. Associativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is associative if: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$ Associativity allows expressions like $a_1 * a_2 * \dots * a_k$ to be written without parentheses, as grouping does not alter the evaluation.
  3. Distributivity: Let $*$ and $\circ$ be two binary operations on $A$. Then $*$ distributes over $\circ$ from the left if: $$a * (b \circ c) = (a * b) \circ (a * c), \quad \forall a, b, c \in A$$ and from the right if: $$(b \circ c) * a = (b * a) \circ (c * a), \quad \forall a, b, c \in A$$

1.9.2 Identity and Inverse Elements

  • Identity Element: An element $e \in A$ is an identity element with respect to $*$ if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a$$ Theorem (Uniqueness of Identity): If an identity element exists for an operation $*$, it is unique.
    Proof: Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identities. Treating $e_1$ as identity: $e_2 * e_1 = e_2$. Treating $e_2$ as identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. By definition of identity, $e_1 * e_2 = e_2 * e_1$. Hence, $e_1 = e_2$. $\blacksquare$
  • Invertible Elements: Given an identity element $e \in A$, an element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that: $$a * b = b * a = e$$ The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$, denoted by $a^{-1}$.
    Theorem (Uniqueness of Inverse): If $*$ is associative and an element $a$ is invertible, its inverse is unique.
    Proof: Suppose $b_1$ and $b_2$ are both inverses of $a$. Then $a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e$ and $a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$. Consider: $$b_1 * (a * b_2) = b_1 * e = b_1$$ Using associativity: $$(b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$ Since $b_1 * (a * b_2) = (b_1 * a) * b_2$, it follows that $b_1 = b_2$. $\blacksquare$

1.10 CBSE Board Examination Architecture: Proof Blueprints and Common Pitfalls

1.10.1 Structured Board Exam Proof Templates

In Class 12 CBSE board assessments, solutions are evaluated under step-marking schemes. Below are standard analytical templates designed to satisfy formal board rubrics:

Standard Proof Blueprint: Showing a Rational/Algebraic Relation is an Equivalence Relation

Problem Statement: Let $\mathbb{R}$ be the set of real numbers. Prove that $R = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} : a - b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is an equivalence relation.

  1. Reflexivity:
    Let $a \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary.
    Then $a - a = 0$.
    Since $0 = \frac{0}{1} \in \mathbb{Q}$, we have $a - a \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Therefore, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
    $\implies R$ is reflexive.
  2. Symmetry:
    Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$.
    $\implies a - b \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Hence, $a - b = q$ for some $q \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Multiplying both sides by $-1$: $-(a - b) = -q \implies b - a = -q$.
    Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under negation, $-q \in \mathbb{Q}$, which implies $b - a \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Therefore, $(b, a) \in R$.
    $\implies R$ is symmetric.
  3. Transitivity:
    Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$.
    $\implies a - b = q_1$ and $b - c = q_2$, where $q_1, q_2 \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Adding the two equations: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = q_1 + q_2 \implies a - c = q_1 + q_2$$
    Since the set of rational numbers is closed under addition, $q_1 + q_2 \in \mathbb{Q}$, which implies $a - c \in \mathbb{Q}$.
    Therefore, $(a, c) \in R$.
    $\implies R$ is transitive.
  4. Conclusion:
    Since the relation $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{R}$.

Standard Proof Blueprint: Verifying Invertibility via Bijection

Problem Statement: Let $f: \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ be defined by $f(x) = \frac{4x + 3}{3x - 4}$. Prove that $f$ is bijective and determine its inverse $f^{-1}$.

  1. Injectivity (One-to-One):
    Let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\frac{4x_1 + 3}{3x_1 - 4} = \frac{4x_2 + 3}{3x_2 - 4}$$ Cross-multiplying: $$(4x_1 + 3)(3x_2 - 4) = (4x_2 + 3)(3x_1 - 4)$$ $$12x_1 x_2 - 16x_1 + 9x_2 - 12 = 12x_1 x_2 - 16x_2 + 9x_1 - 12$$ Subtracting $12x_1 x_2 - 12$ from both sides: $$-16x_1 + 9x_2 = -16x_2 + 9x_1$$ $$9x_2 + 16x_2 = 9x_1 + 16x_1 \implies 25x_2 = 25x_1 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Therefore, $f$ is injective.
  2. Surjectivity (Onto):
    Let $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ be an arbitrary element in the codomain.
    Set $y = f(x)$ and solve for $x$: $$y = \frac{4x + 3}{3x - 4} \implies y(3x - 4) = 4x + 3 \implies 3xy - 4y = 4x + 3$$ $$3xy - 4x = 4y + 3 \implies x(3y - 4) = 4y + 3 \implies x = \frac{4y + 3}{3y - 4}$$ Since $y \neq \frac{4}{3}$, the denominator $3y - 4 \neq 0$, so $x$ is well-defined.
    We check whether $x = \frac{4}{3}$: $$\frac{4y + 3}{3y - 4} = \frac{4}{3} \implies 3(4y + 3) = 4(3y - 4) \implies 12y + 9 = 12y - 16 \implies 9 = -16 \quad (\text{impossible})$$ Thus, $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ for every $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$.
    Evaluating $f(x)$: $$f(x) = f\left(\frac{4y + 3}{3y - 4}\right) = \frac{4\left(\frac{4y + 3}{3y - 4}\right) + 3}{3\left(\frac{4y + 3}{3y - 4}\right) - 4} = \frac{16y + 12 + 9y - 12}{12y + 9 - 12y + 16} = \frac{25y}{25} = y$$ Thus, every $y$ has a pre-image in the domain, confirming $f$ is surjective.
  3. Determining the Inverse:
    Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore invertible: $$f^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}, \quad f^{-1}(x) = \frac{4x + 3}{3x - 4}$$ (In this case, $f$ is an involution: $f = f^{-1}$).

1.10.2 Frequent Conceptual Traps and Board Pitfalls

  • The Transitivity Illusion in Non-Connected Pairs: Many students mistakenly claim that relations like $R = \{(1, 2), (3, 4)\}$ on the set $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ are not transitive because $(1, 4)$ is absent. However, transitivity requires $(a, c) \in R$ only when $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ share a common intermediate element $b$. Here, no such chained pair exists; hence, the transitivity condition is satisfied vacuously.
  • Confusing Reflexive with Identity: A relation $R$ on $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ given by $R = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2)\}$ is reflexive because all diagonal pairs are present. However, it is not an identity relation due to the presence of $(1, 2)$. Remember: $\text{Identity} \subsetneq \text{Reflexive}$.
  • Codomain vs. Range Ambiguity in Surjectivity: Proving that a function is onto requires showing that the range equals the given codomain. If the codomain is changed (e.g., $f(x) = x^2$ as $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ versus $f: \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty)$), surjectivity changes as well: the first is into, whereas the second is onto. Always confirm the specified codomain before writing the proof.
  • Failure to Check Inverse Domain Restrictions: When solving $x$ in terms of $y$, students often neglect to verify that the derived expression avoids division by zero and stays within the stated domain of $f$. Explicit verification of $x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)$ is required for full credit.
  • Composition Notation Confusion: The notation $(g \circ f)(x)$ denotes $g(f(x))$, meaning $f$ is evaluated first and $g$ second. Writing $(g \circ f)(x) = f(g(x))$ reverses the intended order and results in a zero mark for that derivation.

Formal Mathematical Derivation Bank

Derivation 1: Number of Reflexive Relations on a Set of Cardinality $n$

Objective: Prove that the number of reflexive relations on a set $A$ with $|A| = n$ is $2^{n(n - 1)}$.

Step 1: Structure of the Cartesian Product $A \times A$

Let $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$. The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $n \times n = n^2$ ordered pairs:

$$A \times A = \{(a_i, a_j) \mid 1 \le i, j \le n\}$$

Step 2: Partition of Elements in $A \times A$

The total set of ordered pairs can be split into two disjoint categories:

  • Diagonal Elements: Pairs of the form $(a_i, a_i)$ where $i = j$. The count of such pairs is: $$N_D = n$$
  • Non-Diagonal Elements: Pairs of the form $(a_i, a_j)$ where $i \ne j$. The count of such pairs is: $$N_{ND} = n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$$

Step 3: Imposition of the Reflexive Constraint

A relation $R \subseteq A \times A$ is reflexive if and only if:

$$\forall a_i \in A, \quad (a_i, a_i) \in R$$

Thus, for every diagonal pair $(a_i, a_i)$, there is only $1$ deterministic choice: it must be included in $R$.

Step 4: Combinatorial Selection of Non-Diagonal Elements

For each non-diagonal pair $(a_i, a_j)$ where $i \ne j$, there are $2$ independent choices: the pair can either belong to $R$ or not belong to $R$.

Since there are $n(n - 1)$ independent non-diagonal pairs, applying the Fundamental Counting Principle yields:

$$N_{\text{ref}} = 1^n \times 2^{n(n - 1)} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Number of Symmetric Relations on a Set of Cardinality $n$

Objective: Prove that the number of symmetric relations on a set $A$ with $|A| = n$ is $2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$.

Step 1: Matrix Representation of Relation $R$

A relation $R$ on $A$ can be represented by an $n \times n$ adjacency matrix $M = [m_{ij}]$, where:

$$m_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & (a_i, a_j) \in R \\ 0, & (a_i, a_j) \notin R \end{cases}$$

A relation is symmetric if and only if $M = M^T$, which requires $m_{ij} = m_{ji}$ for all $1 \le i, j \le n$.

Step 2: Counting Free Independent Parameters

Due to the symmetry condition $m_{ij} = m_{ji}$, choices for elements strictly below the main diagonal are uniquely determined by the choices made for elements strictly above the main diagonal. Hence, independent binary choices exist only on and above the main diagonal:

  • Number of diagonal elements $(i = j)$: $n$
  • Number of strictly upper-triangular elements $(i < j)$: $\frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$

Step 3: Total Independent Pairings

The total number of independent entries $k$ is the sum of diagonal and upper-triangular positions:

$$k = n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} = \frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2} = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2}$$

Step 4: Cardinality Calculation

Each of the $k$ independent positions can be independently assigned $0$ or $1$. Therefore:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^k = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: The Inverse of a Composite Function, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$

Objective: Given bijections $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$, prove that $(g \circ f)$ is invertible and $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

Step 1: State Inverse Criteria

By definition, a function $h: A \to C$ has an inverse $h^{-1}: C \to A$ if and only if:

$$h^{-1} \circ h = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad h \circ h^{-1} = I_C$$

Here, $h = g \circ f$. We test the candidate inverse $k = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}: C \to A$.

Step 2: Verification of Left Invertibility

Compute $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)$ using the associative property of function composition:

$$\begin{aligned} (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ [g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)] \\ &= f^{-1} \circ [(g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f] \end{aligned}$$

Since $g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$ (the identity function on $B$):

$$\begin{aligned} f^{-1} \circ [(g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f] &= f^{-1} \circ [I_B \circ f] \\ &= f^{-1} \circ f \\ &= I_A \end{aligned}$$

Step 3: Verification of Right Invertibility

Similarly, evaluate $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$:

$$\begin{aligned} (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ [f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})] \\ &= g \circ [(f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}] \end{aligned}$$

Since $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$:

$$\begin{aligned} g \circ [(f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}] &= g \circ [I_B \circ g^{-1}] \\ &= g \circ g^{-1} \\ &= I_C \end{aligned}$$

Conclusion:

Because $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ satisfies both identity conditions for $g \circ f$, by uniqueness of the inverse:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Partition Disjointness)

Objective: Prove that if $R$ is an equivalence relation on set $A$, any two equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either identical or disjoint, i.e., $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$.

Step 1: Set Definition of Equivalence Class

The equivalence class of an element $x \in A$ is defined as:

$$[x] = \{y \in A \mid (x, y) \in R\}$$

Step 2: Non-Empty Intersection Hypothesis

Assume $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $c \in A$ such that:

$$c \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad c \in [b]$$

By definition of an equivalence class, this implies:

$$(a, c) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (b, c) \in R$$

Step 3: Application of Symmetry and Transitivity to Establish Relation Between $a$ and $b$

Since $R$ is symmetric, $(b, c) \in R \implies (c, b) \in R$.

Now, using the transitive property with $(a, c) \in R$ and $(c, b) \in R$:

$$(a, c) \in R \land (c, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$$

Step 4: Prove Mutual Subset Containment ($[a] \subseteq [b]$)

Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $[a]$. Then:

$$(a, x) \in R$$

By symmetry, $(x, a) \in R$. Knowing $(a, b) \in R$ and applying transitivity:

$$(x, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (x, b) \in R$$

Applying symmetry once more gives $(b, x) \in R$, which implies $x \in [b]$. Hence:

$$[a] \subseteq [b]$$

Step 5: Prove Inverse Subset Containment ($[b] \subseteq [a]$)

By symmetry of the initial conditions, swapping the roles of $a$ and $b$ produces:

$$[b] \subseteq [a]$$

Conclusion:

Since $[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a]$, we have $[a] = [b]$. Therefore, equivalence classes are either strictly disjoint or completely identical.

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Number of Surjective Functions from an $n$-Element Set to a $2$-Element Set

Objective: Prove that the number of onto functions $f: A \to B$, where $|A| = n \ge 2$ and $|B| = 2$, is $2^n - 2$.

Step 1: Total Number of Unrestricted Mappings

Let $A = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ and $B = \{y_1, y_2\}$.

For each element $x_i \in A$, there are $2$ possible choices in $B$. By the multiplication principle, total functions are:

$$N_{\text{total}} = 2 \times 2 \times \dots \times 2 = 2^n$$

Step 2: Identification of Non-Surjective Mappings

A function $f$ fails to be surjective if and only if its range is a proper subset of $B$.

Since $|B| = 2$, the only proper non-empty subsets are singleton sets:

  • Case 1: $\text{Range}(f) = \{y_1\}$. Every $x \in A$ maps to $y_1$: $$f(x_i) = y_1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\} \implies 1 \text{ constant function}$$
  • Case 2: $\text{Range}(f) = \{y_2\}$. Every $x \in A$ maps to $y_2$: $$f(x_i) = y_2 \quad \forall i \in \{1, \dots, n\} \implies 1 \text{ constant function}$$

Step 3: Subtraction of Defective Outcomes

The two cases are mutually exclusive because $\{y_1\} \cap \{y_2\} = \emptyset$. Total non-surjective functions:

$$N_{\text{not surj}} = 1 + 1 = 2$$

Step 4: Final Count

Subtracting invalid mappings from the universe of all mappings:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = N_{\text{total}} - N_{\text{not surj}} = 2^n - 2$$

$\blacksquare$
