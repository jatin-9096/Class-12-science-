1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

SECTION 1.0: EPISTEMOLOGICAL & SET-THEORETIC FOUNDATIONS
The architecture of modern higher secondary mathematics, particularly within the framework established by the Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) and aligned with standard real analysis, rests fundamentally on the formalisation of relationships between collections of mathematical objects. The transition from Class 11 naive set theory to Class 12 abstract analysis necessitates an unyielding, rigorous treatment of Cartesian products, binary relations, and functional mappings.

Let $A$ and $B$ be arbitrary non-empty sets defined within a universal universe $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian Product, denoted $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate is drawn exclusively from the domain set $A$ and the second coordinate is drawn exclusively from the codomain set $B$. Formally, this is expressed in set-builder notation as: $$A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$ The ordered nature of the pair dictates that $(a, b) = (c, d) \iff (a = c \land b = d)$. If the cardinalities of the sets are finite, designated by $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ respectively, the cardinality of the resulting Cartesian product space is precisely $|A \times B| = mn$. An algebraic consequence of this definition is non-commutativity: $A \times B \neq B \times A$ whenever $A \neq B$ and both sets are non-empty. The power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ denotes the set of all subsets of $A \times B$.

A Binary Relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any arbitrary subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. That is, $R \subseteq A \times B$, or equivalently, $R \in \mathcal{P}(A \times B)$. If $(a, b) \in R$, we write the infix notation $a R b$, interpreted as “$a$ is related to $b$ under $R$”. Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, we write $a \cancel{R} b$. In the specialized case where $A = B$, $R$ is termed an endorelation or simply a relation on $A$, such that $R \subseteq A \times A$.

The totality of all possible relations that can be constructed on a finite set $A$ with $|A| = n$ is governed by the cardinality of the power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$. Because $|A \times A| = n^2$, the total number of distinct relations is: $$N_{\text{total}} = |\mathcal{P}(A \times A)| = 2^{n^2}$$ Every relation $R \subseteq A \times B$ possesses an intrinsic Domain, Range, and Codomain: $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a \in A : \exists b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq A$$ $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{b \in B : \exists a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq B$$ $$\operatorname{Codom}(R) = B$$ The Inverse Relation $R^{-1} \subseteq B \times A$ is generated by reversing the order of the constituent coordinates: $R^{-1} = \{(b, a) \in B \times A : (a, b) \in R\}$. It follows identically that $\operatorname{Dom}(R^{-1}) = \operatorname{Ran}(R)$ and $\operatorname{Ran}(R^{-1}) = \operatorname{Dom}(R)$. SECTION 1.1: TAXONOMY OF RELATIONS ON A SET
When examining an endorelation $R$ on an arbitrary set $A$, mathematical analysis classifies the relation based on structural invariance under specific logical transformations.

1. The Empty (Void) Relation:
The empty set $\emptyset$ is a trivial subset of every set, hence $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $R = \emptyset$ is denominated the empty relation on $A$. Under this mapping, no element of $A$ is related to any element of $A$: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \notin R$$
2. The Universal Relation:
The maximal subset of $A \times A$ is the product space itself. The relation $R = A \times A$ is denominated the universal relation on $A$. Under this mapping, every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R$$ Both the empty and universal relations are frequently classified as the trivial relations on $A$.

3. The Identity Relation:
The identity relation on a set $A$, denoted exclusively as $I_A$ or $\Delta_A$ (the diagonal relation), is the set of all ordered pairs where an element is related strictly and exclusively to itself: $$I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$$ A vital pedagogical distinction enforced by CBSE examiners is that in the identity relation, an element is related only to itself. If any pair $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$ exists in $I_A$, or if any element $x \in A$ fails to have $(x, x) \in I_A$, the relation ceases to be the identity relation.

4. The Reflexive Relation:
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. Formally: $$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$ In terms of symbolic set inclusion, $R$ is reflexive on $A$ if and only if $I_A \subseteq R$. Analytical Contrast: The distinction between $I_A$ and a generic reflexive relation $R$ is critical. While $I_A$ prohibits any pair of the form $(a, b)$ where $a \neq b$, a reflexive relation merely requires the presence of all pairs of the form $(a, a)$; it is entirely permissive of pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$. Thus, every identity relation is reflexive, but not every reflexive relation is an identity relation.

5. The Symmetric Relation:
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed symmetric if the presence of any directed relation between two elements guarantees the presence of the reverse relation: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$$ In set-theoretic terms, $R$ is symmetric if and only if $R = R^{-1}$. Notice that symmetry is a conditional (implicative) statement. If for a given pair $a, b \in A$, $(a, b) \notin R$, the condition is vacuously satisfied for that pair. Symmetry is violated if and only if there exists at least one pair $(a, b) \in R$ such that $(b, a) \notin R$.

6. The Transitive Relation:
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed transitive if whenever an element $a$ is related to $b$, and $b$ is related to $c$, it necessarily follows that $a$ is related to $c$: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad [(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$$ Transitivity is similarly an implicative condition. The premise requires the existence of a chained intermediate element: $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. If no such intermediate chain exists in the relation, the premise is false, and by the laws of classical propositional logic ($F \implies P \equiv T$), the relation is vacuously transitive. For instance, the singleton relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ on $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ is strictly transitive because there exists no pair of the form $(2, z) \in R$ to activate the antecedent of the implication.

7. The Anti-Symmetric Relation:
Although not always tested directly as an equivalence type, anti-symmetry is foundational in order theory (posets). A relation $R$ on $A$ is anti-symmetric if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad [(a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R] \implies a = b$$ Equivalently, if $a \neq b$, then $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ cannot both belong to $R$. SECTION 1.2: EQUIVALENCE RELATIONS & THE PARTITION THEOREM
A relation $R$ defined on a set $A$ is universally designated an Equivalence Relation if and only if it satisfies the tripartite criteria:

  1. Reflexivity: $\forall a \in A, (a, a) \in R$
  2. Symmetry: $\forall a, b \in A, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$
  3. Transitivity: $\forall a, b, c \in A, [(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$
Equivalence relations capture the abstract essence of “sameness” or structural indistinguishability within a defined scope, without requiring strict mathematical identity.

Archetypal Equivalence Relation: Congruence Modulo $m$:
Let $\mathbb{Z}$ be the set of integers and let $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ be a fixed positive integer. Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by: $$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b) \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } a - b = km$$ Proof of Equivalence:
• Reflexivity: Let $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot m$. Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid (a - a)$, and therefore $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$.
• Symmetry: Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$. Then $m \mid (a - b)$, implying $a - b = km$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiplying by $-1$, we obtain $b - a = (-k)m$. Since $k \in \mathbb{Z} \implies -k \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $m \mid (b - a)$, hence $(b, a) \in R$.
• Transitivity: Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. Then $a - b = k_1 m$ and $b - c = k_2 m$ for some $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding these two linear equations: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 m + k_2 m \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)m$$ Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under addition, $(k_1 + k_2) \in \mathbb{Z}$. Thus $m \mid (a - c)$, confirming $(a, c) \in R$. Consequently, congruence modulo $m$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$.

Equivalence Classes:
Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any element $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$, $[a]_R$, or $\bar{a}$, is the subset of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$: $$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$ Because $R$ is symmetric, $(x, a) \in R \iff (a, x) \in R$, so $[a] = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

Fundamental Properties of Equivalence Classes:
Theorem 1.1: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$, and let $a, b \in A$. Then:
  1. $a \in [a]$ (Every element belongs to its own equivalence class, by reflexivity). Consequently, $[a] \neq \emptyset$.
  2. $(a, b) \in R \iff [a] = [b] \iff a \in [b]$.
  3. Either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. Two equivalence classes are either strictly identical or completely disjoint.
Proof of Property 3 (Disjointness or Identity):
Assume $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. We must demonstrate that this assumption forces $[a] = [b]$. Since the intersection is non-empty, there exists some element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$. By definition of equivalence classes: $$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R \quad (\text{by symmetry})$$ $$x \in [b] \implies (x, b) \in R$$ Applying transitivity to $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, we obtain $(a, b) \in R$. Now, let $y \in [a]$ be an arbitrary element. Then $(y, a) \in R$. Applying transitivity to $(y, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$ yields $(y, b) \in R$, which implies $y \in [b]$. Therefore, $[a] \subseteq [b]$. By exact symmetry of the argument, starting with an arbitrary $z \in [b]$ and utilizing $(b, a) \in R$, we prove that $[b] \subseteq [a]$. By mutual inclusion, $[a] = [b]$. Hence, if two equivalence classes share even a single common element, they are identical sets. If they share no elements, their intersection is empty. $\blacksquare$

The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Partition Theorem):
A partition $\mathcal{P}$ of a set $A$ is a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ whose universal union equals $A$:
  1. $\forall i \in I, A_i \neq \emptyset$
  2. $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$
  3. $\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = A$
Theorem 1.2: An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ partitions the set $A$ into mutually disjoint equivalence classes. Conversely, any partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $A$ uniquely induces an equivalence relation $R_{\mathcal{P}}$ on $A$ whose equivalence classes are precisely the components of the partition.

Proof:
$(\implies)$ Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$. Let $\mathcal{P} = \{[a] : a \in A\}$. By Property 1, every $[a]$ is non-empty. By Property 3, any two distinct equivalence classes are mutually disjoint. Furthermore, since $a \in [a]$ for every $a \in A$, we have: $$A = \bigcup_{a \in A} \{a\} \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A \implies \bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$ Thus, the set of equivalence classes forms a valid partition of $A$.
$(\impliedby)$ Conversely, let $\mathcal{P} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ be a partition of $A$. Define a relation $R$ on $A$ by: $$(a, b) \in R \iff \exists i \in I \text{ such that } a \in A_i \text{ and } b \in A_i$$ We verify the three conditions: - Reflexive: Since $\bigcup A_i = A$, every $a \in A$ must belong to some $A_k$. Therefore, $a$ and $a$ are both in $A_k$, meaning $(a, a) \in R$. - Symmetric: If $(a, b) \in R$, then $a, b \in A_k$. This trivially implies $b, a \in A_k$, so $(b, a) \in R$. - Transitive: If $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, then $a, b \in A_j$ and $b, c \in A_k$. Since $b \in A_j \cap A_k$, and the partition consists of pairwise disjoint sets, it follows that $A_j = A_k$. Hence $a, c \in A_j$, implying $(a, c) \in R$. Thus, $R$ is an equivalence relation. $\blacksquare$ SECTION 1.3: COMBINATORIAL ANALYSIS OF RELATIONS
A favourite testing ground for CBSE competitive edge and higher-order thinking skills (HOTS) questions involves computing the exact number of relations satisfying structural constraints on a finite set $A$ of cardinality $|A| = n$.

Represent the Cartesian product $A \times A$ as an $n \times n$ coordinate grid or boolean adjacency matrix $M = [m_{ij}]$, where $m_{ij} = 1$ if $(a_i, a_j) \in R$ and $0$ otherwise: $$\begin{pmatrix} (a_1, a_1) & (a_1, a_2) & \cdots & (a_1, a_n) \\ (a_2, a_1) & (a_2, a_2) & \cdots & (a_2, a_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ (a_n, a_1) & (a_n, a_2) & \cdots & (a_n, a_n) \end{pmatrix}$$ The matrix contains exactly $n^2$ elements, partitioned into: - The main diagonal: $n$ entries of the form $(a_i, a_i)$. - The off-diagonal pairs: $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$ entries, which form $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ symmetric unordered pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ with $i \neq j$.

1. Number of Reflexive Relations:
For $R$ to be reflexive, all $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ MUST be included in $R$. There is precisely $1$ choice for each diagonal entry. For the remaining $n^2 - n$ off-diagonal pairs, each may be either included or excluded independently (2 choices each). $$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \times 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$
2. Number of Symmetric Relations:
For $R$ to be symmetric: - The $n$ diagonal elements $(a_i, a_i)$ can be chosen independently (included or excluded): $2^n$ choices. - For each of the $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ off-diagonal pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, we cannot choose entries independently. Either both $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ belong to $R$, or neither does. Thus, there are 2 choices per pair. $$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$
3. Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations:
Combining both constraints: all $n$ diagonal elements must be present ($1$ choice each), and the $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ off-diagonal symmetric pairs have $2$ choices each: $$N_{\text{refl} \land \text{symm}} = 1^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$
4. Number of Anti-Symmetric Relations:
For anti-symmetry: - The $n$ diagonal elements can be chosen arbitrarily: $2^n$ choices. - For each of the $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ unordered pairs with $i \neq j$, we can have: (i) $(a_i, a_j) \in R$ only, (ii) $(a_j, a_i) \in R$ only, or (iii) neither belongs to $R$. Both cannot belong simultaneously. Thus, there are 3 choices per pair. $$N_{\text{anti-symmetric}} = 2^n \times 3^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$
5. Number of Equivalence Relations (Bell Numbers):
Because equivalence relations correspond bijectively to set partitions, the total number of equivalence relations on a set of cardinality $n$ is given by the $n$-th Bell Number $B_n$, which satisfies the recurrence relation: $$B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} B_k, \quad \text{with } B_0 = 1, B_1 = 1$$ Values for initial cardinalities: $B_1 = 1$, $B_2 = 2$, $B_3 = 5$, $B_4 = 15$, $B_5 = 52$. SECTION 2.0: FUNCTIONS AS MATHEMATICAL MAPPINGS
Historically regarded simply as formulas or algebraic expressions, modern functional analysis formalises a function as a constrained relation.

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets. A relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ is called a Function (or Mapping) from $A$ into $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, if and only if it satisfies two non-negotiable axioms:
  1. Totality (Existence): Every element of the domain set $A$ has an associated image in the codomain set $B$: $$\forall x \in A, \quad \exists y \in B \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Univalence (Uniqueness): No element in the domain set $A$ is associated with more than one distinct element in the codomain set $B$: $$\forall x \in A \text{ and } \forall y_1, y_2 \in B, \quad [(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f] \implies y_1 = y_2$$
When $(x, y) \in f$, we adopt the operational notation $y = f(x)$, designating $y$ as the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ as a pre-image of $y$ under $f$.

Domain, Codomain, and Range Distinction:
- Domain: $\operatorname{Dom}(f) = A$. In Class 12 real analysis, if the domain is not explicitly stated, the natural domain is the maximal subset of $\mathbb{R}$ for which the functional rule produces a defined, real-valued result (i.e., avoiding division by zero, negative radicands for even roots, and non-positive arguments for logarithms). - Codomain: $\operatorname{Codom}(f) = B$. The ambient target set specified in the mapping signature $f: A \to B$. - Range: The set of all actual outputs generated by evaluating the function across its entire domain: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = f(A) = \{f(x) : x \in A\} = \{y \in B : \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y\}$$ By set-theoretic construction, $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Codom}(f)$. The inclusion may be strict or improper. SECTION 2.1: RIGOROUS TAXONOMY OF MAPPINGS
The classification of mappings dictates their invertibility, solvability, and algebraic preservation properties.

1. Injective (One-to-One) Functions:
A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be Injective (or One-to-One) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the codomain. $$\text{Direct Formulation: } \forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$ $$\text{Contrapositive Analytical Criterion: } \forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$ In standard board examinations, the contrapositive analytical criterion is the mandated algebraic instrument for proofs. A function that fails to be injective is termed Many-to-One: $$\exists x_1, x_2 \in A \text{ such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$
Calculus-Based Monotonicity Criterion for Injectivity:
Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous function defined on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is differentiable on the interior of $I$. - If the derivative $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \operatorname{int}(I)$ (or $f'(x) \ge 0$ with $f'(x) = 0$ only at isolated points not forming an interval), then $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$, which implies $f$ is strictly injective. - If $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in \operatorname{int}(I)$ (or $f'(x) \le 0$ with $f'(x) = 0$ only at isolated points), then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $I$, which implies $f$ is strictly injective. - If $f'(x)$ changes sign within $I$, there exist local extrema, and by Rolle's Theorem, there exist distinct values with equal functional evaluations, rendering $f$ many-to-one.

The Geometric Horizontal Line Test:
A real-valued function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = c$ (where $c \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the graphical Cartesian curve $y = f(x)$ at at most one point. If any horizontal line intersects the graph at two or more points, the function is many-to-one.

2. Surjective (Onto) Functions:
A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be Surjective (or Onto) if every element of the codomain set $B$ has at least one pre-image in the domain set $A$. $$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$ In terms of set-theoretic projections, $f$ is surjective if and only if its Range is identical to its Codomain: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = B$$ If the range is a strict proper subset of the codomain ($\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subset B$), then $f$ is termed an Into function: $$\exists y \in B \text{ such that } \forall x \in A, \quad f(x) \neq y$$
CBSE Analytical Method for Proving Surjectivity:
1. Set up the arbitrary element equation: Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element. 2. Equate $y = f(x)$ and solve algebraically for $x$ strictly in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$. 3. Prove that for every $y \in B$, the resolved expression $x = g(y)$ exists, is mathematically real/defined, and strictly satisfies the membership condition $x \in A$. 4. Substitute the derived expression back into $f$: evaluate $f(g(y))$ and prove identically that $f(g(y)) = y$.

3. Bijective (One-to-One and Onto) Functions:
A function $f: A \to B$ is called a Bijection (or Bijective) if and only if it is simultaneously Injective and Surjective. Bijective functions establish a perfect, invertible one-to-one correspondence between the domain set $A$ and the codomain set $B$.

Cardinality Constraints for Finite Sets:
Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$. - If there exists an injection $f: A \to B$, then $m \le n$. (Pigeonhole Principle: If $m > n$, at least two domain elements must share an image). - If there exists a surjection $f: A \to B$, then $m \ge n$. (If $m < n$, the maximum possible cardinality of the range is $m$, leaving $n - m$ elements in $B$ without pre-images). - If there exists a bijection $f: A \to B$, then $m = n$. SECTION 2.2: COMBINATORIAL MAPPING THEORY
Calculating the number of mappings between finite sets under various constraints is a cornerstone of combinatorial mathematics and advanced Class 12 probability/mapping problems. Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

1. Total Number of Functions:
Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ has $n$ independent choices in $B$: $$N_{\text{functions}} = \underbrace{n \times n \times \cdots \times n}_{m \text{ times}} = n^m$$
2. Total Number of Injective Functions:
The first element in $A$ can be mapped to any of the $n$ elements in $B$; the second element can be mapped to any of the remaining $(n - 1)$ elements; the $k$-th element has $(n - k + 1)$ choices: $$N_{\text{injective}} = \begin{cases} ^n P_m = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}, & \text{if } n \ge m \\ 0, & \text{if } n < m \end{cases}$$
3. Total Number of Bijective Functions:
A bijection requires $m = n$. The total number of permutations of $n$ elements is: $$N_{\text{bijective}} = \begin{cases} n!, & \text{if } m = n \\ 0, & \text{if } m \neq n \end{cases}$$
4. Total Number of Surjective Functions (Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion):
If $m < n$, surjections cannot exist ($N_{\text{surjective}} = 0$). If $m \ge n$, the number of surjective functions from an $m$-element set to an $n$-element set is obtained using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE): Let $U$ be the set of all $n^m$ functions. For each $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, let $P_i$ denote the property that the $i$-th element of $B$ is excluded from the range. The number of onto functions is the number of functions satisfying none of the exclusionary properties: $$N_{\text{surjective}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$ Expanding explicitly: $$N_{\text{surjective}} = n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n - 1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n - 2)^m - \binom{n}{3}(n - 3)^m + \cdots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}(1)^m$$ This value is identically equal to $n! S(m, n)$, where $S(m, n)$ denotes the Stirling numbers of the second kind, representing the number of ways to partition a set of $m$ labeled objects into $n$ non-empty unlabeled subsets. SECTION 3.0: COMPOSITION OF FUNCTIONS
Functional composition represents the chaining of mathematical operations, whereby the output of one functional mapping serves as the input argument for a subsequent mapping.

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions. The Composite Function, denoted by $g \circ f$ (read as “$g$ circle $f$” or “$g$ composed with $f$”), is defined as a mapping from $A$ to $C$: $$g \circ f: A \to C, \quad (g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$
The Rigorous Domain-Range Compatibility Condition:
For the composite function $g \circ f$ to exist, the evaluation $g(f(x))$ must be mathematically valid for all $x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)$. This requires that every output of $f$ must be an acceptable input for $g$. Formally: $$g \circ f \text{ is well-defined} \iff \operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$$ If $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \not\subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$, then $g \circ f$ cannot be evaluated over the entirety of $A$. To construct a valid composite mapping, the domain of $f$ must be restricted to an effective domain $D_{g \circ f}$: $$D_{g \circ f} = \{x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f) : f(x) \in \operatorname{Dom}(g)\}$$
Algebraic Theorems on Composition:
Theorem 3.1 (Non-Commutativity): In general, functional composition is non-commutative: $$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$ Even when $A = B = C$ (so that both $g \circ f$ and $f \circ g$ have matching domain and codomain $A \to A$), the functional assignments will typically diverge.
Example: Let $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$. $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$$ $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)) = f(x^2) = 2(x^2) + 1 = 2x^2 + 1$$ Clearly, $4x^2 + 4x + 1 \neq 2x^2 + 1$ for all $x \neq 0$.

Theorem 3.2 (Associativity): Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$ be functions. Then composition is associative: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$ Proof: Both composite functions map $A \to D$. Let $x \in A$. Evaluate the left-hand composite: $$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Evaluate the right-hand composite: $$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Since the evaluations match identically for all $x \in A$, the mappings are equal: $h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3.3 (Preservation of Injectivity and Surjectivity):
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two mappings.
  1. If both $f$ and $g$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
  2. If both $f$ and $g$ are surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
  3. If both $f$ and $g$ are bijective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is bijective.
Proof of (1): Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. $$\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ Since $g$ is injective, $g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$. Applying this where $u = f(x_1)$ and $v = f(x_2)$: $$\implies f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$ Since $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, confirming $g \circ f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

Proof of (2): Let $z \in C$ be an arbitrary element. Since $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists an element $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Since $f: A \to B$ is surjective, for this element $y \in B$, there exists an element $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Substituting $y$ into $g$: $$g(f(x)) = g(y) = z \implies (g \circ f)(x) = z$$ Thus, for every $z \in C$, there exists $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

  1. If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ MUST be injective. (Note: $g$ need not be injective).

SECTION 4.0: INVERTIBILITY & INVERSE OF A FUNCTION
Inversion is the process of reversing the direction of a mapping, effectively transforming functional outputs back into their originating inputs.

Formal Definition of Invertibility:
A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be Invertible if there exists another function $g: B \to A$ such that the composition of the two functions yields the respective identity maps on their respective sets: $$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$ Where $I_A: A \to A$ is defined by $I_A(x) = x$ for all $x \in A$, and $I_B: B \to B$ is defined by $I_B(y) = y$ for all $y \in B$. The function $g$ is uniquely determined and is termed the Inverse of $f$, denoted $g = f^{-1}$.

The Fundamental Theorem of Invertibility:
Theorem 4.1: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijective function (one-to-one and onto).

Proof ($\implies$ Invertible implies Bijective):
Assume $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$. - Injectivity of $f$: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$ to both sides: $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f = I_A$, this becomes $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Thus, $f$ is injective. - Surjectivity of $f$: Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element. Using $f \circ g = I_B$, we evaluate: $(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$. Notice that $g(y) \in A$. If we set $x = g(y) \in A$, we have found an $x$ such that $f(x) = y$. Thus, every $y \in B$ has a pre-image under $f$, so $f$ is surjective. Having proven both injectivity and surjectivity, $f$ is bijective.

Proof ($\impliedby$ Bijective implies Invertible):
Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective. Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Since $f$ is injective, this element $x$ is uniquely determined (if two such elements $x_1, x_2$ existed, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y \implies x_1 = x_2$). We can therefore define a rule $g: B \to A$ by assigning to each $y \in B$ this unique element $x \in A$ satisfying $f(x) = y$. That is, $g(y) = x \iff f(x) = y$. Now evaluate the compositions: - For any $x \in A$: let $y = f(x)$. Then by definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. Thus, $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x)$. Hence, $g \circ f = I_A$. - For any $y \in B$: let $x = g(y)$. Then by definition of $g$, $f(x) = y$. Thus, $(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y)$. Hence, $f \circ g = I_B$. Consequently, $f$ is invertible and $g = f^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

Uniqueness of the Inverse:
Theorem 4.2: If a function possesses an inverse, that inverse is strictly unique.
Proof: Let $f: A \to B$ be invertible, and suppose $g_1: B \to A$ and $g_2: B \to A$ are both inverses of $f$. Then by definition: $$g_1 \circ f = I_A, \quad f \circ g_1 = I_B \quad \text{and} \quad g_2 \circ f = I_A, \quad f \circ g_2 = I_B$$ Now consider the composition $g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2)$: $$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = g_1 \circ I_B = g_1$$ Using the associativity of functional composition: $$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_A \circ g_2 = g_2$$ Equating both results gives $g_1 = g_2$. The inverse is therefore unique. $\blacksquare$

The Reversal Law for Inverses:
Theorem 4.3: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible mappings. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also invertible, and its inverse is given by: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ Proof: We must demonstrate that composing $(g \circ f)$ with $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$ in both orders yields the corresponding identity mappings. First, evaluate: $$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f))$$ Using associativity: $$= f^{-1} \circ ((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ Next, evaluate: $$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}))$$ Using associativity: $$= g \circ ((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$ Since both composition orders yield the respective identity mappings, $(g \circ f)$ is invertible and $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

Case Study 1: The Linear Fractional (Möbius) Transformation
Let $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. Consider the mapping $f: A \to B$ defined by: $$f(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$ Step 1: Test for Injectivity.
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\frac{x_1 - 2}{x_1 - 3} = \frac{x_2 - 2}{x_2 - 3}$$ Cross-multiplying across the defined domain: $$(x_1 - 2)(x_2 - 3) = (x_2 - 2)(x_1 - 3)$$ $$x_1 x_2 - 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 3x_2 - 2x_1 + 6$$ Subtracting the common terms $(x_1 x_2 + 6)$ from both sides: $$-3x_1 - 2x_2 = -3x_2 - 2x_1$$ $$-3x_1 + 2x_1 = -3x_2 + 2x_2 \implies -x_1 = -x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Since $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, the function is strictly injective.

Step 2: Test for Surjectivity.
Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ be an arbitrary element. We set up $y = f(x)$ and solve for $x$: $$y = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3} \implies y(x - 3) = x - 2 \implies yx - 3y = x - 2$$ $$yx - x = 3y - 2 \implies x(y - 1) = 3y - 2 \implies x = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$ Since $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, the denominator $(y - 1) \neq 0$, which ensures $x \in \mathbb{R}$. We must also verify whether $x$ can equal the excluded domain point $3$: Assume $\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} = 3 \implies 3y - 2 = 3(y - 1) \implies 3y - 2 = 3y - 3 \implies -2 = -3$, a contradiction. Thus, $x \neq 3$, meaning $x \in A$. Evaluating $f(x)$: $$f\left(\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}\right) = \frac{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 2}{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 3} = \frac{\frac{3y - 2 - 2(y - 1)}{y - 1}}{\frac{3y - 2 - 3(y - 1)}{y - 1}} = \frac{3y - 2 - 2y + 2}{3y - 2 - 3y + 3} = \frac{y}{1} = y$$ Hence, for every $y \in B$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. The function is strictly surjective.

Conclusion: Because $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore invertible. Its inverse function is: $$f^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}, \quad f^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x - 2}{x - 1}$$

A binary operation abstractly models the process of combining two elements of a single set to produce another element within the same set.

Formal Definition:
A Binary Operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function from the Cartesian product $A \times A$ into $A$: $$* : A \times A \to A$$ We denote the image of an ordered pair $(a, b) \in A \times A$ under $*$ using infix notation: $*(a, b) = a * b$.
The Closure Property: By defining $*$ as a function whose codomain is $A$, the operation automatically satisfies the closure property: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b \in A$$ If there exist any $a, b \in A$ such that $a * b \notin A$, then $*$ is not a valid binary operation on $A$. For example, normal subtraction is a binary operation on $\mathbb{Z}$, but it is not a binary operation on $\mathbb{N}$ because $3 - 5 = -2 \notin \mathbb{N}$.

Core Axioms of Binary Operations:
1. Commutativity:
A binary operation $*$ on $A$ is called commutative if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$
2. Associativity:
A binary operation $*$ on $A$ is called associative if: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$ Associativity guarantees that parentheses can be omitted in iterated evaluations without ambiguity.

3. Existence of an Identity Element:
An element $e \in A$ is said to be an identity element with respect to the binary operation $*$ on $A$ if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a$$ Theorem 6.1 (Uniqueness of Identity): If an identity element exists for a binary operation, it is unique.
Proof: Suppose $e_1, e_2 \in A$ are both identity elements for $*$ on $A$. Because $e_1$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_2$. Because $e_2$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. Therefore, $e_1 = e_2$. The identity element is unique. $\blacksquare$

4. Existence of an Inverse Element:
Let $*$ be a binary operation on $A$ that possesses an identity element $e \in A$. An element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible with respect to $*$ if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that: $$a * b = b * a = e$$ The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$, denoted $a^{-1}$.
Theorem 6.2 (Uniqueness of the Inverse under Associativity): Let $*$ be an associative binary operation on $A$ with identity $e$. If an element $a \in A$ is invertible, its inverse is unique.
Proof: Suppose $b_1, b_2 \in A$ are both inverses of $a$. Then: $$a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e \quad \text{and} \quad a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$$ Consider the composite expression $b_1 * (a * b_2)$: $$b_1 * (a * b_2) = b_1 * e = b_1$$ Using associativity: $$b_1 * (a * b_2) = (b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$ Therefore, $b_1 = b_2$. The inverse is unique. $\blacksquare$

5. Number of Binary Operations on Finite Sets:
Let $|A| = n$. A binary operation on $A$ is a mapping from $A \times A \to A$. Since $|A \times A| = n^2$, the total number of binary operations is: $$N_{\text{bin\_ops}} = n^{n^2}$$ For a binary operation to be commutative, the outcome $a_i * a_j$ must match $a_j * a_i$. The number of free entries on and above the main diagonal is $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. Drawing from extensive grading rubrics and recurring examiner observations across CBSE Class 12 evaluations, students commonly lose marks due to the following procedural oversights:

Pitfall 1: Insufficient Reflexivity Verification
Stating that a relation is reflexive simply because “$(1, 1) \in R$” on a set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ will result in zero marks for that criterion. Reflexivity is a universally quantified statement: $\forall a \in A, (a, a) \in R$. The student must demonstrate that $(1, 1), (2, 2),$ and $(3, 3)$ all belong to $R$. If testing an infinite algebraic relation (e.g., on $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{Z}$), the general condition $(x, x) \in R$ must be shown analytically using an arbitrary variable $x$.

Pitfall 2: Misinterpreting Vacuous Transitivity
A common error is concluding that a relation is not transitive because there are no chaining pairs to test. Consider $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ on $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Many candidates assert $R$ is not transitive because $(2, 3)$ and $(1, 3)$ are missing. This is conceptually false. Transitivity requires that whenever $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, then $(a, c)$ must be in $R$. If there are no pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ with a matching intermediate element, the premise is false, and the condition is vacuously satisfied. Hence, $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ is strictly transitive.

Pitfall 3: Failing to Address Codomain Constraints in Surjectivity
When testing surjectivity, students often solve $x$ in terms of $y$ algebraically, but fail to verify whether $x$ actually lies within the specified Domain for every possible $y$ in the Codomain. For example, let $f: \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^+$ be defined by $f(x) = x^2 - 2$. Solving for $x$ yields $x = \sqrt{y + 2}$. If $y = 1 \in \text{Codomain}$, $x = \sqrt{3} \in \text{Domain}$. But if the mapping were defined as $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, taking $y = -5 \in \text{Codomain}$ yields $x = \sqrt{-3} \notin \mathbb{R}$. An algebraic expression alone does not prove surjectivity; the candidate must demonstrate that $x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)$ for all $y \in \operatorname{Codom}(f)$.

Pitfall 4: Neglecting Both Composition Orders for Inverses
When proving that a given function $g$ is the inverse of $f$, showing only that $(g \circ f)(x) = x$ (or only that $(f \circ g)(y) = y$) is incomplete. CBSE grading rubrics require demonstrating both directions: $$(g \circ f)(x) = I_A(x) = x \quad \text{AND} \quad (f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) = y$$ Omitting one of the directions typically incurs an immediate deduction.

Derivation 1: Enumeration of Reflexive Relations on a Finite Set

Theorem: Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. The total number of distinct reflexive relations on $A$ is $2^{n(n-1)}$.

Proof:

  1. A relation $R$ on $A$ is defined as an arbitrary subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$: $$R \subseteq A \times A$$ Since $|A| = n$, the cardinality of the product space is: $$|A \times A| = |A| \cdot |A| = n^2$$
  2. We partition $A \times A$ into two disjoint collections of ordered pairs:
    • The diagonal subset $\Delta = \{(a_i, a_i) : a_i \in A, \, 1 \le i \le n\}$, where $|\Delta| = n$.
    • The non-diagonal subset $\Delta^c = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \ne j, \, 1 \le i, j \le n\}$, where $|\Delta^c| = n^2 - n = n(n-1)$.
  3. By definition, a relation $R$ is reflexive if and only if: $$\forall a_i \in A, \quad (a_i, a_i) \in R \implies \Delta \subseteq R$$ Therefore, each of the $n$ diagonal pairs has precisely $1$ choice (it must be included in $R$).
  4. For every non-diagonal element $(a_i, a_j) \in \Delta^c$ ($i \ne j$), there are $2$ independent choices: the pair can either belong to $R$ or not belong to $R$.
  5. By the fundamental multiplication principle of combinatorics: $$N_{\text{ref}} = \prod_{(a_i, a_i) \in \Delta} 1 \times \prod_{(a_i, a_j) \in \Delta^c} 2 = 1^n \cdot 2^{|\Delta^c|} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Enumeration of Symmetric Relations on a Finite Set

Theorem: Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. The total number of distinct symmetric relations on $A$ is $2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$.

Proof:

  1. Any relation $R$ on $A$ can be represented uniquely by an $n \times n$ adjacency matrix $M = [m_{ij}]$, where: $$m_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & (a_i, a_j) \in R \\ 0, & (a_i, a_j) \notin R \end{cases}$$
  2. $R$ is symmetric if and only if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$, which is equivalent to requiring the matrix $M$ to be symmetric: $$M = M^T \iff m_{ij} = m_{ji}, \quad \forall 1 \le i, j \le n$$
  3. A symmetric $n \times n$ matrix is completely determined by its entries on and above the main diagonal:
    • The number of main diagonal entries $(i = j)$ is $n$.
    • The number of strictly upper triangular entries $(i < j)$ is $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
  4. The total number of independent entries that completely specify the symmetric matrix is: $$K = n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$
  5. Each of these $K$ independent positions can take a binary value ($0$ or $1$) independently. Once chosen, the symmetric counterparts $m_{ji}$ for $j > i$ are constrained uniquely to $m_{ji} = m_{ij}$.
  6. Therefore, the total number of symmetric relations is: $$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^K = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: Formula for Surjective (Onto) Functions via Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Theorem: Let $A$ and $B$ be sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ such that $m \ge n$. The number of surjective functions $f: A \to B$ is given by: $$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Proof:

  1. Let $S$ denote the set of all possible functions from $A$ to $B$. The cardinality of $S$ is: $$|S| = n^m$$
  2. Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n\}$. A function $f: A \to B$ fails to be surjective if there exists at least one element $b_i \in B$ that has no pre-image in $A$. Define property $P_i$ as the condition that $b_i$ is not in the range of $f$: $$A_i = \{f \in S : b_i \notin \operatorname{img}(f)\}$$
  3. The set of surjective functions is the complement of the union of these non-surjective mappings: $$N_{\text{surj}} = \left| S \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right| = |S| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right|$$
  4. By the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE): $$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right| = \sum_{1 \le i \le n} |A_i| - \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} |A_i \cap A_j| + \sum_{1 \le i < j < k \le n} |A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k| - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} |A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n|$$
  5. For any intersection of $k$ distinct sets $A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}$, the codomain elements $\{b_{i_1}, \dots, b_{i_k}\}$ cannot be assigned. The function can only map into the remaining $(n - k)$ elements of $B$: $$|A_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = (n - k)^m$$
  6. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways to select $k$ omitted elements from $B$. Thus, the $k$-th symmetric sum evaluates to: $$S_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n} |A_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = \binom{n}{k}(n - k)^m$$
  7. Substituting $S_k$ into the inclusion-exclusion expression: $$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right| = \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$
  8. Therefore, the number of surjective functions is: $$N_{\text{surj}} = n^m - \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = \binom{n}{0}(n-0)^m + \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$ $$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Reversal Law for the Inverse of a Composite Function

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be bijective functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and its inverse is: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Proof:

  1. Bijectivity of $g \circ f$:
    • Injectivity: Suppose $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$ for $x_1, x_2 \in A$. $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ Since $g$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Since $f$ is injective, $x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is injective.
    • Surjectivity: Let $z \in C$. Since $g$ is surjective, $\exists y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Since $f$ is surjective, $\exists x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = z$$ Hence, $g \circ f$ is surjective.
    Since $g \circ f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore $(g \circ f)^{-1}$ exists.
  2. Uniqueness Criterion: A map $h: C \to A$ is the inverse of $g \circ f$ if and only if: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad (g \circ f) \circ h = I_C$$ Let us test the candidate map $h = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}: C \to A$.
  3. Left Composition: $$\begin{aligned} (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)) && \text{(Associativity)} \\ &= f^{-1} \circ ((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f) && \text{(Associativity)} \\ &= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) && (\text{Since } g^{-1} \circ g = I_B) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ f && (\text{Since } I_B \circ f = f) \\ &= I_A && (\text{Since } f^{-1} \circ f = I_A) \end{aligned}$$
  4. Right Composition: $$\begin{aligned} (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ (f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})) && \text{(Associativity)} \\ &= g \circ ((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}) && \text{(Associativity)} \\ &= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) && (\text{Since } f \circ f^{-1} = I_B) \\ &= g \circ g^{-1} && (\text{Since } I_B \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}) \\ &= I_C && (\text{Since } g \circ g^{-1} = I_C) \end{aligned}$$
  5. Since both composition identities hold identically, by uniqueness of the inverse mapping: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Partitioning)

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. The equivalence classes of $R$ form a partition of $A$. Specifically:

  1. $\forall a \in A, \, [a] \ne \emptyset$ and $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$.
  2. For any $a, b \in A$, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.

Proof:

  1. Proof that $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$ and $[a] \ne \emptyset$:
    Let $a \in A$. Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R$. By definition of the equivalence class: $$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$ Therefore, $a \in [a]$, which proves $[a] \ne \emptyset$.
    Since $a \in [a]$ for every $a \in A$, it follows trivially that: $$A \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$$ Furthermore, since $[a] \subseteq A$ for each $a \in A$, $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A$. Consequently: $$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$
  2. Lemma: If $(a, b) \in R$, then $[a] = [b]$.
    Proof of Lemma: Let $x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R$. Since $(x, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$, transitivity of $R$ implies: $$(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$ Thus, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
    Conversely, by symmetry, $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$. Let $y \in [b] \implies (y, b) \in R$. By transitivity with $(b, a) \in R$, we obtain $(y, a) \in R \implies y \in [a]$. Thus, $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Combining both set inclusions yields: $$[a] = [b]$$
  3. Proof of Pairwise Disjointness:
    Let $a, b \in A$. There are two possibilities: either $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ or $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$.
    Assume $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $c \in A$ such that: $$c \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad c \in [b]$$ By definition of equivalence classes: $$(c, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (c, b) \in R$$ Applying symmetry to the first relation yields: $$(a, c) \in R$$ Now applying transitivity to $(a, c) \in R$ and $(c, b) \in R$: $$(a, b) \in R$$ By our Lemma established in step 2, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$.
  4. Therefore, any two equivalence classes are either strictly identical ($[a] = [b]$) or completely disjoint ($[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$). Hence, the set of all equivalence classes $A/R$ forms a true partition of $A$.

$\blacksquare$
