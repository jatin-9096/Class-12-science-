1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Set Theory and the Cartesian Architecture

The mathematical universe of Class 12 Advanced Calculus and Abstract Algebra is constructed upon the bedrock of axiomatic set theory. To rigorously analyze relations and functions, one must first formalize the arena in which these objects reside: the Cartesian product of sets.

1.1 The Cartesian Product of Two Sets

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets. The Cartesian Product, denoted by $A \times B$ (read as “$A$ cross $B$”), is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate $a$ belongs to $A$ and the second coordinate $b$ belongs to $B$. Formally, using set-builder notation:

$$\mathbf{A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}}$$

An ordered pair $(a, b)$ is a fundamental primitive wherein the order of components is mathematically immutable. By the Axiom of Extensionality for ordered pairs:

$$(a_1, b_1) = (a_2, b_2) \iff (a_1 = a_2 \;\land\; b_1 = b_2)$$

Consequently, if $A \neq B$, then $A \times B \neq B \times A$. The Cartesian product is commutative if and only if $A = B$, or at least one of the constituent sets is the empty set $\emptyset$.

1.2 Cardinality Metrics of Cartesian Spaces

Let $|A| = n(A) = m$ and $|B| = n(B) = n$, where $m, n \in \mathbb{N}_0$. The cardinality of the Cartesian product is given by the multiplicative principle:

$$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n$$

The power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ represents the collection of all subsets of $A \times B$. By elementary combinatorics, the cardinality of the power set is:

$$|\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{|A \times B|} = 2^{mn}$$

1.3 Formal Definition of a Binary Relation

A binary relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. That is:

$$R \subseteq A \times B \iff R \in \mathcal{P}(A \times B)$$

If $(a, b) \in R$, we write $a \, R \, b$, which is read as “$a$ is related to $b$ under relation $R$”. Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, we write $a \!\not R\, b$. When the relation is defined from a set $A$ to itself ($R \subseteq A \times A$), $R$ is termed a binary relation on $A$.

Total Number of Relations: Since every subset of $A \times A$ defines a unique binary relation on $A$, the total number of distinct relations that can be defined on a set $A$ containing $|A| = n$ elements is precisely equal to the cardinality of $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$:

$$N_{\text{total}} = 2^{n^2}$$

1.4 Domain, Range, and Codomain

For any relation $R \subseteq A \times B$:

  • Domain of $R$ ($\operatorname{Dom}(R)$): The set of all first components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$: $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a \in A : \exists\, b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq A$$
  • Range of $R$ ($\operatorname{Ran}(R)$): The set of all second components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$: $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{b \in B : \exists\, a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq B$$
  • Codomain of $R$: The entire ambient target set $B$, irrespective of whether every element in $B$ participates in the relation. Note that $\operatorname{Ran}(R) \subseteq \text{Codomain}(R)$.

2. Axiomatic Taxonomy of Binary Relations on a Set

Binary relations on an arbitrary non-empty set $A$ are categorized according to the satisfaction of specific mathematical axioms governing reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity.

2.1 Trivial and Extremal Relations

  • The Empty (Void) Relation ($\emptyset$): Since the empty set is a subset of every set, $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation containing no ordered pairs is called the empty relation: $$\emptyset = \{\} \subset A \times A$$
  • The Universal Relation ($U$): The maximal subset of $A \times A$ is the Cartesian product itself: $$U = A \times A$$ Every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$. Both the empty and universal relations are collectively referred to as trivial relations.
  • The Identity Relation ($I_A$): Defined strictly as the relation pairing every element of $A$ exclusively with itself: $$I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$$ Critical Analytical Distinction: While $I_A$ contains only pairs of the form $(a, a)$, a reflexive relation must contain at least all pairs $(a, a)$, but may also contain additional pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$. Thus, $I_A \subseteq R_{\text{reflexive}}$, meaning the identity relation is a proper or improper subset of every reflexive relation on $A$.

2.2 The Fundamental Triad of Relation Properties

Let $R$ be a binary relation on a non-empty set $A$.

I. Reflexivity

The relation $R$ is reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself:

$$\mathbf{\forall a \in A \implies (a, a) \in R}$$

Examiner Trap: To disprove reflexivity, it is sufficient to exhibit a single counterexample element $x \in A$ such that $(x, x) \notin R$.

II. Symmetry

The relation $R$ is symmetric if the presence of any ordered pair implies the presence of its inverted counterpart:

$$\mathbf{\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R}$$

Mathematical Caveat: Symmetry is an implication ($P \implies Q$). If the premise $(a, b) \in R$ is false for distinct elements, the condition holds vacuously. Thus, the empty relation $\emptyset$ is vacuously symmetric.

III. Transitivity

The relation $R$ is transitive if the chaining of two compatible relations implies the direct relation:

$$\mathbf{\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad ((a, b) \in R \;\land\; (b, c) \in R) \implies (a, c) \in R}$$

The Vacuous Truth Condition: If there exist no pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ with identical intermediate elements $b$ where $a \neq c$, transitivity cannot be broken. It requires an active violation—the existence of $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ while $(a, c) \notin R$—to render a relation non-transitive.

IV. Anti-Symmetry (Comparative Structural Property)

A relation $R$ on $A$ is anti-symmetric if:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad ((a, b) \in R \;\land\; (b, a) \in R) \implies a = b$$

Equivalently, if $a \neq b$, then $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ cannot both belong to $R$. Standard partial order relations (such as $\le$ on $\mathbb{R}$, or $\subseteq$ on $\mathcal{P}(X)$) are reflexive, anti-symmetric, and transitive.


3. Equivalence Relations, Equivalence Classes, and Partitioning

3.1 Definition of an Equivalence Relation

A binary relation $R$ defined on a set $A$ is an equivalence relation if and only if it satisfies all three primary axiomatic conditions simultaneously:

  1. $R$ is reflexive: $\forall a \in A, (a, a) \in R$.
  2. $R$ is symmetric: $\forall a, b \in A, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$.
  3. $R$ is transitive: $\forall a, b, c \in A, ((a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R) \implies (a, c) \in R$.

3.2 Canonical Exemplar: Congruence Modulo $n$

Let $\mathbb{Z}$ denote the set of integers, and let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ be a fixed positive integer. We define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by:

$$a \equiv b \pmod n \iff n \mid (a - b) \iff a - b = k n \quad \text{for some } k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

Rigorous Proof of Equivalence:

  • Reflexivity: For any $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot n$. Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mid (a - a)$. Hence, $(a, a) \in R, \forall a \in \mathbb{Z}$. $R$ is reflexive.
  • Symmetry: Let $(a, b) \in R$. Then $a - b = k_1 n$ for some $k_1 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiplying both sides by $-1$ yields $b - a = (-k_1)n$. Since $k_1 \in \mathbb{Z} \implies -k_1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $n \mid (b - a)$, meaning $(b, a) \in R$. $R$ is symmetric.
  • Transitivity: Let $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. Then there exist integers $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that: $$a - b = k_1 n \quad \text{and} \quad b - c = k_2 n$$ Summing these two algebraic equations: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 n + k_2 n \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)n$$ Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under addition, $k_3 = k_1 + k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Thus, $n \mid (a - c)$, which confirms $(a, c) \in R$. $R$ is transitive.

Having verified all three properties, Congruence Modulo $n$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$.

3.3 Equivalence Classes: Definitions and Theorems

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$, and let $a \in A$. The equivalence class determined by the element $a$, denoted by $[a]$ or $\operatorname{cl}(a)$, is the set of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:

$$\mathbf{[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}}$$

Due to the symmetry of $R$, $[a] = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

Fundamental Theorems on Equivalence Classes

Theorem 1: For any $a \in A$, the equivalence class $[a]$ is non-empty.
Proof: Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in A$. Therefore, $a \in [a]$. Thus, no equivalence class is empty.

Theorem 2: Let $a, b \in A$. Then $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
Proof ($\implies$): Assume $[a] = [b]$. Since $a \in [a]$ (by reflexivity), and $[a] = [b]$, we must have $a \in [b]$. By definition of $[b]$, this means $(a, b) \in R$.
Proof ($\impliedby$): Assume $(a, b) \in R$. We must prove $[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Let $x \in [a]$. Then $(x, a) \in R$. We are given $(a, b) \in R$. By transitivity, $(x, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (x, b) \in R$. Hence, $x \in [b]$, which establishes $[a] \subseteq [b]$. Now, since $(a, b) \in R$, symmetry dictates $(b, a) \in R$. By symmetric reasoning, if $y \in [b]$, then $(y, b) \in R$, which with $(b, a) \in R$ yields $(y, a) \in R$ by transitivity, so $y \in [a]$. Thus $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Combined, $[a] = [b]$.

Theorem 3 (Disjointness or Identity): Two equivalence classes are either identical or completely disjoint. That is, for all $a, b \in A$:

$$[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset \quad \lor \quad [a] = [b]$$

Proof: Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists an element $x \in [a] \cap [b]$. This implies $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$. Therefore, $(x, a) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$. By symmetry, $(x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$. Now, by transitivity, $(a, x) \in R \land (x, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$. By Theorem 2, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$. Hence, if the intersection contains even one element, the classes coincide entirely.

3.4 Partitions and the Fundamental Decomposition Theorem

A partition $\mathcal{P}$ of a non-empty set $A$ is a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ whose union recovers $A$:

  1. $A_i \neq \emptyset$ for all $i \in I$.
  2. $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ whenever $i \neq j$ (Mutually Disjoint).
  3. $\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = A$ (Exhaustive Union).

The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations: An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ induces a unique partition of $A$ into equivalence classes. Conversely, any partition of $A$ uniquely defines an equivalence relation on $A$ whose equivalence classes are precisely the cells of the partition.

The set of all equivalence classes of $A$ modulo $R$ is termed the quotient set, denoted:

$$A/R = \{[a] : a \in A\}$$

4. Combinatorial Profiling of Binary Relations

Let $A$ be a finite set with cardinality $|A| = n$. The total number of pairs in the Cartesian matrix $A \times A$ is $n^2$. This $n \times n$ matrix contains $n$ diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_i)$ and $n^2 - n = n(n-1)$ non-diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \neq j$.

4.1 Enumeration Formulae

  • Total Number of Relations: $$N_{\text{total}} = 2^{n^2}$$
  • Number of Reflexive Relations: To be reflexive, every diagonal element $(a_i, a_i)$ must be present. Thus, there is only $1$ choice for each of the $n$ diagonal pairs. The remaining $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal elements can independently be chosen or excluded ($2$ choices each): $$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \cdot 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n^2 - n}$$
  • Number of Symmetric Relations: Off-diagonal elements occur in pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$. There are $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ such unordered pairs. For each pair, there are 2 choices (both are in $R$, or neither is in $R$). The $n$ diagonal elements can also be chosen or excluded independently ($2^n$ choices). Therefore: $$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$
  • Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations: All $n$ diagonal pairs must be included (1 choice). The $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ off-diagonal pairs each have 2 choices. Thus: $$N_{\text{ref} \land \text{sym}} = 1^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$
  • Number of Equivalence Relations: The total number of distinct equivalence relations on a set of $n$ elements is given by the Bell Number $B_n$, which satisfies the recurrence relation: $$B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} B_k, \quad \text{with } B_0 = 1$$ Evaluating for small values:
    • $n = 1 \implies B_1 = 1$
    • $n = 2 \implies B_2 = 2$
    • $n = 3 \implies B_3 = 5$
    • $n = 4 \implies B_4 = 15$
    • $n = 5 \implies B_5 = 52$

5. The Morphological Structure of Functions (Mappings)

5.1 Axiomatic Definition of a Function

A function $f$ from a domain set $A$ to a codomain set $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, is a special class of binary relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ satisfying two definitive axioms:

  1. Totality (Universal Domain Coverage): Every element of the domain must possess an assignment: $$\forall x \in A, \; \exists y \in B \quad \text{such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Single-Valuedness): No element of the domain may be mapped to more than one distinct element in the codomain: $$\forall x \in A, \; \forall y_1, y_2 \in B, \quad ((x, y_1) \in f \;\land\; (x, y_2) \in f) \implies y_1 = y_2$$

If $(x, y) \in f$, we denote this functionally as $y = f(x)$, where $y$ is the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is the pre-image of $y$.

5.2 Domain, Codomain, and Range

For a mapping $f: A \to B$:

  • $\operatorname{Domain}(f) = A$.
  • $\operatorname{Codomain}(f) = B$.
  • Range (Image Set) of $f$: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = f(A) = \{f(x) \in B : x \in A\} = \{y \in B : \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y\}$$ By definition, $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Codomain}(f)$.

6. Classification of Functions: Injectivity, Surjectivity, and Bijectivity

6.1 Injective Functions (One-to-One / Injection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is injective (or one-to-one) if distinct domain elements map to distinct codomain elements:

$$\mathbf{\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)}$$

In standard board examinations, the contrapositive equivalent is used as the working algebraic definition:

$$\mathbf{\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2}$$

A function failing injectivity is classified as many-to-one:

$$\exists x_1, x_2 \in A \quad \text{such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \;\land\; f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$
Analytical and Calculus Tests for Injectivity:
  • The Horizontal Line Test: Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. The function $f$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = c$ (where $c \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the Cartesian graph of $y = f(x)$ at at most one point.
  • Monotonicity Criterion: Let $f$ be continuous on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and differentiable on the interior of $I$. If $f'(x) > 0$ strictly for all $x \in I$ (strictly increasing) or $f'(x) < 0$ strictly for all $x \in I$ (strictly decreasing), then $f$ is strictly monotonic on $I$, and consequently, $f$ is injective on $I$. If $f'(x)$ changes sign across the domain, $f$ cannot be injective.

6.2 Surjective Functions (Onto / Surjection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is surjective (or onto) if every element of the codomain possesses at least one corresponding pre-image in the domain:

$$\mathbf{\forall y \in B, \; \exists x \in A \quad \text{such that } f(x) = y}$$

Equivalently, surjectivity is achieved if and only if the range coincides completely with the codomain:

$$\mathbf{\operatorname{Ran}(f) = \operatorname{Codomain}(B)}$$

A function failing surjectivity is termed an into function, in which case $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subset B$ (a proper subset).

The Algorithmic Procedure for Proving Surjectivity in Class 12:
  1. Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element of the codomain.
  2. Set up the equation $y = f(x)$.
  3. Solve this equation algebraically to express the independent variable $x$ entirely in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
  4. Evaluate whether $x = g(y)$ exists and belongs to the specified domain $A$ for all possible values of $y \in B$.
  5. Demonstrate consistency by back-substituting: compute $f(g(y))$ and prove that $f(g(y)) = y$.

6.3 Bijective Functions (One-to-One and Onto / Bijection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is bijective if and only if it is simultaneously injective and surjective:

$$\mathbf{f \text{ is bijective}} \iff (f \text{ is one-to-one}) \;\land\; (f \text{ is onto})$$

A bijection establishes an exact, reversible, one-to-one correspondence between the sets $A$ and $B$. If a bijection exists between two finite sets $A$ and $B$, their cardinalities must be identical: $|A| = |B|$.

6.4 Combinatorial Enumeration of Mappings

Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

  • Total Functions: Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ has $n$ independent choices in $B$: $$N_{\text{functions}} = n^m$$
  • Number of Injective Functions (One-to-One):
    • If $m > n$: By the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two domain elements must share an image. Hence, $N_{\text{inj}} = 0$.
    • If $m \le n$: The first element has $n$ choices, the second has $n-1$, continuing down to the $m$-th element: $$N_{\text{inj}} = P(n, m) = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}$$
  • Number of Bijective Functions: Requires $m = n$. When $m = n$, any injection is automatically a surjection: $$N_{\text{bij}} = n!$$ If $m \neq n$, $N_{\text{bij}} = 0$.
  • Number of Surjective Functions (Onto): Using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (or Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind $S(m, n)$): $$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-k} \binom{n}{k} k^m = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$ Specifically:
    • If $m < n$: $N_{\text{surj}} = 0$.
    • If $m = n$: $N_{\text{surj}} = n!$.
    • If $m = n + 1$: $N_{\text{surj}} = \binom{n+1}{2} \cdot n! = \frac{n(n+1)!}{2}$.

7. Composition of Functions: Algebraic & Analytical Mechanics

7.1 Formal Construction and Domain Constraints

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B' \to C$ be two functions. The composite function $g \circ f$ (read as “$g$ circle $f$” or “$g$ of $f$”) is a function mapping from $A$ to $C$, defined by:

$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$$

Fundamental Criterion for Existence: The composite function $g \circ f$ is well-defined if and only if the range of the inner function $f$ is a subset of the domain of the outer function $g$:

$$\mathbf{\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)}$$

If this condition is violated, $g(f(x))$ will encounter inputs for which the outer operation is undefined. In such instances, the domain of $f$ must be restricted to:

$$D_{g \circ f} = \{x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f) : f(x) \in \operatorname{Dom}(g)\}$$

7.2 Key Theorems of Function Composition

Theorem 1 (Non-Commutativity): Function composition is generally non-commutative:

$$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$

Indeed, $f \circ g$ may not even be defined when $g \circ f$ is.

Theorem 2 (Associativity): Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$. Then:

$$\mathbf{h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f}$$

Proof: Both composite functions map $A \to D$. For any arbitrary $x \in A$: $$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ $$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Since the mappings agree on all elements $x \in A$ and share identical domains and codomains, the functions are identical.

Theorem 3 (Preservation of Injectivity and Surjectivity):

  • If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both injective, then $(g \circ f): A \to C$ is injective.
  • If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both surjective, then $(g \circ f): A \to C$ is surjective.
  • Consequently, the composition of two bijections is itself a bijection.

Theorem 4 (Decomposition Properties - Critical Examiner Focus):

  • If $g \circ f$ is injective, then the inner function $f$ must be injective.
    Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$, which means $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f$ is injective, this implies $x_1 = x_2$. Thus, $f$ is injective. (Note: $g$ need not be injective).
  • If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then the outer function $g$ must be surjective.
    Proof: Let $z \in C$. Since $g \circ f$ is surjective, there exists $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z$. Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have found an element $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Thus, $g$ is surjective. (Note: $f$ need not be surjective).

8. Invertibility and Inverse Mappings

8.1 Definition of an Invertible Function

A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be invertible if there exists another function $g: B \to A$ such that:

$$\mathbf{g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B}$$

where $I_A: A \to A$ ($I_A(x) = x, \forall x \in A$) and $I_B: B \to B$ ($I_B(y) = y, \forall y \in B$) are the respective identity functions. The function $g$ is uniquely determined and is called the inverse of $f$, denoted $f^{-1}$.

8.2 The Invertibility Theorem (Fundamental Criterion)

Theorem: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is bijective (both one-to-one and onto).

Rigorous Direct and Converse Proof:

Part 1 ($\implies$): Invertibility implies Bijectivity.
Suppose $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.

  • Injectivity: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Then $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f = I_A$, this simplifies to $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ is injective.
  • Surjectivity: Let $y \in B$. Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we have $(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$. Set $x = g(y)$. Since $g: B \to A$, $x \in A$. Thus, for any $y \in B$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Hence, $f$ is surjective.

Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Part 2 ($\impliedby$): Bijectivity implies Invertibility.
Suppose $f: A \to B$ is bijective. We must construct an explicit function $g: B \to A$ and prove $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.

  • Let $y \in B$. Since $f$ is surjective, there exists at least one element $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is injective, this element $x$ is strictly unique. If there were $x_1, x_2 \in A$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y$, then $x_1 = x_2$.
  • Thus, we can define a rule $g: B \to A$ that maps each $y \in B$ to this uniquely associated element $x \in A$: $g(y) = x \iff f(x) = y$.
  • Now check composition: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x), \quad \forall x \in A \implies g \circ f = I_A$$ $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y), \quad \forall y \in B \implies f \circ g = I_B$$

Thus, $g$ satisfies the invertibility criterion. Hence, $f$ is invertible and $g = f^{-1}$.

8.3 Uniqueness and the Reversal Law

Theorem (Uniqueness): The inverse of a bijective function is unique. If $g_1$ and $g_2$ are both inverses of $f: A \to B$, then:

$$g_1 = g_1 \circ I_B = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_A \circ g_2 = g_2$$

Theorem (The Reversal Law): Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and:

$$\mathbf{(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}}$$

Proof: To prove that $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ is the inverse of $g \circ f$, we must verify both identity equations:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ $$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

By uniqueness of the inverse, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

8.4 Geometric Symmetry of Inverses

The graph of the inverse function $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the precise geometric reflection of the graph of $y = f(x)$ across the line of symmetry $y = x$. If a point $(p, q)$ lies on the curve of $f$, then the reflected point $(q, p)$ must lie on the curve of $f^{-1}$.


9. Binary Operations and Elementary Algebraic Structures

9.1 Formal Definition and Closure

A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function mapping the Cartesian product $A \times A$ into $A$:

$$*: A \times A \to A$$

We denote the image of an ordered pair $(a, b) \in A \times A$ under $*$ as $a * b$. The requirement that $*$ maps into $A$ guarantees the Closure Property:

$$\forall a, b \in A \implies a * b \in A$$

The total number of binary operations that can be defined on a finite set $A$ of cardinality $|A| = n$ is equal to the total number of functions from $A \times A$ to $A$:

$$N_{\text{bin\_ops}} = |A|^{|A \times A|} = n^{(n^2)}$$

9.2 Structural Properties of Binary Operations

  • Commutativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is commutative if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$ The number of commutative binary operations on a set of size $n$ is: $$N_{\text{comm}} = n^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$
  • Associativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is associative if: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$
  • Distributivity: Let $*$ and $\circ$ be two binary operations on $A$. Then $*$ distributes over $\circ$ if: $$a * (b \circ c) = (a * b) \circ (a * c) \quad \text{(Left Distributive)}$$ $$(b \circ c) * a = (b * a) \circ (c * a) \quad \text{(Right Distributive)}$$

9.3 Identity and Invertible Elements

I. Identity Element

An element $e \in A$ is an identity element for the binary operation $*$ if:

$$\mathbf{\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a}$$

Theorem (Uniqueness of Identity): If an identity element exists for an operation $*$, it is strictly unique.
Proof: Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identities for $*$ on $A$. Since $e_1$ is an identity: $e_2 * e_1 = e_2$. Since $e_2$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. If $*$ is well-defined, and setting $a = e_1$ in the second condition and $a = e_2$ in the first, then $e_1 * e_2 = e_2$ and $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. Therefore:

$$e_1 = e_1 * e_2 = e_2 \implies e_1 = e_2$$
II. Inverse of an Element

Let $*$ be a binary operation on $A$ admitting an identity element $e$. An element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that:

$$\mathbf{a * b = b * a = e}$$

The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$, denoted $a^{-1}$.

Theorem (Uniqueness of Inverse): In an associative algebraic system $(A, *)$ possessing an identity $e$, if an element $a \in A$ has an inverse, it is strictly unique.
Proof: Let $b_1$ and $b_2$ be two inverses of $a$. Then:

$$a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e \quad \text{and} \quad a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$$

Evaluate the expression $b_1 * (a * b_2)$:

$$b_1 * (a * b_2) = b_1 * e = b_1$$

By associativity, this can be regrouped as:

$$(b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$

Equating both results gives $b_1 = b_2$.

9.4 Cayley Tables for Finite Sets

For a small finite set $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$, a binary operation can be fully characterized by an $n \times n$ matrix called a Cayley Table (or composition table), where the entry at row $i$ and column $j$ is $a_i * a_j$.

  • Symmetry Test: The operation $*$ is commutative if and only if the Cayley table is completely symmetric across its main leading diagonal.
  • Identity Test: An element $e$ is the identity if the row corresponding to $e$ matches the header row, and the column corresponding to $e$ matches the header column.
  • Invertibility Test: An element $a$ is invertible if the identity element $e$ appears in both the row and the column corresponding to $a$.

10. Master Examiner Blueprints & Analytical Problem Archetypes

Archetype 1: Equivalence Relations on Coordinate Product Spaces

Problem Formulation: Let $A = \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. Define a relation $R$ on $A$ by:

$$(a, b) \, R \, (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$$

Prove that $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$.

Standard Board Marking Scheme Solution:

  1. Reflexivity:
    Let $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ be arbitrary. Since addition is commutative in $\mathbb{N}$: $$a + b = b + a$$ By the definition of $R$, this immediately implies: $$(a, b) \, R \, (a, b), \quad \forall (a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$$ Thus, $R$ is reflexive on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.
  2. Symmetry:
    Let $(a, b), (c, d) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$. $$\implies a + d = b + c$$ By the symmetric property of equality: $$b + c = a + d \implies c + b = d + a$$ Rearranging by commutativity: $$c + b = d + a \implies (c, d) \, R \, (a, b)$$ Hence, $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d) \implies (c, d) \, R \, (a, b)$. $R$ is symmetric.
  3. Transitivity:
    Let $(a, b), (c, d), (e, f) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \, R \, (e, f)$. $$\implies a + d = b + c \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$ $$\implies c + f = d + e \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$ Adding both algebraic equations: $$(a + d) + (c + f) = (b + c) + (d + e)$$ Associating and eliminating intermediate terms $(c + d)$ from both sides using the cancellation law of addition in $\mathbb{N}$: $$a + f + (c + d) = b + e + (c + d) \implies a + f = b + e$$ By definition of $R$, this yields: $$(a, b) \, R \, (e, f)$$ Therefore, $R$ is transitive.

Conclusion: Since $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.

Archetype 2: Bijectivity and Inversion of Rational Functions

Problem Formulation: Let $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. Consider the function $f: A \to B$ defined by:

$$f(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$

Prove that $f$ is bijective, and hence find its inverse formula $f^{-1}(x)$.

Standard Board Marking Scheme Solution:

  1. Step 1: Proof of Injectivity (One-to-One):
    Let $x_1, x_2 \in A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\frac{x_1 - 2}{x_1 - 3} = \frac{x_2 - 2}{x_2 - 3}$$ Cross-multiplying: $$(x_1 - 2)(x_2 - 3) = (x_2 - 2)(x_1 - 3)$$ Expanding both algebraic products: $$x_1 x_2 - 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 3x_2 - 2x_1 + 6$$ Cancelling identical terms $x_1 x_2 + 6$ from both sides: $$-3x_1 - 2x_2 = -3x_2 - 2x_1$$ Collecting like terms: $$-3x_1 + 2x_1 = -3x_2 + 2x_2 \implies -x_1 = -x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Hence, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. The function $f$ is injective.
  2. Step 2: Proof of Surjectivity (Onto):
    Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ be an arbitrary element. We must find an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. $$y = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$ $$y(x - 3) = x - 2 \implies xy - 3y = x - 2$$ $$xy - x = 3y - 2 \implies x(y - 1) = 3y - 2$$ Since $y \in B$, $y \neq 1$, which ensures $y - 1 \neq 0$. Dividing gives: $$x = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$ Now verify whether $x \in A$ (meaning $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $x \neq 3$): Since $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, $x$ is real and well-defined. Check whether $x$ can equal $3$: $$\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} = 3 \implies 3y - 2 = 3(y - 1) \implies 3y - 2 = 3y - 3 \implies -2 = -3 \quad \text{(Contradiction!)}$$ Thus, $x \neq 3$ for any $y \in B$. Hence, $x \in A$. Finally, compute $f(x)$ for completeness: $$f(x) = \frac{\frac{3y-2}{y-1} - 2}{\frac{3y-2}{y-1} - 3} = \frac{\frac{3y - 2 - 2(y - 1)}{y - 1}}{\frac{3y - 2 - 3(y - 1)}{y - 1}} = \frac{3y - 2 - 2y + 2}{3y - 2 - 3y + 3} = \frac{y}{1} = y$$ Thus, every $y \in B$ has a valid pre-image $x \in A$. $f$ is surjective.
  3. Step 3: Bijectivity and Inverse:
    Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective, and therefore invertible. The inverse function $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is obtained by swapping the dummy variable in the expression for $x$: $$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} \implies \mathbf{f^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x - 2}{x - 1}}$$

11. Critical Marking Deductions & Student Misconceptions

  • Universal Quantifier Omission: In reflexive relation proofs, writing “$(a, a) \in R$” without explicitly appending “$\forall a \in A$” leads to an automatic 0.5 to 1 mark deduction. Reflexivity is a universal condition across the entire base set.
  • Confusing Anti-Symmetry with Non-Symmetry: Non-symmetry merely means there is at least one pair $(a, b) \in R$ such that $(b, a) \notin R$. Anti-symmetry requires that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, a) \in R$ can happen only when $a = b$. A relation can be both symmetric and anti-symmetric simultaneously (for example, the identity relation $I_A$).
  • The Transitivity Trap: Transitivity is broken only when $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, but $(a, c) \notin R$. For instance, the relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ on $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ is transitive because there is no matching pair $(2, z)$ to test against; the condition holds vacuously. Many students erroneously mark this as non-transitive.
  • Incomplete Invertibility Arguments: Under the CBSE marking scheme, stating that “$f$ is invertible because $f$ is one-to-one and onto” without showing the explicit verification $f \circ g = I_B$ and $g \circ f = I_A$ (when asked to “find the inverse and prove invertibility via composition”) forfeits partial credit.
  • Misunderstanding Codomain vs. Range: Showing that a function takes real values is insufficient to establish surjectivity. One must prove that every element of the specified codomain is attained by the function.

Rigorous Mathematical Derivations

Derivation 1: Combinatorial Cardinality of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

Objective: Let $A$ be a finite set containing $n$ elements ($|A| = n$). Derive the closed-form expressions for the total number of (i) Reflexive relations, (ii) Symmetric relations, and (iii) Relations that are simultaneously reflexive and symmetric on $A$.

Step 1: Decomposition of Cartesian Product $A \times A$

The total number of elements in the Cartesian product $A \times A$ is given by:

$$|A \times A| = |A| \times |A| = n^2$$

Any binary relation $R$ on $A$ is a subset of $A \times A$. The set $A \times A$ can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets:

  1. Diagonal elements ($D$): Elements of the form $(a_i, a_i)$ where $1 \le i \le n$. $$|D| = n$$
  2. Off-diagonal elements ($O$): Elements of the form $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \ne j$. $$|O| = n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$$

The off-diagonal set $O$ consists of ordered pairs that can be grouped into unordered symmetric pairs $\{\,(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\,\}$ with $i < j$. The number of such pairs is:

$$N_{\text{pairs}} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$$

Step 2: Derivation of Reflexive Relations

By definition, a relation $R$ on $A$ is reflexive if and only if:

$$\forall a_i \in A \implies (a_i, a_i) \in R$$

Thus, every diagonal element must be included in $R$. For each of the $n$ diagonal pairs, there is exactly $1$ choice (mandatory presence). For each of the $n(n - 1)$ off-diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ ($i \ne j$), an independent choice exists: the pair may either belong to $R$ or not belong to $R$ ($2$ choices per pair).

Applying the Fundamental Principle of Counting:

$$N_{\text{refl}} = (1)^n \times 2^{n(n - 1)} = 2^{n^2 - n}$$

Step 3: Derivation of Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if:

$$(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$$
  • For the $n$ diagonal elements $(a_i, a_i)$, the condition $(a_i, a_i) \in R \implies (a_i, a_i) \in R$ is trivially true. Hence, each diagonal element has $2$ choices (present or absent). Total choices for diagonal elements = $2^n$.
  • For the off-diagonal elements, pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ cannot be chosen independently. Either both are included, or neither is included. Thus, for each of the $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ symmetric pairs, there are $2$ choices.

Multiplying the independent choices:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

Step 4: Derivation of Reflexive & Symmetric Relations

For $R$ to be both reflexive and symmetric:

  • All $n$ diagonal elements must be present: $(1)^n = 1$ choice.
  • The off-diagonal elements must satisfy symmetry: each of the $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ pairs has $2$ mutually exclusive outcomes (both in $R$ or both not in $R$).
$$N_{\text{refl \& sym}} = 1 \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Objective: Prove that an equivalence relation $R$ on a non-empty set $A$ partitions $A$ into pairwise disjoint equivalence classes whose union is $A$. That is:

  1. For any $a, b \in A$, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.
  2. $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$.

Step 1: Definition of Equivalence Class

For any $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$ is defined as:

$$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$

Step 2: Proof that $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$

Since $R$ is an equivalence relation, it is reflexive, meaning:

$$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R \implies a \in [a]$$

Since $a \in [a]$, it follows directly that:

$$A \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$$

Conversely, by definition, for every $a \in A$, $[a] \subseteq A$. Therefore:

$$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A$$

By mutual inclusion:

$$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$

Step 3: Proof that $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$

Let $a, b \in A$ and assume that their intersection is non-empty. Let $c \in [a] \cap [b]$.

$$c \in [a] \implies (c, a) \in R$$ $$c \in [b] \implies (c, b) \in R$$

Since $R$ is symmetric:

$$(c, a) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$$

Using transitivity of $R$ on $(a, c) \in R$ and $(c, b) \in R$:

$$(a, c) \in R \land (c, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$$

Now, let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $[a]$. Then:

$$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R$$

Applying transitivity with $(x, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$:

$$(x, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$

Hence, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.

By absolute symmetry of the argument, reversing the roles of $a$ and $b$ yields $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Therefore:

$$[a] = [b]$$

Consequently, if $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$, then $[a] = [b]$. The contrapositive logically holds: if $[a] \ne [b]$, then $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. The equivalence classes partition $A$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: Associativity of Function Composition

Objective: Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$ be three functions. Prove that composition of functions is associative, i.e.:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$

Step 1: Domain and Codomain Analysis

First, verify the mapping structures on both sides of the identity:

  1. $g \circ f$ maps $A \to C$. Therefore, $h \circ (g \circ f)$ maps $A \to D$.
  2. $h \circ g$ maps $B \to D$. Therefore, $(h \circ g) \circ f$ maps $A \to D$.

Both compound mappings have the same domain $A$ and the same codomain $D$.

Step 2: Pointwise Evaluation

Let $x \in A$ be an arbitrary element. Evaluate the left-hand side using the formal definition of function composition:

$$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x))$$

Since $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$, substitution yields:

$$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h(g(f(x)))$$

Now evaluate the right-hand side:

$$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x))$$

Let $y = f(x) \in B$. By the definition of the composition $h \circ g$:

$$(h \circ g)(y) = h(g(y)) \implies (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$

Step 3: Equivalence of Operators

Comparing the pointwise evaluation results:

$$\forall x \in A, \quad (h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = ((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = h(g(f(x)))$$

Since both functions have identical domains, codomains, and assignments for every element in the domain:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Invertibility

Objective: Prove that a function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection (both one-one and onto).

Part 1: Necessity ($\text{Invertible} \implies \text{Bijective}$)

Assume $f: A \to B$ is invertible. Then there exists a unique function $g: B \to A$ such that:

$$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$

(i) Injective Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$ to both sides:

$$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ $$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$

Since $g \circ f = I_A$, we have $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ is one-one (injective).

(ii) Surjective Proof: Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element. Consider $x = g(y) \in A$. Applying $f$ to $x$:

$$f(x) = f(g(y)) = (f \circ g)(y)$$

Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we obtain:

$$f(x) = I_B(y) = y$$

Thus, for every $y \in B$, there exists an element $x = g(y) \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Hence, $f$ is onto (surjective).

Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Part 2: Sufficiency ($\text{Bijective} \implies \text{Invertible}$)

Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.

  • Since $f$ is onto, for each $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is one-one, this element $x$ is unique.

Define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element in $A$ satisfying $f(x) = y$.

Now evaluate compositions:

  1. For any $x \in A$, let $y = f(x)$. By definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. Thus: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x) \implies g \circ f = I_A$$
  2. For any $y \in B$, let $x = g(y)$. Then $f(x) = y$. Thus: $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y) \implies f \circ g = I_B$$

Because $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$, $f$ is invertible with inverse $f^{-1} = g$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Reverse Order Law for the Inverse of a Composition

Objective: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible and that its inverse satisfies:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Step 1: Verification of Identity Inverses

By definition of an inverse mapping, a function $H: C \to A$ is the inverse of $g \circ f: A \to C$ if and only if it satisfies the two conditions:

$$H \circ (g \circ f) = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad (g \circ f) \circ H = I_C$$

We propose the candidate inverse function $H = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

Step 2: Proof of Left Inversion: $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$

Using the associative property of function composition proven in Derivation 3:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ \big(g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)\big)$$ $$= f^{-1} \circ \big((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f\big)$$

Since $g$ is invertible, $g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$. Substituting this yields:

$$= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f)$$

Since the identity function satisfies $I_B \circ f = f$:

$$= f^{-1} \circ f$$

Since $f$ is invertible, $f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$. Therefore:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$$

Step 3: Proof of Right Inversion: $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$

Similarly, applying associativity to the composition in reverse order:

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ \big(f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})\big)$$ $$= g \circ \big((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}\big)$$

Since $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$:

$$= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1})$$

Because $I_B \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}$:

$$= g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

Conclusion:

Since $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ acts as both the left and right inverse to $g \circ f$, by the uniqueness of function inverses, $g \circ f$ is invertible and:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 6: Number of Surjective (Onto) Functions via Inclusion-Exclusion Principle

Objective: Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$, where $m \ge n \ge 1$. Derive the closed-form expression for the total number of surjective functions from $A$ to $B$.

Step 1: Problem Formulation via Complementary Counting

Let $S$ denote the universal set of all possible functions from $A$ to $B$:

$$|S| = n^m$$

Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n\}$. A function $f: A \to B$ is not surjective if its range omits at least one element of $B$.

Define $P_i$ as the property that $b_i$ is not in the range of $f$. Let $A_i \subseteq S$ be the set of functions satisfying property $P_i$:

$$A_i = \{f \in S : b_i \notin f(A)\}$$

The total number of onto functions, $N_{\text{surj}}$, is the number of functions that satisfy none of the properties $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = |S| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right|$$

Step 2: Application of the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

By the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion:

$$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right| = \sum_{1 \le i \le n} |A_i| - \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} |A_i \cap A_j| + \sum_{1 \le i < j < k \le n} |A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k| - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} |A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n|$$

Step 3: Computation of Cardinalities of Intersections

  1. If a function $f \in A_i$, then its codomain is effectively restricted to $B \setminus \{b_i\}$, which has $n - 1$ available elements. The number of such functions is: $$|A_i| = (n - 1)^m$$ There are $\binom{n}{1}$ ways to select the omitted element $b_i$.
  2. If $f \in A_i \cap A_j$ ($i \ne j$), both $b_i$ and $b_j$ are omitted from the range. The codomain is restricted to $B \setminus \{b_i, b_j\}$, containing $n - 2$ elements: $$|A_i \cap A_j| = (n - 2)^m$$ There are $\binom{n}{2}$ ways to select 2 omitted elements.
  3. In general, for the intersection of any $k$ sets ($1 \le k \le n$): $$|A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = (n - k)^m$$ The number of ways to choose $k$ excluded elements is $\binom{n}{k}$.

Step 4: Algebraic Summation

Substituting these cardinalities into the PIE formula:

$$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i \right| = \binom{n}{1}(n - 1)^m - \binom{n}{2}(n - 2)^m + \binom{n}{3}(n - 3)^m - \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n}(0)^m$$ $$= \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Now calculate $N_{\text{surj}}$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = n^m - \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k-1} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Since $n^m = \binom{n}{0}(n - 0)^m$, we absorb $n^m$ into the summation at index $k = 0$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \binom{n}{0}(n - 0)^m + \sum_{k=1}^n (-1)^{k} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Equivalently, letting $r = n - k$ represent the number of elements actually present in the range:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = n! \cdot S(m, n) = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n - r} \binom{n}{r} r^m$$

where $S(m, n)$ denotes the Stirling numbers of the second kind. $\blacksquare$
