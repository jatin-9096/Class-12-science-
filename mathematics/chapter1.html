1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Set-Theoretic Prerequisites and the Cartesian Architecture

The mathematical formulation of relations and functions rests upon axiomatic set theory. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be two non-empty sets defined within a universal discourse \(\mathcal{U}\). The fundamental structural primitive linking these spaces is the Cartesian Product, denoted by \(A \times B\), which constitutes the collection of all ordered pairs whose first coordinate is selected from \(A\) and whose second coordinate is selected from \(B\):

\[ A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\} \]

The defining axiom governing ordered pairs states that \((a_1, b_1) = (a_2, b_2)\) if and only if \(a_1 = a_2\) and \(b_1 = b_2\). This directional asymmetry distinguishes the ordered pair \((a, b)\) from the unordered set \(\{a, b\}\), thereby providing the coordinates required to map dependency structures.

When \(A\) and \(B\) are finite sets with cardinalities \(|A| = m\) and \(|B| = n\), the cardinality of their Cartesian product is given by:

\[ |A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n \]

The power set \(\mathcal{P}(A \times B)\) denotes the set of all subsets of \(A \times B\). The cardinality of this power set is \(|\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{mn}\). Any mathematical relation \(R\) from \(A\) to \(B\) is formally defined as an arbitrary element of this power set:

\[ R \subseteq A \times B \iff R \in \mathcal{P}(A \times B) \]

If \((a, b) \in R\), we say that \(a\) is related to \(b\) under \(R\), conventionally written as \(a\,R\,b\). Conversely, if \((a, b) \notin R\), \(a\) is not related to \(b\), denoted \(a \not R\, b\). When \(A = B\), \(R \subseteq A \times A\) is referred to as an endorelation or simply a binary relation on set \(A\).

2. Rigorous Taxonomy of Binary Relations on a Set

Let \(A\) be a non-empty set. A relation \(R\) on \(A\) can exhibit specific algebraic properties that categorize its operational behavior. We classify these properties through precise predicate calculus expressions:

2.1 Trivial and Extremal Relations

  • Empty Relation (Void Relation): A relation \(\phi \subset A \times A\) containing no ordered pairs:
    \[ R = \emptyset \subset A \times A \]
    The empty relation satisfies properties conditionally when universal quantification is vacuously satisfied.
  • Universal Relation: The complete Cartesian product itself:
    \[ R = A \times A \]
    Every element of \(A\) is related to every element of \(A\).
  • Identity Relation (\(I_A\)): The diagonal subset of \(A \times A\):
    \[ I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\} \]
    Under \(I_A\), every element is related exclusively to itself and to no other element.

2.2 The Fundamental Triad: Reflexivity, Symmetry, and Transitivity

The classification of relations in Class 12 CBSE Mathematics is anchored upon three properties:

Definition 2.2.1: Reflexivity

A relation \(R\) on set \(A\) is reflexive if every element of \(A\) is related to itself:

\[ \forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R \]

Critical Analytical Distinction: The identity relation \(I_A\) requires that \((a, b) \in I_A \iff a = b\). In contrast, reflexivity requires that \((a, a) \in R\) for all \(a \in A\), while permitting additional pairs \((a, b)\) with \(a \neq b\). Thus, every identity relation is reflexive, but a reflexive relation is not necessarily an identity relation: \(I_A \subseteq R_{\text{reflexive}}\).

Definition 2.2.2: Symmetry

A relation \(R\) on set \(A\) is symmetric if the presence of any pair implies the presence of its inverted pair:

\[ \forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R \]

In terms of the inverse relation \(R^{-1} = \{(b, a) : (a, b) \in R\}\), a relation is symmetric if and only if \(R = R^{-1}\). Note that symmetry does not demand that \((a, b)\) exists for any arbitrary pair; it merely states that if an edge exists in the directed graph of the relation, the reciprocal anti-parallel edge must also exist.

Definition 2.2.3: Transitivity

A relation \(R\) on set \(A\) is transitive if a chain of two related elements guarantees the relation of the endpoints:

\[ \forall a, b, c \in A, \quad \big((a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R\big) \implies (a, c) \in R \]

The Vacuous Truth Condition: If there are no pairs \((a, b)\) and \((b, c)\) in \(R\) with a common intermediary \(b\), the hypothesis of the conditional statement is false. By the rules of classical propositional logic (\(P \implies Q\) is true whenever \(P\) is false), transitivity holds vacuously. For example, if \(A = \{1, 2, 3\}\) and \(R = \{(1, 2)\}\), the antecedent \((1, 2) \in R \land (2, c) \in R\) is never met, since no pair starts with \(2\). Hence, \(R\) is transitive.

2.3 Additional Structural Classifications

  • Anti-Symmetric Relation: \(R\) is anti-symmetric if:
    \[ \forall a, b \in A, \quad \big((a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R\big) \implies a = b \]
    Equivalently, if \(a \neq b\), then \((a, b)\) and \((b, a)\) cannot both belong to \(R\). (Example: the relation "\(\le\)" on \(\mathbb{R}\)).
  • Equivalence Relation: A relation \(R\) on a non-empty set \(A\) is an equivalence relation if and only if it is simultaneously:
    1. Reflexive,
    2. Symmetric, and
    3. Transitive.

3. Equivalence Classes and the Fundamental Partition Theorem

Equivalence relations group together elements that share a defined mathematical property, abstracting away differences between elements within the same group.

3.1 Definition of an Equivalence Class

Let \(R\) be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set \(A\). For any arbitrary element \(a \in A\), the equivalence class of \(a\), denoted by \([a]\) or \(\text{cl}(a)\), is the set of all elements in \(A\) that are related to \(a\) under \(R\):

\[ [a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\} \]

Because \(R\) is symmetric, \((x, a) \in R \iff (a, x) \in R\), meaning \([a] = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}\).

3.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

The fundamental theorem states that an equivalence relation on a set \(A\) partitions \(A\) into mutually disjoint subsets whose union is \(A\). Conversely, any partition of \(A\) induces a unique equivalence relation on \(A\).

Formal Proof of Equivalence Class Properties:

  1. Non-emptiness: Since \(R\) is reflexive, \((a, a) \in R\) for all \(a \in A\). Therefore, \(a \in [a]\), which implies \([a] \neq \emptyset\).
  2. Coincidence vs Disjointness: For any two elements \(a, b \in A\), either \([a] = [b]\) or \([a] \cap [b] = \emptyset\).
    Proof: Assume \([a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset\). We must show that \([a] = [b]\).
    Let \(x \in [a] \cap [b]\). Then \(x \in [a]\) and \(x \in [b]\).
    By definition: \((x, a) \in R\) and \((x, b) \in R\).
    By symmetry, \((x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R\).
    By transitivity, \((a, x) \in R\) and \((x, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R\).
    Now, pick an arbitrary element \(y \in [a]\). Then \((y, a) \in R\).
    Since \((y, a) \in R\) and \((a, b) \in R\), transitivity gives \((y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]\).
    Hence, \([a] \subseteq [b]\).
    By exact symmetry of argument, \([b] \subseteq [a]\).
    Therefore, \([a] = [b]\).
  3. Exhaustive Union: Since \(a \in [a]\) for every \(a \in A\):
    \[ \bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A \]

The set of all equivalence classes is called the quotient set of \(A\) by \(R\), denoted:

\[ A / R = \{ [a] : a \in A \} \]

3.3 The Canonical Fallacy: "Symmetric + Transitive \(\implies\) Reflexive"

A common misconception in discrete mathematics is the false proof:

"Assume \((a, b) \in R\). By symmetry, \((b, a) \in R\). By transitivity, since \((a, b) \in R\) and \((b, a) \in R\), we have \((a, a) \in R\). Thus \(R\) is reflexive."

The Logical Fallacy: The proof assumes that for every \(a \in A\), there exists some \(b \in A\) such that \((a, b) \in R\). If an element \(x \in A\) is completely isolated (i.e., \(\forall y \in A, (x, y) \notin R\)), the antecedent never triggers.

Concrete Counterexample: Let \(A = \{1, 2, 3\}\) and \(R = \{(1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 2)\}\).
1. Reflexivity: Fails, because \(3 \in A\) but \((3, 3) \notin R\).
2. Symmetry: Holds; for every pair, its reverse is in \(R\).
3. Transitivity: Holds across all chains.
Hence, \(R\) is symmetric and transitive, but not reflexive on \(A\).

4. Combinatorial Analysis of Relations on Finite Sets

Let \(A\) be a finite set with \(|A| = n\). The Cartesian product \(A \times A\) contains \(n^2\) ordered pairs, which can be visualized as an \(n \times n\) adjacency matrix \(M\) where \(M_{ij} = 1\) if \((a_i, a_j) \in R\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Type of Relation Closed-Form Formula Mathematical Derivation Mechanism
Total Relations \(2^{n^2}\) Each of the \(n^2\) elements in \(A \times A\) has 2 independent choices: to be included or excluded.
Reflexive Relations \(2^{n(n - 1)}\) All \(n\) diagonal pairs \((a_i, a_i)\) must be included (1 choice). The remaining \(n^2 - n = n(n - 1)\) off-diagonal pairs can be freely included or excluded (2 choices each).
Symmetric Relations \(2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}\) Diagonal elements have \(n\) independent choices. There are \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) pairs of symmetric off-diagonal positions \(\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}\). For each pair, either both are present or both are absent (2 choices). Total choices: \(2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\).
Reflexive and Symmetric \(2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}\) All \(n\) diagonal pairs are fixed (present). Only the \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) unordered off-diagonal pairs can be independently chosen.
Anti-Symmetric Relations \(2^n \cdot 3^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}\) The \(n\) diagonal pairs can be included or excluded (\(2^n\)). For each of the \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) off-diagonal pairs, there are 3 valid states: only \((a_i, a_j)\), only \((a_j, a_i)\), or neither. Both cannot be present.

5. Advanced Function Theory: Mapping Dynamics & Classification

A function is a relation that imposes strict uniqueness constraints on its pairings. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two non-empty sets. A relation \(f \subseteq X \times Y\) is a function (or mapping), written \(f: X \to Y\), if and only if:

\[ \forall x \in X, \quad \exists! y \in Y \quad \text{such that} \quad (x, y) \in f \]

This condition splits into two structural requirements:

  1. Totality: Every element \(x \in X\) has an assigned image: \(\text{Dom}(f) = X\).
  2. Well-Definedness (Single-Valuedness): No element \(x \in X\) maps to more than one element in \(Y\):
    \[ (x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f \implies y_1 = y_2 \]

We write \(y = f(x)\). Here, \(X\) is the Domain, \(Y\) is the Codomain, and the Range (or Image set) is:

\[ f(X) = \text{Ran}(f) = \{y \in Y : \exists x \in X, f(x) = y\} \subseteq Y \]

5.1 Injective (One-to-One) Functions

A mapping \(f: X \to Y\) is injective (or one-to-one) if distinct domain elements always produce distinct images in the codomain:

\[ \forall x_1, x_2 \in X, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2 \]

The contrapositive form is equally useful for proofs:

\[ \forall x_1, x_2 \in X, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2) \]

If a function is not injective, it is termed many-to-one:

\[ \exists x_1, x_2 \in X \quad \text{such that} \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2) \]

Calculus-Based Injectivity Criteria (Monotonicity Method):

Let \(f: I \to \mathbb{R}\) be a differentiable function on an open interval \(I \subseteq \mathbb{R}\).

  • If \(f'(x) > 0\) for all \(x \in I\), then \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(I\), and therefore injective.
  • If \(f'(x) < 0\) for all \(x \in I\), then \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \(I\), and therefore injective.
  • If \(f'(x) \ge 0\) (or \(\le 0\)) where \(f'(x) = 0\) only at isolated discrete points (not over any sub-interval), \(f\) remains strictly monotonic and thus injective.
  • If \(f'(x)\) changes sign over \(I\), by the Intermediate Value Property of derivatives (Darboux's Theorem), there exist multiple domain values producing the same image, making \(f\) many-to-one.

5.2 Surjective (Onto) Functions

A mapping \(f: X \to Y\) is surjective (or onto) if every element in the codomain has at least one pre-image in the domain:

\[ \forall y \in Y, \quad \exists x \in X \quad \text{such that} \quad f(x) = y \]

Equivalently, \(f\) is surjective if and only if its range equals its codomain:

\[ \text{Ran}(f) = Y \]

If \(\text{Ran}(f) \subsetneq Y\), the function is termed into.

Analytical Surjectivity Determination Method:

To determine whether \(f: X \to Y\) is surjective analytically:

  1. Set up the equation \(y = f(x)\), where \(y\) is treated as an arbitrary, fixed parameter in the codomain \(Y\).
  2. Algebraically invert the relation to solve for \(x\) entirely in terms of \(y\): \(x = g(y)\).
  3. Verify whether the resulting expression \(g(y)\) is defined and produces a valid value in the domain \(X\) for every choice of \(y \in Y\).
  4. If any \(y \in Y\) forces \(x \notin X\), or makes \(x\) undefined/non-real, surjectivity fails.

5.3 Bijective (One-to-One and Onto) Functions

A mapping \(f: X \to Y\) is bijective if and only if it is simultaneously injective and surjective:

\[ f \text{ is bijective} \iff (f \text{ is injective}) \land (f \text{ is surjective}) \]

A bijection establishes a one-to-one correspondence between sets \(X\) and \(Y\). For finite sets, a bijection exists if and only if \(|X| = |Y|\).

6. Combinatorial Enumeration of Mappings

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be finite sets with cardinalities \(|X| = m\) and \(|Y| = n\).

6.1 Total Number of Functions

Each of the \(m\) elements in \(X\) can be independently mapped to any of the \(n\) elements in \(Y\):

\[ N_{\text{total}} = n^m = |Y|^{|X|} \]

6.2 Number of Injective Functions

If \(m > n\), by the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two domain elements must map to the same codomain element. Thus, no injective function can exist. If \(m \le n\), the first element in \(X\) has \(n\) possible choices, the second has \((n - 1)\), and so on:

\[ N_{\text{inj}} = \begin{cases} P(n, m) = \dfrac{n!}{(n - m)!}, & \text{if } m \le n \\[8pt] 0, & \text{if } m > n \end{cases} \]

6.3 Number of Surjective Functions

If \(m < n\), the range can contain at most \(m\) elements, which is strictly less than \(n\); hence, no surjective function can exist.

For \(m \ge n\), the count is computed using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE) or Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind, \(S(m, n)\):

\[ N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} (n - r)^m = n! \cdot S(m, n) \]

Expanding this formula gives:

\[ N_{\text{surj}} = n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m - \binom{n}{3}(n-3)^m + \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}(1)^m \]

6.4 Number of Bijective Functions

Bijective mappings require \(m = n\). In this case, surjectivity and injectivity coincide:

\[ N_{\text{bij}} = \begin{cases} n!, & \text{if } m = n \\ 0, & \text{if } m \neq n \end{cases} \]

7. Composition of Functions

Let \(f: A \to B\) and \(g: B \to C\) be two functions. The composite function, denoted \(g \circ f\) (read as "\(g\) circle \(f\)" or "\(g\) composed with \(f\)"), is a mapping from \(A\) to \(C\) defined by:

\[ (g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A \]

Domain-Range Compatibility Condition:

The composite \(g \circ f\) is well-defined if and only if the range of the inner function is a subset of the domain of the outer function:

\[ \text{Ran}(f) \subseteq \text{Dom}(g) \]

If this condition fails, the domain of \(g \circ f\) must be restricted:

\[ \text{Dom}(g \circ f) = \{x \in \text{Dom}(f) : f(x) \in \text{Dom}(g)\} \]

7.1 Algebraic Properties of Function Composition

  • Non-Commutativity: In general, function composition does not commute:
    \[ g \circ f \neq f \circ g \]
    Often, if \(g \circ f\) is defined, \(f \circ g\) may not even exist unless \(\text{Dom}(f) = \text{Codom}(g)\) and \(\text{Dom}(g) = \text{Codom}(f)\). Even when both are defined, they rarely coincide.
  • Associativity: If \(f: A \to B\), \(g: B \to C\), and \(h: C \to D\), then:
    \[ h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f \]

    Proof: For any arbitrary element \(x \in A\):

    \[ [h \circ (g \circ f)](x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x))) \] \[ [((h \circ g) \circ f)](x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x))) \]
    Because their operational values and domains agree, the two composites are identical.
  • Identity Morphism: For any function \(f: A \to B\):
    \[ f \circ I_A = f \quad \text{and} \quad I_B \circ f = f \]
    where \(I_A\) and \(I_B\) represent the identity functions on sets \(A\) and \(B\), respectively.

7.2 Structural Preservation Theorems

Theorem 7.2.1: Preservation of Injectivity and Surjectivity

  1. If \(f: A \to B\) and \(g: B \to C\) are injective, then \(g \circ f: A \to C\) is injective.
    Proof: Let \(x_1, x_2 \in A\) such that \((g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)\).
    \(\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))\).
    Since \(g\) is injective, this implies \(f(x_1) = f(x_2)\).
    Since \(f\) is injective, this implies \(x_1 = x_2\). Thus, \(g \circ f\) is injective.
  2. If \(f: A \to B\) and \(g: B \to C\) are surjective, then \(g \circ f: A \to C\) is surjective.
    Proof: Let \(z \in C\). Since \(g\) is surjective, there exists \(y \in B\) such that \(g(y) = z\).
    Since \(y \in B\) and \(f\) is surjective, there exists \(x \in A\) such that \(f(x) = y\).
    Therefore, \((g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = z\). Thus, \(g \circ f\) is surjective.

Theorem 7.2.2: Reverse Deduction Conditions

  1. If \(g \circ f\) is injective, then \(f\) must be injective. (Note: \(g\) need not be injective).
    Proof: Assume \(f(x_1) = f(x_2)\). Apply \(g\) to both sides: \(g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)\). Since \(g \circ f\) is injective, \(x_1 = x_2\). Hence, \(f\) is injective.
  2. If \(g \circ f\) is surjective, then \(g\) must be surjective. (Note: \(f\) need not be surjective).
    Proof: Let \(z \in C\). Since \(g \circ f\) is surjective, there exists \(x \in A\) such that \((g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z\). Choosing \(y = f(x) \in B\), we have found a \(y \in B\) such that \(g(y) = z\). Hence, \(g\) is surjective.

8. Invertibility and Inverse Functions

A function \(f: X \to Y\) is said to be invertible if there exists a function \(g: Y \to X\) such that:

\[ g \circ f = I_X \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_Y \]

Under these conditions, \(g\) is called the inverse of \(f\) and is denoted \(g = f^{-1}\).

The Invertibility Equivalence Theorem:

A function \(f: X \to Y\) is invertible if and only if it is a bijection (both one-to-one and onto).

Proof (\(\implies\)): Invertibility implies Bijectivity:

Assume \(f\) is invertible; then there exists \(g: Y \to X\) such that \(g \circ f = I_X\) and \(f \circ g = I_Y\).
Since \(g \circ f = I_X\) and \(I_X\) is an injection, by Theorem 7.2.2, \(f\) must be injective.
Since \(f \circ g = I_Y\) and \(I_Y\) is a surjection, by Theorem 7.2.2, \(f\) must be surjective.
Consequently, \(f\) is bijective.

Proof (\(\impliedby\)): Bijectivity implies Invertibility:

Assume \(f\) is bijective. Since \(f\) is surjective, for every \(y \in Y\), there exists \(x \in X\) such that \(f(x) = y\).
Since \(f\) is injective, this \(x\) is unique.
We can define a rule \(g: Y \to X\) by setting \(g(y) = x\), where \(x\) is the unique pre-image of \(y\).
Now check the composition:
\((g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_X(x)\), so \(g \circ f = I_X\).
\((f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_Y(y)\), so \(f \circ g = I_Y\).
Hence, \(f\) is invertible, and \(g = f^{-1}\).

8.1 Uniqueness and Reversal Laws

  • Uniqueness: The inverse of a bijective function is unique.
    Proof: Suppose \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) are both inverses of \(f\). Then:
    \[ g_1 = g_1 \circ I_Y = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_X \circ g_2 = g_2 \]
  • Involution Property: If \(f: X \to Y\) is bijective, then \(f^{-1}: Y \to X\) is also bijective, and:
    \[ (f^{-1})^{-1} = f \]
  • Reversal Law for Composition: If \(f: A \to B\) and \(g: B \to C\) are two invertible functions, then \(g \circ f\) is invertible, and:
    \[ (g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1} \]

    Proof: Verify by composition with the identity:

    \[ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A \] \[ (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C \]
    By uniqueness of the inverse, \((g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}\).

9. Abstract Algebraic Extensions: Binary Operations

A binary operation \(*\) on a non-empty set \(S\) is a mapping from the Cartesian product \(S \times S\) into \(S\):

\[ * : S \times S \to S \]

We denote \(*(a, b)\) by \(a * b\). This definition requires closure: for all \(a, b \in S\), the result \(a * b\) must belong to \(S\).

9.1 Operational Axioms

  • Commutativity: An operation \(*\) on \(S\) is commutative if:
    \[ \forall a, b \in S, \quad a * b = b * a \]
  • Associativity: An operation \(*\) on \(S\) is associative if:
    \[ \forall a, b, c \in S, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c) \]
  • Identity Element: An element \(e \in S\) is the identity element with respect to \(*\) if:
    \[ \forall a \in S, \quad a * e = e * a = a \]
    Uniqueness: If an identity exists, it is unique. If \(e_1, e_2\) are both identity elements, then \(e_1 * e_2 = e_1\) (using \(e_2\) as identity) and \(e_1 * e_2 = e_2\) (using \(e_1\) as identity), so \(e_1 = e_2\).
  • Invertible Elements: Given an identity \(e \in S\), an element \(a \in S\) is invertible if there exists an element \(b \in S\) such that:
    \[ a * b = b * a = e \]
    Here, \(b\) is called the inverse of \(a\), written \(a^{-1}\). In an associative structure, the inverse of any element is unique.

10. Examination Traps and Diagnostic Counterexamples

Examiner Note 1: Verifying Transitivity with Vacuous Cases

Consider \(R = \{(1, 2)\}\) on \(A = \{1, 2, 3\}\). Many students incorrectly claim \(R\) is not transitive because there is no \((2, 1)\) or \((1, 1)\).
However, transitivity only requires: IF \((a, b) \in R\) and \((b, c) \in R\), THEN \((a, c) \in R\).
Since there is no pair starting with \(2\), the premise is false, meaning the implication is vacuously true. Thus, \(R\) is transitive.

Examiner Note 2: Domain and Codomain Sensitivity

Injectivity and surjectivity depend directly on the specified domain and codomain:

  • \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, \; f(x) = x^2\): Neither injective (\(f(-2) = f(2) = 4\)) nor surjective (\(-4 \in \text{Codomain}\) has no real pre-image).
  • \(f: [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty), \; f(x) = x^2\): Both injective and surjective (Bijective), with inverse \(f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt{x}\).
  • \(f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}, \; f(x) = 2x\): Injective, but not surjective (odd natural numbers have no pre-images in \(\mathbb{N}\)).
  • \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, \; f(x) = x^3 - x\): Not injective (\(f(-1) = f(0) = f(1) = 0\)), but surjective, since \(\lim_{x \to \pm\infty} f(x) = \pm\infty\) and \(f\) is continuous.

Examiner Note 3: Modulus and Piecewise Bijections

For functions such as \(f(x) = \frac{x}{1 + |x|}\) where \(f: \mathbb{R} \to (-1, 1)\):

  1. Injectivity: Split into cases:
    • Case I (\(x_1, x_2 \ge 0\)): \(\frac{x_1}{1+x_1} = \frac{x_2}{1+x_2} \implies x_1 + x_1 x_2 = x_2 + x_1 x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2\).
    • Case II (\(x_1, x_2 < 0\)): \(\frac{x_1}{1-x_1} = \frac{x_2}{1-x_2} \implies x_1 - x_1 x_2 = x_2 - x_1 x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2\).
    • Case III (\(x_1 \ge 0\) and \(x_2 < 0\)): Here \(f(x_1) \ge 0\) while \(f(x_2) < 0\), so \(f(x_1) = f(x_2)\) is impossible unless \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\).
    Thus, \(f\) is strictly injective across all cases.
  2. Surjectivity: Take any \(y \in (-1, 1)\):
    • If \(0 \le y < 1\), solve \(y = \frac{x}{1+x} \implies x = \frac{y}{1-y}\). Since \(y \ge 0\) and \(1-y > 0\), we have \(x \ge 0\).
    • If \(-1 < y < 0\), solve \(y = \frac{x}{1-x} \implies x = \frac{y}{1+y}\). Since \(y < 0\) and \(1+y > 0\), we have \(x < 0\).
    In both cases, a valid pre-image \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) exists for every \(y \in (-1, 1)\).
    Thus, \(f\) is surjective, making it a bijective mapping.

11. Standard Proof Templates for Board Examinations

To secure full marks on CBSE Class 12 board examinations, students must use structured proofs with explicit quantifiers:

Protocol A: Proving an Equivalence Relation

  1. Reflexivity: State: "Let \(a \in A\) be arbitrary." Show that \((a, a) \in R\) by verifying the defining condition. Conclude: "Since \((a, a) \in R\) for all \(a \in A\), \(R\) is reflexive."
  2. Symmetry: State: "Let \((a, b) \in R\)." Show that this condition directly implies \((b, a) \in R\). Conclude: "Since \((a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R\) for all \(a, b \in A\), \(R\) is symmetric."
  3. Transitivity: State: "Let \((a, b) \in R\) and \((b, c) \in R\)." Combine these algebraic conditions to show that \((a, c) \in R\). Conclude: "Since \((a, b) \in R\) and \((b, c) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R\) for all \(a, b, c \in A\), \(R\) is transitive."
  4. Final Conclusion: "Since \(R\) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, \(R\) is an equivalence relation on \(A\)."

Protocol B: Proving Invertibility and Finding the Inverse

  1. Show Injectivity: Let \(f(x_1) = f(x_2)\). Use algebra to deduce \(x_1 = x_2\).
  2. Show Surjectivity: Let \(y \in Y\) (the codomain). Solve the equation \(y = f(x)\) for \(x\) in terms of \(y\). Show that \(x \in X\) (the domain) for all \(y \in Y\), and confirm that \(f(x) = y\).
  3. Construct Inverse: State: "Since \(f\) is one-to-one and onto, \(f\) is invertible." Write down the inverse function explicitly: \(f^{-1}(y) = g(y)\), replacing the dummy variable with \(x\) if requested: \(f^{-1}(x) = g(x)\).

Derivation 1: Total Number of Reflexive Relations on a Finite Set

Statement: Let $A$ be a finite set containing $n$ elements. Prove that the total number of distinct reflexive relations that can be defined on $A$ is $2^{n(n - 1)}$.

Step 1: Set representation and Cartesian product dimension.
Let $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$ such that $|A| = n$.
The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $|A \times A| = n \times n = n^2$ ordered pairs of the form $(a_i, a_j)$, where $1 \le i, j \le n$.
Step 2: Partitioning ordered pairs into diagonal and non-diagonal subsets.
The elements of $A \times A$ can be partitioned into two mutually exclusive and exhaustive subsets: $$\Delta = \{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\} \quad \text{(Diagonal / Identical pairs)}$$ $$\Delta^c = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \ne j, \, 1 \le i, j \le n\} \quad \text{(Non-diagonal pairs)}$$ The cardinalities are: $$|\Delta| = n$$ $$|\Delta^c| = n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$$
Step 3: Imposing the reflexive condition.
A binary relation $R$ on $A$ is reflexive if and only if $\forall a \in A$, $(a, a) \in R$.
Consequently, all elements of $\Delta$ must be mandatorily included in $R$: $$\Delta \subseteq R$$ Therefore, for each pair $(a_i, a_i) \in \Delta$, the number of choices is exactly $1$.
Step 4: Evaluating combinatorial choices for non-diagonal pairs.
For each of the remaining $n(n - 1)$ pairs in $\Delta^c$, there are no constraints imposed by the definition of reflexivity. Hence, each pair $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \ne j$ may either belong to $R$ or not belong to $R$ (2 independent choices per pair).
Using the fundamental principle of multiplication, the total number of reflexive relations $N_{ref}$ is: $$N_{ref} = (1)^n \times 2^{|\Delta^c|} = 1 \times 2^{n(n - 1)} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

Derivation 2: Total Number of Symmetric Relations on a Finite Set

Statement: Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. Prove that the total number of symmetric relations on $A$ is $2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$.

Step 1: Symmetry condition on the Cartesian product matrix.
A relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \implies (a_j, a_i) \in R$ for all $1 \le i, j \le n$.
Consider the $n \times n$ matrix representing $A \times A$: $$\begin{pmatrix} (a_1, a_1) & (a_1, a_2) & \cdots & (a_1, a_n) \\ (a_2, a_1) & (a_2, a_2) & \cdots & (a_2, a_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ (a_n, a_1) & (a_n, a_2) & \cdots & (a_n, a_n) \end{pmatrix}$$
Step 2: Analysis of diagonal entries.
The main diagonal consists of $n$ elements: $(a_1, a_1), (a_2, a_2), \dots, (a_n, a_n)$.
For any diagonal pair, $(a_i, a_i) \in R \implies (a_i, a_i) \in R$, which is inherently satisfied.
Thus, each of the $n$ diagonal pairs can independently either be included or excluded from $R$. This yields: $$2^n \text{ choices}$$
Step 3: Analysis of non-diagonal symmetric pairs.
The remaining $n^2 - n$ elements consist of pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \ne j$. These pairs naturally group into unordered sets of size 2: $$\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\} \quad \text{for } 1 \le i < j \le n$$ The total number of such unordered symmetric pairs is: $$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$$ For each unordered pair $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, by the definition of symmetry, there are only 2 valid configurations:
  1. Both $(a_i, a_j) \in R$ and $(a_j, a_i) \in R$
  2. Neither $(a_i, a_j) \in R$ nor $(a_j, a_i) \in R$
(Configurations with only one pair present violate symmetry).
Step 4: Total calculation.
Combining independent choices for diagonal elements and unordered non-diagonal pairs: $$N_{sym} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n^2 + n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

Derivation 3: Number of Injective (One-One) Functions

Statement: Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$. Find the total number of injective mappings from $A$ to $B$.

Step 1: Definition of injectivity.
A function $f: A \to B$ is injective (one-to-one) if and only if for all $x_1, x_2 \in A$: $$f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Equivalently: $$x_1 \ne x_2 \implies f(x_1) \ne f(x_2)$$
Step 2: Pigeonhole constraint.
If $m > n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two distinct elements in $A$ must map to the same image in $B$. Thus: $$N_{inj} = 0 \quad \text{if } m > n$$
Step 3: Sequential assignment principle ($n \ge m$).
Let $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m\}$.
  • For the image of $a_1$, $f(a_1)$ can be chosen from any of the $n$ elements of $B$: $n$ options.
  • For the image of $a_2$, $f(a_2) \ne f(a_1)$, leaving $n - 1$ possible options in $B$.
  • For the image of $a_3$, $f(a_3) \notin \{f(a_1), f(a_2)\}$, leaving $n - 2$ options.
  • Continuing this process up to the $m$-th element, $a_m$ has $n - (m - 1) = n - m + 1$ options.
Step 4: Product formulation.
By the Fundamental Principle of Counting: $$N_{inj} = n \times (n - 1) \times (n - 2) \times \cdots \times (n - m + 1) = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!} = {}^n P_m$$

Derivation 4: Number of Surjective (Onto) Functions via Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Statement: Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ with $m \ge n$. Derive the formula for the number of onto functions from $A$ to $B$.

Step 1: Complementary formulation via bad properties.
Let $U$ be the universe of all possible functions from $A$ to $B$. The total number of functions is: $$|U| = n^m$$ Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n\}$. A function $f: A \to B$ is surjective if and only if $\text{Range}(f) = B$, meaning no element $b_i \in B$ is omitted from the range.
For each $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, define the property $P_i$ as the condition that $b_i$ is not in the range of $f$: $$E_i = \{f: A \to B \mid b_i \notin f(A)\}$$ A function is surjective if it satisfies none of the properties $E_1, E_2, \dots, E_n$.
Step 2: Evaluating intersections of sets $E_i$.
If a function excludes a specific set of $r$ elements of $B$, all elements of $A$ must map into the remaining $(n - r)$ elements of $B$:
  • For any single element $b_i$: $|E_i| = (n - 1)^m$
  • For any intersection of 2 elements: $|E_i \cap E_j| = (n - 2)^m \quad (i \ne j)$
  • For any intersection of $r$ elements: $\left|\bigcap_{j=1}^r E_{i_j}\right| = (n - r)^m$
The number of ways to select $r$ excluded elements from $n$ elements is $\binom{n}{r}$.
Step 3: Application of the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion.
The number of surjective functions $N_{surj}$ is given by: $$N_{surj} = |U| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n E_i \right|$$ Applying PIE: $$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^n E_i \right| = \sum_{r=1}^n (-1)^{r - 1} \binom{n}{r} (n - r)^m$$ Subtracting this from $|U| = \binom{n}{0} n^m$: $$N_{surj} = \binom{n}{0} n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n - 1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n - 2)^m - \cdots + (-1)^n \binom{n}{n}(0)^m$$ $$N_{surj} = \sum_{r=0}^n (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} (n - r)^m$$

Derivation 5: Reversal Law for Inverse of Composite Functions

Statement: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible (bijective) functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible and that: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Step 1: Establishment of inverse mapping existence.
Since $f: A \to B$ is invertible, there exists a unique function $f^{-1}: B \to A$ such that: $$f^{-1} \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$$ Since $g: B \to C$ is invertible, there exists a unique function $g^{-1}: C \to B$ such that: $$g^{-1} \circ g = I_B \quad \text{and} \quad g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$
Step 2: Verifying left identity condition for $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$.
Consider the composition $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f): A \to A$.
Using the associative property of function composition: $$\begin{aligned} (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ \left( g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f) \right) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ \left( (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f \right) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) \end{aligned}$$ Since $I_B \circ f = f$: $$f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$
Step 3: Verifying right identity condition for $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$.
Consider the composition $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}): C \to C$.
Applying associativity: $$\begin{aligned} (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ \left( f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \right) \\ &= g \circ \left( (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} \right) \\ &= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) \end{aligned}$$ Since $I_B \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}$: $$g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$
Step 4: Uniqueness conclusion.
A map $h: C \to A$ is the inverse of $g \circ f$ if and only if $h \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$ and $(g \circ f) \circ h = I_C$.
Since $h = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ satisfies both conditions uniquely: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Derivation 6: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Equivalence Classes Partition a Set)

Statement: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Prove that any two equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either identical or disjoint, i.e., $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.

Step 1: Definition of equivalence class.
For any $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$ is defined as: $$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$ Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R \implies a \in [a]$. Thus, no equivalence class is empty ($[a] \ne \emptyset, \forall a \in A$).
Step 2: Proof by contradiction / alternative cases.
Let $[a]$ and $[b]$ be two equivalence classes for $a, b \in A$.
Either $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ or $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$.
If $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$, the condition holds trivially.
Now, assume $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$. We must prove that this implies $[a] = [b]$.
Step 3: Proving containment $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
Since $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$, there exists an element $c \in A$ such that: $$c \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad c \in [b]$$ By definition of equivalence classes: $$(c, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (c, b) \in R$$ Since $R$ is symmetric: $$(c, a) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$$ Now let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $[a]$. Then: $$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R$$ Using transitivity of $R$ on $(x, a) \in R$ and $(a, c) \in R$: $$(x, c) \in R$$ Using transitivity again on $(x, c) \in R$ and $(c, b) \in R$: $$(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$ Since every $x \in [a]$ belongs to $[b]$, we establish: $$[a] \subseteq [b]$$
Step 4: Proving reverse containment and concluding equality.
By symmetry of the argument, reversing the roles of $a$ and $b$ gives: $$[b] \subseteq [a]$$ From $[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a]$, it follows that: $$[a] = [b]$$ Hence, any two equivalence classes are either disjoint or completely identical.
