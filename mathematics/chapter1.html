1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Set-Theoretic Mechanics & Cartesian Products

At the foundation of modern mathematical analysis, algebra, and the Class 12 CBSE curriculum lies the formalization of relationships between abstract entities. In naive and axiomatic set theory, a relation is not an ambiguous qualitative association; it is a strictly defined, concrete subset of an underlying Cartesian product space.

1.1 The Cartesian Product of Sets

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets in an ambient universal set $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian Product, denoted by $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate $a$ belongs to $A$ and the second coordinate $b$ belongs to $B$. Formally, this is written:

A × B = { (a, b) : a ∈ A ∧ b ∈ B }

The order within the pair $(a, b)$ is critical: $(a, b) = (c, d) \iff (a = c) \land (b = d)$. If $A \neq B$, then generally $A \times B \neq B \times A$. The Cartesian product satisfies the distributive properties over set union and intersection:

  • $A \times (B \cup C) = (A \times B) \cup (A \times C)$
  • $A \times (B \cap C) = (A \times B) \cap (A \times C)$
  • $A \times (B \setminus C) = (A \times B) \setminus (A \times C)$

1.2 Formal Definition of a Binary Relation

A binary relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any arbitrary subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. Symbolically:

R ⊆ A × B

If $(a, b) \in R$, we say that $a$ is related to $b$ under $R$, conventionally abbreviated as $a \, R \, b$. If $(a, b) \notin R$, then $a$ is not related to $b$ under $R$, written $a \cancel{R} b$. When $A = B$, $R \subseteq A \times A$ is explicitly referred to as a binary relation on the set $A$.

1.3 Domain, Range, and Co-domain of a Relation

Let $R \subseteq A \times B$ be a relation. We systematically delineate its fundamental subsets:

  • Domain of $R$ ($\operatorname{Dom}(R)$): The set of all first components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{ a \in A : \exists\, b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R \} \subseteq A$$
  • Range of $R$ ($\operatorname{Ran}(R)$): The set of all second components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{ b \in B : \exists\, a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R \} \subseteq B$$
  • Co-domain of $R$: The entire set $B$, regardless of whether every element of $B$ appears as a second component in an ordered pair within $R$. Thus, $\operatorname{Ran}(R) \subseteq \operatorname{Codom}(R) = B$.

2. Exhaustive Taxonomy of Binary Relations on a Set

In Class 12 CBSE examinations, questions routinely hinge upon the strict identification, verification, and counterexample construction of relations defined over discrete sets or continuous domains (such as $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}$). Let $A$ be a non-empty set and $R \subseteq A \times A$.

2.1 Trivial and Extremal Relations

  • Empty (Void) Relation: The empty set $\emptyset$ is a subset of every Cartesian product: $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $R = \emptyset$ is termed the empty or void relation on $A$. In this relation, no element of $A$ is related to any element of $A$. That is, $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \notin R$.
  • Universal Relation: The full Cartesian product $A \times A$ is trivially a subset of itself: $A \times A \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $R = A \times A$ is called the universal relation on $A$. Under this relation, every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R$.

Both the empty relation and the universal relation on a set $A$ are frequently referred to as trivial relations.

2.2 The Identity Relation vs. The Reflexive Relation

This distinction is one of the most common pitfalls in higher secondary examinations.

  • Identity Relation ($I_A$): A relation on $A$ where every element is related to itself and only itself. $$I_A = \{ (a, a) : a \in A \}$$ If $(a, b) \in I_A$, then it must be that $a = b$. If $a \neq b$, $(a, b)$ can never belong to $I_A$.
  • Reflexive Relation: A relation $R$ on $A$ is reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. $$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$
Rigorous Analytical Distinction:
Every identity relation is reflexive, but not every reflexive relation is an identity relation. In a reflexive relation, the condition $(a, a) \in R$ must hold for all $a \in A$, but additional ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$ are completely permissible. In an identity relation, cross-element pairs $(a, b)$ where $a \neq b$ are strictly prohibited.

Example: Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$.
$R_1 = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3)\}$ is both an identity relation and a reflexive relation.
$R_2 = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2)\}$ is reflexive, but it is not the identity relation because $(1, 2) \in R_2$ with $1 \neq 2$.

2.3 Symmetric Relation

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is called symmetric if and only if the presence of any ordered pair $(a, b)$ guarantees the presence of its reverse $(b, a)$. Formally:

∀ a, b ∈ A, &quad; (a, b) ∈ R &implies; (b, a) ∈ R

In terms of inverse relations: Let $R^{-1} = \{(b, a) : (a, b) \in R\}$. A relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if $R = R^{-1}$.

2.4 Transitive Relation

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is called transitive if, whenever an element $a$ is related to an element $b$, and $b$ is related to an element $c$, it necessarily follows that $a$ is related to $c$. Formally:

∀ a, b, c ∈ A, &quad; [(a, b) ∈ R ∧ (b, c) ∈ R] &implies; (a, c) ∈ R
Crucial Board-Examiner Trap (Vacuous Truth of Transitivity):
Transitivity is an implication: $P \land Q \implies S$, where $P \equiv (a, b) \in R$, $Q \equiv (b, c) \in R$, and $S \equiv (a, c) \in R$. Under standard mathematical logic, an implication $P \land Q \implies S$ is false only if the antecedent $(P \land Q)$ is true while the consequent $S$ is false.

Hence, if there are no pairs $(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ with a shared intermediate element $b$ in $R$, the antecedent is false, and the condition is vacuously satisfied.

Example: Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $R = \{(1, 2)\}$.
Is $R$ transitive? Yes! Here, $(1, 2) \in R$, but there is no pair starting with $2$ (i.e., no $(2, k) \in R$). Thus, the premise $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R$ cannot be constructed, meaning the condition cannot be violated. Therefore, $R$ is vacuously transitive.

2.5 Equivalence Relation

A relation $R$ defined on a non-empty set $A$ is defined to be an equivalence relation if and only if it satisfies all three primary properties simultaneously:

  1. Reflexivity: $(a, a) \in R$ for every $a \in A$.
  2. Symmetry: $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$ for all $a, b \in A$.
  3. Transitivity: $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$ for all $a, b, c \in A$.

3. Equivalence Classes, Partitions, and the Fundamental Theorem

The profound power of an equivalence relation lies in its capacity to partition an arbitrary set into mutually disjoint subsets composed of elements that are indistinguishable with respect to the relation.

3.1 Definition of an Equivalence Class

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Let $a \in A$. The equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$ or $\operatorname{cl}(a)$, is the set of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:

[a] = { x ∈ A : (x, a) ∈ R }

By symmetry, this is identically equal to $\{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

3.2 Foundational Properties of Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$, and let $a, b \in A$. The following properties hold universally:

  • Non-emptiness: For every $a \in A$, $[a] \neq \emptyset$. This follows directly from reflexivity, as $(a, a) \in R \implies a \in [a]$.
  • Equivalence of Classes: $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
  • Disjointness Property: Two equivalence classes are either strictly identical or completely disjoint: $$[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$$ Conversely, if $[a] \neq [b]$, then $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.
Rigorous Proof of the Disjointness Property:
Statement: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$. If $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$, then $[a] = [b]$.
Proof:
  1. Assume $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$.
  2. By definition of equivalence classes: $(x, a) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$.
  3. By symmetry of $R$: $(x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$.
  4. By transitivity of $R$: Since $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, we must have $(a, b) \in R$.
  5. Now, let $y \in [b]$ be an arbitrary element. Then $(y, b) \in R$, which implies $(b, y) \in R$ by symmetry.
  6. Since $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, y) \in R$, by transitivity we obtain $(a, y) \in R$, which implies $(y, a) \in R$, meaning $y \in [a]$. Therefore, $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
  7. By an entirely symmetric argument, if $z \in [a]$, then $(z, a) \in R$. Since $(a, b) \in R$, by transitivity $(z, b) \in R$, meaning $z \in [b]$. Therefore, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
  8. Since $[b] \subseteq [a]$ and $[a] \subseteq [b]$, it follows by mutual inclusion that $[a] = [b]$. $\blacksquare$

3.3 The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. The set of all equivalence classes forms a partition of $A$. That is:

  1. Every equivalence class $[a]$ is a non-empty subset of $A$.
  2. Any two distinct equivalence classes are pairwise disjoint: $[a] \neq [b] \implies [a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.
  3. The union of all equivalence classes reconstructs the entire set $A$: $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$.

Conversely, any partition $\mathcal{P} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ uniquely induces an equivalence relation $R$ on $A$ defined by $(x, y) \in R \iff x \text{ and } y \text{ belong to the same block } A_i$.

3.4 Canonical Application: Congruence Modulo $n$

Let $\mathbb{Z}$ be the set of all integers, and let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ be a fixed positive integer. Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by:

a ≡ b &pmod; n ⇔ n \mid (a - b) ⇔ a - b = k n \quad \text{for some } k ∈ \mathbb{Z}

Verification of Equivalence:

  • Reflexive: For any $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot n$. Thus $n \mid (a - a)$, meaning $a \equiv a \pmod n$.
  • Symmetric: If $a \equiv b \pmod n$, then $a - b = kn$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$). Thus $b - a = -(a - b) = (-k)n$. Since $-k \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mid (b - a)$, so $b \equiv a \pmod n$.
  • Transitive: If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $b \equiv c \pmod n$, then $a - b = k_1 n$ and $b - c = k_2 n$ for $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Summing these yields: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 n + k_2 n \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)n$$ Since $k_1 + k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mid (a - c)$, so $a \equiv c \pmod n$.

The resulting partition divides $\mathbb{Z}$ into exactly $n$ equivalence classes, called residue classes modulo $n$:

$$[0] = \{\dots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \dots\}$$ $$[1] = \{\dots, -2n+1, -n+1, 1, n+1, 2n+1, \dots\}$$ $$\vdots$$ $$[n-1] = \{\dots, -n-1, -1, n-1, 2n-1, 3n-1, \dots\}$$

Notice that $\bigcup_{r=0}^{n-1} [r] = \mathbb{Z}$, and $[i] \cap [j] = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$.

4. Taxonomy of Mappings: Injectivity, Surjectivity, and Bijectivity

In mathematical analysis, a function (or mapping) $f: A \to B$ is a special type of binary relation from $A$ to $B$ satisfying two strict axiomatic conditions:

  1. Totality: Every element $x \in A$ has an assigned image in $B$. That is, $\forall x \in A, \exists y \in B$ such that $(x, y) \in f$.
  2. Uniqueness (Well-definedness): No element $x \in A$ can be associated with more than one element in $B$. That is, $(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f \implies y_1 = y_2$.

We write $y = f(x)$ to indicate $(x, y) \in f$. $A$ is the domain, $B$ is the co-domain, and the range is $f(A) = \{f(x) : x \in A\} \subseteq B$.

4.1 Injective Functions (One-to-One / Injection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is called injective (or one-one) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the co-domain.

∀ x_1, x_2 ∈ A, &quad; f(x_1) = f(x_2) &implies; x_1 = x_2

Equivalently, by contraposition:

∀ x_1, x_2 ∈ A, &quad; x_1 ≠ x_2 &implies; f(x_1) ≠ f(x_2)

A function that fails to be injective is called many-to-one: $\exists x_1, x_2 \in A$ with $x_1 \neq x_2$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.

4.2 Analytical Determination of Injectivity

In CBSE Class 12 board examinations, students are required to rigorously prove or disprove injectivity via two standard methods:

  • Direct Algebraic Method: Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ for arbitrary $x_1, x_2 \in A$. Through rigorous algebraic simplification, deduce whether $x_1 = x_2$ is the unique solution. If any other valid solution appears (e.g., $x_1 = -x_2$ when the domain includes negative numbers), the function is not injective.
  • Calculus Monotonicity Criterion: Let $f$ be continuous on a real interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$.
    If $f'(x) > 0$ strictly for all $x \in (a, b)$ (strictly increasing), or $f'(x) < 0$ strictly for all $x \in (a, b)$ (strictly decreasing), then $f$ is strictly monotonic on $(a, b)$, and hence $f$ is strictly injective.
    If $f'(x)$ changes sign across the interval, there exist points where the tangent is horizontal ($f'(c) = 0$), generally indicating local extrema and implying many-to-one behavior.

4.3 Surjective Functions (Onto / Surjection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is called surjective (or onto) if every element of the co-domain $B$ is the image of at least one element of the domain $A$.

∀ y ∈ B, &quad; ∃ x ∈ A \quad \text{such that} \quad f(x) = y

In set-theoretic terms:

f \text{ is surjective} \iff \operatorname{Ran}(f) = \operatorname{Codom}(f) = B

A function that fails to be surjective is called into: $\exists y \in B$ such that $\forall x \in A$, $f(x) \neq y$.

4.4 Analytical Determination of Surjectivity

To prove surjectivity in standard board problems:

  1. Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element of the specified co-domain.
  2. Set up the equation $y = f(x)$ and algebraically solve for $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
  3. Inspect whether $x = g(y)$ exists and lies within the assigned domain $A$ for all $y \in B$.
  4. If $g(y) \in A$ for every $y \in B$, and $f(g(y)) = y$, surjectivity is proven. If there exists even a single $y \in B$ for which $g(y) \notin A$ or where $g(y)$ is undefined, surjectivity fails.

4.5 Bijective Functions (One-to-One and Onto / Bijection)

A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be bijective if it is simultaneously injective and surjective. A bijection creates a perfect one-to-one correspondence between the elements of $A$ and the elements of $B$.

Type of Mapping Defining Formal Condition Range vs. Co-domain Horizontal Line Test Property
One-to-One (Injective) $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$ $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq B$ Every horizontal line intersects the graph at most once.
Many-to-One $\exists x_1 \neq x_2$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq B$ At least one horizontal line intersects the graph more than once.
Onto (Surjective) $\forall y \in B, \, \exists x \in A : f(x) = y$ $\operatorname{Ran}(f) = B$ Every line $y = k$ ($k \in B$) intersects the graph at least once.
Into $\exists y \in B \text{ such that } y \notin \operatorname{Ran}(f)$ $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subsetneq B$ There exists at least one line $y = k$ ($k \in B$) having no intersection.
Bijective Injective AND Surjective $\operatorname{Ran}(f) = B$ Every line $y = k$ ($k \in B$) intersects the graph exactly once.

5. Rigorous Combinatorial Enumeration of Relations and Functions

Understanding the quantitative bounds on mappings and relations is an indispensable component of the advanced curriculum. Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

5.1 Counting Relations on a Finite Set

Let $|A| = n$. The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $|A \times A| = n^2$ ordered pairs.

  • Total Number of Binary Relations: Any relation is a subset of $A \times A$. The power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$ contains: $$N_{\text{total relations}} = 2^{n^2}$$
  • Number of Reflexive Relations: An ordered pair matrix representation has $n$ diagonal elements $(a_i, a_i)$ and $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal elements $(a_i, a_j)$ with $i \neq j$. For a reflexive relation, all $n$ diagonal pairs must be included (1 choice each). The remaining $n(n - 1)$ pairs can either be included or excluded independently ($2$ choices each). $$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \times 2^{n(n - 1)} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$
  • Number of Symmetric Relations: The $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ can be chosen freely ($2^n$ combinations). The $(n^2 - n)$ non-diagonal elements pair up into $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ symmetric pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$. For symmetry, both must be present or both absent (2 choices per pair). $$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$
  • Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations: All $n$ diagonal pairs are mandatory ($1$ choice). The $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ symmetric off-diagonal pairs each have $2$ choices. $$N_{\text{refl} \cap \text{symm}} = 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$$

5.2 Counting Functions and Mappings

Let $|A| = m$ (domain) and $|B| = n$ (co-domain).

  • Total Number of Functions: Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ has $n$ independent choices in $B$: $$N_{\text{functions}} = n^m$$
  • Total Number of Injective Functions (One-One):
    • If $m > n$: By the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two distinct elements of $A$ must map to the same element of $B$. Thus, $N_{\text{injections}} = 0$.
    • If $m \le n$: The first element has $n$ choices, the second has $n - 1$, and the $m$-th has $n - m + 1$: $$N_{\text{injections}} = P(n, m) = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}$$
  • Total Number of Bijective Functions: A bijection requires $|A| = |B|$, so $m = n$. $$N_{\text{bijections}} = n! \quad (\text{if } m = n), \quad 0 \quad (\text{if } m \neq n)$$
  • Total Number of Surjective Functions (Onto): Using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE):
    • If $m < n$: By definition, the range can have at most $m$ elements, which is strictly less than $n$. Hence, $N_{\text{surjections}} = 0$.
    • If $m \ge n$: The number of onto functions from a set of size $m$ to a set of size $n$ is: $$N_{\text{surjections}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} (n - r)^m = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$ where $S(m, n)$ denotes the Stirling numbers of the second kind.
Concrete Evaluation of Surjections for $m = 4, n = 3$:
$$N_{\text{surjections}} = \binom{3}{0}(3)^4 - \binom{3}{1}(2)^4 + \binom{3}{2}(1)^4 - \binom{3}{3}(0)^4$$ $$= 1(81) - 3(16) + 3(1) - 0 = 81 - 48 + 3 = 36$$

6. Composition of Mappings and Formal Theory of Invertibility

6.1 Composition of Functions

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions. The composite function, denoted $g \circ f$ (read as “$g$ circle $f$” or “$g$ composed with $f$”), is a function mapping $A$ directly into $C$:

(g ∘ f): A &to; C, &quad; (g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)) \quad \forall x ∈ A
Domain-Range Existence Criterion for $g \circ f$:
The composite function $g \circ f$ is well-defined if and only if the range of the inner function $f$ is a subset of the domain of the outer function $g$: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$$ If this condition is violated, $g(f(x))$ is undefined for those $x \in A$ whose image $f(x) \notin \operatorname{Dom}(g)$.

6.2 Algebraic Properties of Function Composition

  • Non-Commutativity: Composition is generally non-commutative. Even when both $f \circ g$ and $g \circ f$ exist and have identical domains and co-domains ($A = B = C$): $$f \circ g \neq g \circ f \quad (\text{in general})$$
  • Associativity: Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$. Composition is strictly associative: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$
  • Preservation Properties:
    1. If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
    2. If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
    3. If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are both bijective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is bijective.
  • Deductions from Composite Behavior (Board Favorites):
    1. If $g \circ f$ is injective, then the inner function $f$ must be injective. (The outer function $g$ may or may not be injective).
    2. If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then the outer function $g$ must be surjective. (The inner function $f$ may or may not be surjective).
Proof that $g \circ f$ Injective $\implies f$ Injective:
Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
Apply $g$ to both sides: $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$.
By definition of composition: $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
Since $g \circ f$ is given to be injective, it immediately follows that $x_1 = x_2$.
Hence, $f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

6.3 The Theory of Invertibility

A function $f: A \to B$ is defined to be invertible if there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that:

g ∘ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f ∘ g = I_B

where $I_A: A \to A$ ($I_A(x) = x, \forall x \in A$) and $I_B: B \to B$ ($I_B(y) = y, \forall y \in B$) are the identity mappings on $A$ and $B$, respectively.
The function $g$ is called the inverse of $f$ and is uniquely denoted by $f^{-1}$.

Fundamental Theorem of Invertibility:
Theorem: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijective function (both one-to-one and onto).

Proof Part 1 ($\implies$): Invertibility implies Bijectivity:
  1. Suppose $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.
  2. Since $I_A$ is injective, $g \circ f$ is injective. As proven earlier, this implies that $f$ is injective.
  3. Since $I_B$ is surjective, $f \circ g$ is surjective. This implies that the outer function $f$ is surjective.
  4. Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.
Proof Part 2 ($\impliedby$): Bijectivity implies Invertibility:
  1. Suppose $f$ is bijective. Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  2. Since $f$ is injective, this $x$ is unique. We can therefore unambiguously define a function $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element such that $f(x) = y$.
  3. Then $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x$ for all $x \in A$, so $g \circ f = I_A$.
  4. Similarly, $(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y$ for all $y \in B$, so $f \circ g = I_B$.
  5. Therefore, $g = f^{-1}$ exists, and $f$ is invertible. $\blacksquare$

6.4 Reversal Law of Composition Inversion

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also invertible, and its inverse is given by the reversal law:

(g ∘ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} ∘ g^{-1}
Proof: We must show that $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$ and $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$.
  1. $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f))$ (Associativity)
  2. $= f^{-1} \circ ((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$.
  3. Similarly: $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$.
  4. By uniqueness of the inverse, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

7. Binary Operations and Elementary Algebraic Structures

A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a calculation that takes two elements from $A$ and combines them to produce another element of $A$. Rigorously, it is a function mapping the Cartesian product $A \times A$ to $A$:

* : A × A &to; A, \quad \text{denoted by } *(a, b) = a * b \in A

Because a binary operation is a function with co-domain $A$, the condition that $a * b \in A$ for all $a, b \in A$ is built directly into the definition. This property is known as closure.

7.1 Core Algebraic Axioms of Binary Operations

  • Commutativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is commutative if: $$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$
  • Associativity: An operation $*$ on $A$ is associative if: $$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$ Associativity allows us to write expressions like $a * b * c$ unambiguously without parentheses.
  • Existence of Identity Element: An element $e \in A$ is an identity element with respect to $*$ if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a$$ Theorem: If an identity element exists for a binary operation, it is unique.
  • Existence of an Invertible Element: Let $e \in A$ be the identity with respect to $*$. An element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that: $$a * b = b * a = e$$ The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$ and is denoted $a^{-1}$.
    Theorem: If an element $a \in A$ has an inverse under an associative operation $*$, then this inverse is unique.
Proof of Uniqueness of the Identity Element:
Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identity elements in $A$ with respect to $*$.
  1. Since $e_1$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_2$.
  2. Since $e_2$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$.
  3. Therefore: $e_1 = e_2$. The identity element is unique. $\blacksquare$

8. Graphical Analysis and Geometric Transformations

8.1 Graphical Verification Tests

  • Vertical Line Test (Functionality): A curve in the Cartesian plane represents a function $y = f(x)$ if and only if every vertical line $x = x_0$ (where $x_0 \in \operatorname{Dom}(f)$) intersects the curve at exactly one point. If any vertical line intersects the curve more than once, a single input generates multiple outputs, violating the definition of a function.
  • Horizontal Line Test (Injectivity): A function $y = f(x)$ is injective (one-one) if and only if every horizontal line $y = y_0$ intersects the graph of $f$ at at most one point. An intersection at two or more points implies $f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y_0$ for $x_1 \neq x_2$, confirming the function is many-to-one.

8.2 Geometric Symmetry of Inverse Functions

If a function $f: A \to B$ is bijective, its inverse $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is defined by $f^{-1}(y) = x \iff f(x) = y$. In coordinate geometry, the point $(a, b)$ lies on the graph of $y = f(x)$ if and only if the transposed point $(b, a)$ lies on the graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$.

Geometrically, the transformation $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$ corresponds to an orthogonal reflection across the line $y = x$.

Fundamental Geometric Invariant: The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the mirror image of the graph of $y = f(x)$ reflected across the principal line of symmetry $y = x$. Any point of intersection between $y = f(x)$ and $y = f^{-1}(x)$ for an increasing function must lie along the identity line $y = x$.

9. Advanced Analytical Exemplars & CBSE Examination Cases

Exemplar 9.1: Rational Bijective Verification on Restricted Domains

Let $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. Consider the function: $$f: A \to B, \quad f(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$ Prove that $f$ is bijective, and systematically derive its inverse function $f^{-1}$.

Step 1: Rigorous Injectivity Proof:
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\frac{x_1 - 2}{x_1 - 3} = \frac{x_2 - 2}{x_2 - 3}$$ Cross-multiplying (valid since $x_1, x_2 \neq 3$): $$(x_1 - 2)(x_2 - 3) = (x_2 - 2)(x_1 - 3)$$ $$x_1 x_2 - 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 3x_2 - 2x_1 + 6$$ Subtracting the common terms $(x_1 x_2 + 6)$ from both sides: $$-3x_1 - 2x_2 = -3x_2 - 2x_1$$ $$-3x_1 + 2x_1 = -3x_2 + 2x_2 \implies -x_1 = -x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Since $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, $f$ is strictly injective (one-one).

Step 2: Rigorous Surjectivity Proof:
Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ be arbitrary. We must determine whether there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$: $$y = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3} \implies y(x - 3) = x - 2 \implies y x - 3y = x - 2$$ Rearranging to isolate $x$: $$y x - x = 3y - 2 \implies x(y - 1) = 3y - 2 \implies x = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$ Because $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, the denominator $y - 1 \neq 0$, so $x$ is well-defined. Now check whether $x \in A$ (that is, $x \neq 3$): Assume for contradiction that $x = 3$: $$\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} = 3 \implies 3y - 2 = 3(y - 1) \implies 3y - 2 = 3y - 3 \implies -2 = -3$$ This is an absurdity (contradiction). Hence, $x \neq 3$, meaning $x \in A$.
Check image: $$f(x) = f\left(\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}\right) = \frac{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 2}{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 3} = \frac{(3y - 2) - 2(y - 1)}{(3y - 2) - 3(y - 1)} = \frac{3y - 2 - 2y + 2}{3y - 2 - 3y + 3} = \frac{y}{1} = y$$ Thus, for every $y \in B$, there exists $x = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Therefore, $f$ is surjective (onto).

Conclusion & Inverse: Because $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore invertible. The inverse function is: $$f^{-1}: B \to A, \quad f^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x - 2}{x - 1}$$

Exemplar 9.2: Analysis of Equivalence on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$

Let $A = \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. Define a relation $R$ on $A$ by: $$(a, b) \, R \, (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$$ Prove that $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.

Proof:
  1. Reflexivity: Let $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ be arbitrary.
    Addition in $\mathbb{N}$ is commutative, so $a + b = b + a$.
    By definition of the relation, this means $(a, b) \, R \, (a, b)$.
    Hence, $R$ is reflexive on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.
  2. Symmetry: Let $(a, b), (c, d) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$. $$a + d = b + c \implies b + c = a + d \implies c + b = d + a$$ By definition of $R$, this implies $(c, d) \, R \, (a, b)$.
    Hence, $R$ is symmetric.
  3. Transitivity: Let $(a, b), (c, d), (e, f) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$ and $(c, d) \, R \, (e, f)$.
    From $(a, b) \, R \, (c, d)$, we have: $$a + d = b + c \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$ From $(c, d) \, R \, (e, f)$, we have: $$c + f = d + e \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$ Adding Equation 1 and Equation 2: $$(a + d) + (c + f) = (b + c) + (d + e)$$ Using associativity and commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{N}$: $$(a + f) + (c + d) = (b + e) + (c + d)$$ Subtracting the quantity $(c + d)$ from both sides: $$a + f = b + e$$ By definition of the relation, this implies: $$(a, b) \, R \, (e, f)$$ Hence, $R$ is transitive.
Since $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$. $\blacksquare$

10. Examiner's Analytical Compass: Traps & Common Pitfalls

  • The "Empty Set" Relation Pitfall: Consider $R = \emptyset$ defined on a non-empty set $A \neq \emptyset$. Is $R$ reflexive? No, because $(a, a) \notin \emptyset$. Is $R$ symmetric? Yes, because $(a, b) \in \emptyset \implies (b, a) \in \emptyset$ is vacuously true. Is $R$ transitive? Yes, also vacuously true. Thus, the empty relation on a non-empty set is symmetric and transitive, but never reflexive.
  • Codomain vs. Range Discrepancy: In CBSE examinations, functions are often presented with real numbers as the co-domain, such as $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f(x) = x^2$. Students frequently write “onto because every positive number has a square root.” This is an incomplete response: the negative reals in the co-domain have no pre-images in $\mathbb{R}$! Always explicitly verify that the entire given co-domain equals the range.
  • Failure of Commutativity in Verification: When testing relations like $a \, R \, b \iff a \le b^2$, never assume symmetry holds. For instance, $(2, 5) \in R$ because $2 \le 25$, but $(5, 2) \notin R$ because $5 \not\le 4$. Testing a single numerical counterexample is sufficient to disprove a property, but proving it requires a general algebraic proof.
  • Strict Inequality Counterexamples in Transitivity: For the relation $R = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} : a \le b^3\}$, transitivity often fails when fractional numbers and negative values are introduced. Consider $a = 2$, $b = 1.5$, $c = 1.2$: $$2 \le (1.5)^3 = 3.375 \quad \text{True} \implies (2, 1.5) \in R$$ $$1.5 \le (1.2)^3 = 1.728 \quad \text{True} \implies (1.5, 1.2) \in R$$ Now test $(a, c)$: $$2 \le (1.2)^3 = 1.728 \quad \text{FALSE} \implies (2, 1.2) \notin R$$ Hence, transitivity fails.

Derivation 1: Combinatorics of Relation Subsets over a Finite Set

Proposition: Let $A$ be a finite set with $n(A) = n$. Prove that:

  1. The total number of distinct relations is $2^{n^2}$.
  2. The number of reflexive relations is $2^{n(n - 1)}$.
  3. The number of symmetric relations is $2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$.
  4. The number of reflexive and symmetric relations is $2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$.

Step 1: Total Relations

A relation $R$ on $A$ is defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$:

$$n(A \times A) = n(A) \cdot n(A) = n \cdot n = n^2$$

The total number of relations is the cardinality of the power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$:

$$N_R = |\mathcal{P}(A \times A)| = 2^{n(A \times A)} = 2^{n^2}$$

Step 2: Reflexive Relations

The Cartesian product $A \times A$ can be partitioned into diagonal elements $\Delta$ and non-diagonal elements $\Delta^c$:

$$\Delta = \{(a_i, a_i) : a_i \in A\}, \quad |\Delta| = n$$ $$\Delta^c = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j, \; a_i, a_j \in A\}, \quad |\Delta^c| = n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$$

For $R$ to be reflexive, every diagonal element must be an element of $R$:

$$\forall a_i \in A, \quad (a_i, a_i) \in R \implies \Delta \subseteq R$$

Thus, diagonal pairs have only $1$ choice (must be present). Each pair in $\Delta^c$ can be either included or excluded ($2$ choices):

$$N_{\text{refl}} = \prod_{k=1}^{n} 1 \times \prod_{m=1}^{n(n-1)} 2 = 1^n \cdot 2^{n(n - 1)} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

Step 3: Symmetric Relations

For symmetry, $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$. Consider the $n \times n$ matrix grid of pairs:

  • Diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$: There are $n$ such pairs. Each can be included or excluded independently ($2^n$ choices).
  • Unordered off-diagonal pairs $\{ (a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i) \}$ with $i < j$: The total number of such unordered pairs is: $$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$$

For each unordered pair, either both $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ are in $R$, or neither is in $R$ ($2$ choices per unordered pair). Hence:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

Step 4: Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

If $R$ is both reflexive and symmetric, then all $n$ diagonal pairs must be present ($1$ choice each), while the off-diagonal pairs must still be chosen in symmetric pairs:

$$N_{\text{refl, sym}} = 1^n \cdot 2^{\binom{n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any $a, b \in A$, the equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either identical or mutually disjoint. That is:

$$[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$$

Proof:

Step 1: Assume non-empty intersection.
Suppose $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists an element $x \in A$ such that:

$$x \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad x \in [b]$$

By the definition of an equivalence class $[y] = \{z \in A : (z, y) \in R\}$:

$$(x, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (x, b) \in R$$

Step 2: Utilize Symmetry and Transitivity.
Since $R$ is an equivalence relation, it is symmetric:

$$(x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$$

Now apply transitivity to $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$:

$$\big((a, x) \in R \land (x, b) \in R\big) \implies (a, b) \in R$$

Step 3: Establish the subset relation $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
Let $y$ be an arbitrary element of $[a]$. By definition:

$$y \in [a] \implies (y, a) \in R$$

Applying transitivity with $(y, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$:

$$\big((y, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R\big) \implies (y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$$

Since every element of $[a]$ is in $[b]$, we obtain:

$$[a] \subseteq [b]$$

Step 4: Establish the reverse subset relation $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
Because $(a, b) \in R$, symmetry gives $(b, a) \in R$. Let $w \in [b]$. Then:

$$w \in [b] \implies (w, b) \in R$$

By transitivity with $(w, b) \in R$ and $(b, a) \in R$:

$$\big((w, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R\big) \implies (w, a) \in R \implies w \in [a]$$

Thus:

$$[b] \subseteq [a]$$

Step 5: Mutual Inclusion Conclusion.
By double set inclusion:

$$([a] \subseteq [b] \land [b] \subseteq [a]) \implies [a] = [b]$$

Therefore, either $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ or $[a] = [b]$. Consequently, the set of all equivalence classes forms a partition of $A$:

$$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A \quad \text{and} \quad [a] \neq [b] \implies [a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: Associativity of Function Composition

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$ be functions. Prove that composition of functions is associative:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$

Proof:

Step 1: Verification of Domains and Codomains.

First examine the left-hand composite mapping:

$$g \circ f: A \to C \implies h \circ (g \circ f): A \to D$$

Next examine the right-hand composite mapping:

$$h \circ g: B \to D \implies (h \circ g) \circ f: A \to D$$

Both mappings share the identical domain $A$ and codomain $D$.

Step 2: Pointwise Evaluation.
Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $A$. By the fundamental definition of function composition $(u \circ v)(t) = u(v(t))$:

$$\big(h \circ (g \circ f)\big)(x) = h\big((g \circ f)(x)\big)$$

Evaluating the inner composition:

$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$$

Substituting into the expression:

$$\big(h \circ (g \circ f)\big)(x) = h\big(g(f(x))\big) \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

Now evaluate the right-hand expression at $x$:

$$\big((h \circ g) \circ f\big)(x) = (h \circ g)\big(f(x)\big)$$

Let $y = f(x) \in B$. By definition of $h \circ g$:

$$(h \circ g)(y) = h(g(y)) \implies (h \circ g)\big(f(x)\big) = h\big(g(f(x))\big) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Step 3: Identity of Mappings.
Comparing Equation 1 and Equation 2:

$$\big(h \circ (g \circ f)\big)(x) = \big((h \circ g) \circ f\big)(x), \quad \forall x \in A$$

Since both functions have identical domains, identical codomains, and agree at every point in their domain:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Uniqueness and Reversal Law of Function Inverses

Part A: Uniqueness of Inverse Mapping

Theorem: If a function $f: X \to Y$ is invertible, its inverse is uniquely determined.

Proof:
Suppose $g_1: Y \to X$ and $g_2: Y \to X$ are both inverses of $f$. Then by definition of inverse functions:

$$f \circ g_1 = I_Y, \quad g_1 \circ f = I_X$$ $$f \circ g_2 = I_Y, \quad g_2 \circ f = I_X$$

Consider the composition $g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2)$:

$$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = g_1 \circ I_Y = g_1$$

By associativity of composition (Derivation 3):

$$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2$$

Since $g_1 \circ f = I_X$:

$$(g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_X \circ g_2 = g_2$$

Equating the results:

$$g_1 = g_2$$

Hence, the inverse function is unique, denoted unambiguously as $f^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

Part B: The Reverse Order Law $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$

Theorem: Let $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$ be invertible functions. Then $g \circ f: X \to Z$ is invertible, and:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Proof:
To prove that an arbitrary mapping $T: Z \to X$ is the inverse of $g \circ f$, it is necessary and sufficient to demonstrate:

$$(g \circ f) \circ T = I_Z \quad \text{and} \quad T \circ (g \circ f) = I_X$$

Let $T = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}: Z \to X$. We verify both conditions:

Condition 1: Right Composition

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ \big(f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})\big)$$

Applying associativity to the sub-expression:

$$f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}$$

Since $f \circ f^{-1} = I_Y$:

$$(f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = I_Y \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}$$

Substituting this back into the primary expression:

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ g^{-1} = I_Z$$

Condition 2: Left Composition

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ \big(g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)\big)$$

Applying associativity to the inner term:

$$g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f) = (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = I_Y \circ f = f$$

Substituting back:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ f = I_X$$

Since both composition conditions are satisfied, by uniqueness of the inverse:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Analytical Invertibility Criterion ($f \text{ is Invertible} \iff f \text{ is Bijective}$)

Theorem: A function $f: X \to Y$ has an inverse $f^{-1}: Y \to X$ if and only if $f$ is bijective (both injective and surjective).

Part 1: Invertible $\implies$ Bijective

Given: There exists $g: Y \to X$ such that $g \circ f = I_X$ and $f \circ g = I_Y$.

Proof of Injectivity: Let $x_1, x_2 \in X$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$ to both sides:

$$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$

Because $g \circ f = I_X$:

$$I_X(x_1) = I_X(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Thus, $f$ is injective (one-one).

Proof of Surjectivity: Let $y$ be any element in codomain $Y$. Since $f \circ g = I_Y$:

$$(f \circ g)(y) = I_Y(y) = y \implies f(g(y)) = y$$

Let $x = g(y)$. Since $g: Y \to X$, $x \in X$. Thus, for every $y \in Y$, there exists $x = g(y) \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$. Hence, $f$ is surjective (onto).

Therefore, $f$ is bijective.

Part 2: Bijective $\implies$ Invertible

Given: $f: X \to Y$ is one-one and onto.

We construct a mapping $g: Y \to X$ as follows: Let $y \in Y$. Because $f$ is onto, there exists $x \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$. Because $f$ is one-one, this element $x$ is strictly unique.

We can therefore define the rule $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique preimage satisfying $f(x) = y$.

Evaluating $g \circ f$: For any $x \in X$, let $y = f(x)$. By definition of $g$:

$$g(y) = x \implies g(f(x)) = x \implies (g \circ f)(x) = I_X(x), \quad \forall x \in X \implies g \circ f = I_X$$

Evaluating $f \circ g$: For any $y \in Y$, let $x = g(y)$, where $f(x) = y$. Then:

$$f(g(y)) = f(x) = y \implies (f \circ g)(y) = I_Y(y), \quad \forall y \in Y \implies f \circ g = I_Y$$

Since $g \circ f = I_X$ and $f \circ g = I_Y$, $g$ is the inverse of $f$, making $f$ invertible.

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 6: Uniqueness of Identity and Inverse Elements in Binary Operations

Theorem: Let $*$ be a binary operation defined on a set $S$. Prove that:

  1. If an identity element exists, it is unique.
  2. If $*$ is associative and an element $a \in S$ possesses an inverse, it is unique.

Part 1: Uniqueness of the Identity Element

Let $e_1, e_2 \in S$ both be identity elements for the binary operation $*$.

By definition of an identity element, for any $x \in S$:

$$e * x = x * e = x$$

Considering $e_1$ as an identity and $e_2 \in S$ as a set element:

$$e_1 * e_2 = e_2 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

Now considering $e_2$ as an identity and $e_1 \in S$ as a set element:

$$e_1 * e_2 = e_1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Equating Equation 1 and Equation 2:

$$e_1 = e_2$$

Hence, the identity element is unique. $\blacksquare$

Part 2: Uniqueness of the Inverse Element

Let $*$ be associative: $(x * y) * z = x * (y * z)$ for all $x, y, z \in S$, and let $e \in S$ be the unique identity.

Let $a \in S$ be an invertible element. Suppose $b_1, b_2 \in S$ are both inverses of $a$. By definition of inverse elements:

$$a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e$$ $$a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$$

Examine the product $b_1 * (a * b_2)$:

$$b_1 * (a * b_2) = b_1 * e = b_1$$

By associativity of the binary operation $*$, we rebracket the operation:

$$b_1 * (a * b_2) = (b_1 * a) * b_2$$

Substitute $b_1 * a = e$ into this rebracketed form:

$$(b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$

Comparing the two evaluated expressions:

$$b_1 = b_2$$

Thus, every invertible element $a \in S$ has a uniquely determined inverse $a^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$
