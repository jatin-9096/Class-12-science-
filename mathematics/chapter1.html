Comprehensive Theory

1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Set Theory & Cartesian Spaces

The architecture of modern mathematical analysis, algebra, and discrete systems rests upon set theory. In higher secondary mathematics, relations and functions serve as the foundational language through which mappings, transformations, calculus, and abstract algebraic structures are formalized. To establish rigorous mechanics for relations, we begin with the Cartesian product of arbitrary sets.

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets. The Cartesian product of $A$ and $B$, denoted by $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that $a \in A$ and $b \in B$. Formally, this is written as:

$$A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$

An ordered pair $(a, b)$ is a collection of two elements where the order of appearance is paramount. By Kuratowski's definition of an ordered pair, $(a, b) = \{\{a\}, \{a, b\}\}$. Hence, $(a, b) = (c, d)$ if and only if $a = c$ and $b = d$. This strict ordering distinguishes the Cartesian product from standard unordered sets; generally, if $A \neq B$, then $A \times B \neq B \times A$.

If the cardinality of set $A$ is denoted by $|A| = n(A) = m$ and the cardinality of set $B$ is $|B| = n(B) = n$, where $m, n \in \mathbb{N}_0$, the fundamental counting principle asserts that the total number of ordered pairs in the Cartesian product is:

$$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n$$

When a Cartesian product is formed on a set with itself, denoted $A \times A$ or $A^2$, it forms the universal relational space for that set. If $|A| = n$, then $|A \times A| = n^2$.

2. Mathematical Formulation of a Relation

A relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any arbitrary subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. Symbolically:

$$R \subseteq A \times B \iff R \in \mathcal{P}(A \times B)$$

where $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ denotes the power set of $A \times B$. If an ordered pair $(a, b) \in R$, we state that “$a$ is related to $b$ under $R$”, written conventionally in infix notation as $a \, R \, b$. Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, then $a$ is not related to $b$, denoted $a \!\not R\, b$.

Since $R$ is a subset of $A \times B$, the total number of distinct relations that can be defined from set $A$ to set $B$ corresponds to the total number of subsets of $A \times B$, which is the cardinality of the power set:

$$N_{\text{relations}} = |\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{|A \times B|} = 2^{mn}$$

Specifically, for a relation defined on a single set $A$ (i.e., a homogeneous binary relation where $R \subseteq A \times A$), if $|A| = n$, the total number of distinct relations is:

$$N_{\text{relations on } A} = 2^{n^2}$$

Domain, Codomain, and Range

Every relation $R \subseteq A \times B$ possesses three intrinsically bound structural domains:

  • Domain of $R$ ($\operatorname{Dom}(R)$): The set of all initial components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a \in A : \exists b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq A$$
  • Codomain of $R$: The entire designated target set $B$, irrespective of whether every element in $B$ participates in the relation. $$\operatorname{Codom}(R) = B$$
  • Range of $R$ ($\operatorname{Ran}(R)$): The set of all second components of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$. $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{b \in B : \exists a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq B$$

Inverse of a Relation

Given any relation $R \subseteq A \times B$, the inverse relation, denoted $R^{-1} \subseteq B \times A$, is obtained by reversing the elements of each ordered pair in $R$:

$$R^{-1} = \{(b, a) \in B \times A : (a, b) \in R\}$$

Directly from this formulation, it follows that $\operatorname{Dom}(R^{-1}) = \operatorname{Ran}(R)$ and $\operatorname{Ran}(R^{-1}) = \operatorname{Dom}(R)$. Furthermore, $(R^{-1})^{-1} = R$.

3. Rigorous Taxonomy of Binary Relations on a Set $A$

Binary relations on a set $A$ are categorized according to whether they satisfy fundamental structural axioms. The principal classifications evaluated under the CBSE Class 12 Advanced curriculum include the following:

3.1 Trivial and Extremal Relations

  • Empty Relation (Void Relation): A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is empty if no element of $A$ is related to any element of $A$. That is, $R = \emptyset \subset A \times A$. The empty relation represents the minimal element in the lattice of relations under inclusion.
  • Universal Relation: A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is universal if every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$. That is, $R = A \times A$. The universal relation represents the maximal element under inclusion.

Both the empty relation and the universal relation are often referred to as trivial relations.

3.2 The Identity Relation vs. Reflexive Relation

A common point of conceptual confusion in board examinations is the distinction between the identity relation and a reflexive relation.

The Identity Relation ($I_A$): A relation $I_A$ on set $A$ is defined as:

$$I_A = \{(a, a) : \forall a \in A\}$$

In the identity relation, every element of $A$ must be related to itself, and only to itself. If any extraneous pair $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$ belongs to $I_A$, the relation ceases to be an identity relation.

Reflexive Relation: A relation $R$ on set $A$ is reflexive if:

$$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$

In a reflexive relation, the condition $(a, a) \in R$ is mandatory for all $a \in A$. However, unlike the identity relation, additional pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$ are permissible. Therefore, every identity relation is reflexive, but not every reflexive relation is an identity relation:

$$I_A \subseteq R_{\text{reflexive}} \subseteq A \times A$$

3.3 Symmetric Relation

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is called symmetric if and only if:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$$

In algebraic terms, a relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if it is equal to its own inverse:

$$R = R^{-1}$$

Examiner's Note on Implication: The logical condition $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$ is conditional. If an element pair $(x, y)$ is not present in $R$, there is no requirement for $(y, x)$ to be in $R$. Thus, the empty relation $\emptyset$ on a non-empty set is vacuously symmetric because the hypothesis $(a, b) \in \emptyset$ is false for all $a, b$.

3.4 Transitive Relation

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is called transitive if and only if:

$$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad \Big((a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R\Big) \implies (a, c) \in R$$

The Vacuous Transitivity Fallacy: Students frequently stumble when verifying transitivity in sparse relations. Consider a relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ on $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Is $R$ transitive? To violate transitivity, one must find an instance where $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, but $(a, c) \notin R$. In this example, there is no pair $(2, c) \in R$. Because the premise $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R$ cannot be satisfied for any chain, the implication evaluates to true vacuously. Hence, $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ is strictly transitive.

3.5 Antisymmetric Relation

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is defined as antisymmetric if:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad \Big((a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R\Big) \implies a = b$$

Equivalently, if $a \neq b$, then $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ cannot both belong to $R$. Antisymmetry is not the mere opposite of symmetry; a relation can be both symmetric and antisymmetric (e.g., the identity relation $I_A$), or neither symmetric nor antisymmetric.

4. Equivalence Relations, Equivalence Classes & Partitions

Definition of an Equivalence Relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is an equivalence relation if and only if it satisfies all three core axioms simultaneously:

  1. Reflexivity: $\forall a \in A, \, (a, a) \in R$.
  2. Symmetry: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$.
  3. Transitivity: $\forall a, b, c \in A, \, \big((a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R\big) \implies (a, c) \in R$.

Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any element $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$, $cl(a)$, or $\bar{a}$, is the set of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:

$$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\} = \{x \in A : x \, R \, a\}$$

Fundamental Properties of Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$. Then:

  • Non-emptiness: Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in A$. Therefore, $a \in [a]$, guaranteeing that no equivalence class is empty: $[a] \neq \emptyset$.
  • Equality of Classes: For any $a, b \in A$, $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
  • Disjointness: For any $a, b \in A$, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. Two equivalence classes are either identical or completely disjoint.
Formal Proof of the Disjointness Property:
Let $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. We must prove that $[a] = [b]$.
Since $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$, there exists an element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$.
By definition of equivalence classes: $$(x, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (x, b) \in R$$ Applying symmetry to the first pair gives $(a, x) \in R$.
Now, by transitivity, since $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, we deduce: $$(a, b) \in R$$ Now let $y \in [a]$ be an arbitrary element. Then $(y, a) \in R$.
Since $(y, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$, by transitivity we obtain $(y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$. Hence, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
By an identical line of reasoning starting with $z \in [b]$, we find $(z, b) \in R$. Since $(b, a) \in R$ (by symmetry of $(a, b) \in R$), transitivity gives $(z, a) \in R \implies z \in [a]$, so $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
By mutual subset inclusion, $[a] = [b]$.
Consequently, if $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$, then $[a] = [b]$. By contraposition, if $[a] \neq [b]$, then $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. $\blacksquare$

The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Set Partition)

A partition $\mathcal{P}$ of a set $A$ is a collection of non-empty, pairwise disjoint subsets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ whose union is the entire set $A$:

$$\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = A \quad \text{and} \quad A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \quad \forall i \neq j$$

Theorem: An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ induces a partition on $A$, where the cells of the partition are the equivalence classes of $R$. Conversely, any partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $A$ uniquely defines an equivalence relation $R$ on $A$ for which the elements of $\mathcal{P}$ are the equivalence classes.

The set of all equivalence classes is called the quotient set of $A$ by $R$, denoted by:

$$A / R = \{[a] : a \in A\}$$

5. Combinatorics of Relations on Finite Sets

Let $A$ be a finite set such that $|A| = n$. The total number of pairs in $A \times A$ is $n^2$. We can represent any relation $R \subseteq A \times A$ as an $n \times n$ Boolean adjacency matrix $M_R = [m_{ij}]$, where:

$$m_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } (a_i, a_j) \in R \\ 0 & \text{if } (a_i, a_j) \notin R \end{cases}$$

The matrix contains $n$ elements along its primary diagonal ($m_{ii}$ where $i = j$) and $n^2 - n$ off-diagonal elements divided symmetrically into two triangles of size $\frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$ pairs each.

Relation Property Matrix / Structural Condition Total Count Formula ($|A| = n$)
Total Relations Arbitrary selection of any cells $2^{n^2}$
Reflexive All $n$ diagonal entries fixed to 1; off-diagonals arbitrary $2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n-1)}$
Symmetric $m_{ij} = m_{ji}$. Choose $n$ diagonal cells and $\binom{n}{2}$ independent off-diagonal pairs $2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$
Reflexive & Symmetric Diagonal fixed to 1; choose from $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs $2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$
Anti-symmetric Diagonal arbitrary ($2^n$). For each pair $(i,j)$, at most one is 1 ($3$ choices) $2^n \cdot 3^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$
Equivalence Corresponds directly to partitions of set $A$ $B_n$ (The $n$-th Bell Number)

The Bell numbers satisfy the recurrence relation:

$$B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} B_k, \quad \text{with } B_0 = 1$$

For small values of $n$: $B_1 = 1$, $B_2 = 2$, $B_3 = 5$, $B_4 = 15$, and $B_5 = 52$. Thus, on a set of 3 elements, exactly 5 distinct equivalence relations can be constructed.

6. Functions as Structured Binary Relations

A function is a special type of relation that enforces determinism and completeness.

Formal Definition: Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets. A relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ is called a function (or mapping) from $A$ to $B$, written $f: A \to B$, if and only if:

  1. Existence (Completeness): For every element $x \in A$, there exists an element $y \in B$ such that $(x, y) \in f$. $$\forall x \in A, \, \exists y \in B \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Well-definedness): No element of $A$ is associated with more than one element of $B$. $$\big((x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f\big) \implies y_1 = y_2$$

When $(x, y) \in f$, we denote this by $y = f(x)$, where $y$ is called the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is called a pre-image of $y$ under $f$.

Image and Pre-Image Sets

For any arbitrary subset $S \subseteq A$, the direct image of $S$ under $f$ is:

$$f(S) = \{f(x) \in B : x \in S\} \subseteq B$$

The range of the function is the direct image of its entire domain: $\operatorname{Ran}(f) = f(A)$.

For any subset $T \subseteq B$, the inverse image (or pre-image) of $T$ under $f$ is:

$$f^{-1}(T) = \{x \in A : f(x) \in T\} \subseteq A$$

7. Comprehensive Classification of Functions (Injections, Surjections, Bijections)

7.1 Injective (One-to-One) Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be injective (or one-to-one) if distinct elements of the domain map to distinct elements of the codomain.

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Its logically equivalent contrapositive statement is:

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$

A function that fails this injectivity criterion is termed many-to-one:

$$\exists x_1, x_2 \in A \text{ such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$

Analytical Tests for Injectivity:

  • Direct Algebraic Method: Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ and manipulate the equation algebraically. If this condition simplifies exclusively to $x_1 = x_2$, the function is injective. If an alternative solution arises (such as $x_1 = -x_2$ or $x_1 = 1/x_2$), injectivity is violated on that domain.
  • Calculus Monotonicity Criterion: Let $f$ be a continuous function on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and differentiable on the interior of $I$. If $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in \operatorname{int}(I)$ (strictly increasing) or $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in \operatorname{int}(I)$ (strictly decreasing), then $f$ is strictly monotonic and therefore injective on $I$. If the derivative changes sign, the function has local extrema and cannot be injective over $I$.
  • The Horizontal Line Test: A real-valued function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = k$ ($k \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the graph of $f$ at most once.

7.2 Surjective (Onto) Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be surjective (or onto) if every element of the codomain possesses at least one pre-image in the domain.

$$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$

In set-theoretic terms, surjectivity is achieved if and only if the range coincides with the codomain:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = f(A) = B$$

If $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subsetneq B$, the function is called into.

Board Exam Method to Prove Surjectivity: Let $y \in B$ be an arbitrary element. Set $y = f(x)$ and solve for $x$ in terms of $y$, obtaining $x = g(y)$. To prove surjectivity, one must confirm two criteria:

  1. The formula $x = g(y)$ is defined and real for all possible choices of $y \in B$.
  2. The resulting value $x$ satisfies $x \in A$.

7.3 Bijective (One-to-One and Onto) Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is called a bijection (or one-to-one correspondence) if it is both injective and surjective.

$$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists! x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$

where the quantifier $\exists!$ denotes unique existence.

8. Combinatorial Enumeration of Mappings Between Finite Sets

Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

1. Total Number of Functions

Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ has $n$ independent choices in $B$:

$$N_{\text{total}} = n^m$$

2. Total Number of Injections

If $m > n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, at least two domain elements must map to the same codomain element. Thus, no injection can exist. If $m \le n$, the first element has $n$ choices, the second has $n-1$, and so on:

$$N_{\text{inj}} = \begin{cases} P(n, m) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-m)!} & \text{if } m \le n \\ 0 & \text{if } m > n \end{cases}$$

3. Total Number of Surjections

If $m < n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, the range can contain at most $m$ elements, so it cannot span all $n$ elements of $B$. If $m \ge n$, we count surjections using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE):

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$

where $S(m, n)$ represents the Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind, which count partitions of a set of size $m$ into $n$ non-empty sets.

4. Total Number of Bijections

A bijection requires $m = n$. When this holds, every injection is automatically a surjection:

$$N_{\text{bij}} = \begin{cases} n! & \text{if } m = n \\ 0 & \text{if } m \neq n \end{cases}$$

9. Composition of Functions

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions. The composite function, denoted by $g \circ f$ (read “$g$ circle $f$” or “$g$ composed with $f$”), is the function mapping from $A$ to $C$ defined by:

$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$

Existence Criterion for Composite Functions

For $g \circ f$ to be well-defined, the output of $f$ must be accepted by $g$. That is, the range of the internal function must be a subset of the domain of the external function:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$$

If this condition is violated, $g(f(x))$ cannot be evaluated for elements $x$ where $f(x) \notin \operatorname{Dom}(g)$. In such cases, the domain of the composite function must be restricted to:

$$\operatorname{Dom}(g \circ f) = \{x \in \operatorname{Dom}(f) : f(x) \in \operatorname{Dom}(g)\}$$

Algebraic Properties of Composition

  • Non-Commutativity: In general, composition is non-commutative: $$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$ In fact, even if $g \circ f$ is defined, $f \circ g$ may not be defined unless $\operatorname{Ran}(g) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(f)$. Even when both $f: A \to A$ and $g: A \to A$ share the same domain and codomain, $g(f(x))$ and $f(g(x))$ are generally distinct expressions.
  • Associativity: Function composition is strictly associative wherever defined. If $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$, then: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$
  • Identity Morphism: Let $I_A: A \to A$ ($I_A(x) = x$) and $I_B: B \to B$ ($I_B(y) = y$) be identity functions. Then for any $f: A \to B$: $$f \circ I_A = f \quad \text{and} \quad I_B \circ f = f$$

Theorems on Inheritance of Injective and Surjective Character

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$.

  1. If both $f$ and $g$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
  2. If both $f$ and $g$ are surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
  3. If both $f$ and $g$ are bijective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is bijective.
  4. Decomposition Inferences:
    • If $g \circ f$ is injective, then the inner function $f$ must be injective. (However, $g$ need not be injective).
    • If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then the outer function $g$ must be surjective. (However, $f$ need not be surjective).
    • If $g \circ f$ is bijective, then $f$ is injective and $g$ is surjective.
Proof of Decomposition: If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective:
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
Applying the function $g$ to these values gives: $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ By definition of composite functions: $$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$ Since $g \circ f$ is injective, we conclude: $$x_1 = x_2$$ Therefore, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, which proves $f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

10. Invertible Functions and the Construction of Inverses

Definition: A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be invertible if there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that:

$$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$

If such a function $g$ exists, it is called the inverse of $f$ and is uniquely denoted by $f^{-1}$.

The Fundamental Invertibility Theorem

Theorem: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection (both one-to-one and onto).

Proof ($\implies$ Direction):
Assume $f: A \to B$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.
1. Injectivity of $f$: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Then: $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$ Since $g \circ f = I_A$, this gives $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Thus, $f$ is injective.
2. Surjectivity of $f$: Let $y \in B$. Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we have: $$(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$$ Setting $x = g(y) \in A$, we find $f(x) = y$. Thus, every $y \in B$ has a pre-image under $f$, so $f$ is surjective.
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective. $\blacksquare$

Properties of Inverse Functions

  • Uniqueness: If an inverse exists, it is unique.
  • Involution: If $f: A \to B$ is bijective, then $(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$.
  • The Reversal Rule (Socks-and-Shoes Principle): If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are two invertible functions, then $g \circ f$ is invertible, and its inverse is: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Geometric Interpretation and the Reflection Principle

Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be an invertible real function. If the point $(a, b)$ lies on the curve $y = f(x)$, then $b = f(a)$, which means $a = f^{-1}(b)$. Consequently, the ordered pair $(b, a)$ lies on the curve $y = f^{-1}(x)$.

The transformation $(a, b) \mapsto (b, a)$ represents a geometric reflection across the line $y = x$. Therefore:

$$\text{The graph of } y = f^{-1}(x) \text{ is the reflection of the graph of } y = f(x) \text{ across the line } y = x.$$

Any point where a function meets its inverse on the Cartesian plane (for monotonic increasing functions) must lie along the line of symmetry $y = x$, satisfying $f(x) = x$.

11. Abstract Binary Operations

A binary operation is an algebraic formulation of an internal composition law.

Definition: A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function from the Cartesian product $A \times A$ into $A$:

$$*: A \times A \to A$$

We write $*(a, b)$ in infix notation as $a * b$.

The Closure Property: The requirement $*: A \times A \to A$ means the operation must satisfy closure:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b \in A$$

If any pair $a, b \in A$ produces a result outside $A$, then $*$ is not a binary operation on $A$. For example, normal division $\div$ is not a binary operation on $\mathbb{R}$ because $(a, 0)$ is undefined, and subtraction is not a binary operation on $\mathbb{N}$ because $2 - 5 = -3 \notin \mathbb{N}$.

11.1 Commutativity

A binary operation $*$ on set $A$ is commutative if:

$$a * b = b * a, \quad \forall a, b \in A$$

11.2 Associativity

A binary operation $*$ on set $A$ is associative if:

$$(a * b) * c = a * (b * c), \quad \forall a, b, c \in A$$

Associativity guarantees that multi-term expressions $a_1 * a_2 * \dots * a_n$ can be evaluated unambiguously without parentheses.

11.3 Identity Element

Let $*$ be a binary operation on set $A$. An element $e \in A$ is an identity element for $*$ if:

$$a * e = e * a = a, \quad \forall a \in A$$

If an identity element exists, it is unique.

11.4 Invertible Elements

Let $*$ be a binary operation on $A$ with identity element $e$. An element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible with respect to $*$ if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that:

$$a * b = b * a = e$$

The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$ and is typically denoted by $a^{-1}$. Under an associative operation, if an element has an inverse, that inverse is unique.

Combinatorial Count of Binary Operations

Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$.

  • A binary operation is any mapping from $A \times A$ to $A$. Since $|A \times A| = n^2$, the total number of binary operations is: $$N_{\text{bin}} = n^{(n^2)}$$
  • For commutativity, the Cayley operation table must be symmetric across the main diagonal. The diagonal has $n$ entries, and the upper triangle has $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ entries. Together, these $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ entries can be chosen arbitrarily from $n$ elements, fixing the lower triangle: $$N_{\text{comm}} = n^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

12. Rigorous Analytical Problem Paradigms (CBSE Pattern)

Paradigm 1: Equivalence Relation Proof with Congruence Arithmetic

Problem: Let $\mathbb{Z}$ be the set of all integers. Prove that the relation $R$ defined on $\mathbb{Z}$ by $R = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} : n \mid (a - b)\}$, where $n$ is a fixed positive integer ($n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$), is an equivalence relation. Find its equivalence classes.

Demonstration of Proof:
Recall that $n \mid (a - b) \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } a - b = k n$.
1. Reflexivity:
Let $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot n$.
Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mid (a - a)$. Thus, $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$. $R$ is reflexive.
2. Symmetry:
Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$.
Then $a - b = k n$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
Multiplying by $-1$ gives $b - a = -k n = (-k) n$.
Since $k \in \mathbb{Z} \implies -k \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $n \mid (b - a)$.
Thus, $(b, a) \in R$, proving that $R$ is symmetric.
3. Transitivity:
Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$.
Then there exist integers $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that: $$a - b = k_1 n \quad \text{and} \quad b - c = k_2 n$$ Adding these two equations yields: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 n + k_2 n \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2) n$$ Since the integers are closed under addition, $(k_1 + k_2) \in \mathbb{Z}$.
Therefore, $n \mid (a - c)$, which means $(a, c) \in R$. $R$ is transitive.
Since $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$.

Determination of Equivalence Classes:
By the Division Algorithm, any integer $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ can be uniquely written as $a = q n + r$, where $q \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n - 1\}$.
This yields exactly $n$ pairwise disjoint equivalence classes: $$[r] = \{x \in \mathbb{Z} : x \equiv r \pmod n\} = \{q n + r : q \in \mathbb{Z}\}$$ where $r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n - 1\}$. The partition is $\mathbb{Z}/R = \{[0], [1], [2], \dots, [n-1]\}$.

Paradigm 2: Advanced Bijectivity and Functional Inversion

Problem: Consider the function $f: \mathbb{R} - \left\{-\frac{4}{3}\right\} \to \mathbb{R} - \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ defined by:

$$f(x) = \frac{4x + 3}{3x + 4}$$

Show that $f$ is a bijective function, and compute its analytical inverse $f^{-1}$.

Demonstration of Proof:
Let $A = \mathbb{R} - \left\{-\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} - \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$.
1. Proof of Injectivity (One-to-One):
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\frac{4x_1 + 3}{3x_1 + 4} = \frac{4x_2 + 3}{3x_2 + 4}$$ Cross-multiplying gives: $$(4x_1 + 3)(3x_2 + 4) = (4x_2 + 3)(3x_1 + 4)$$ Expanding both products: $$12x_1 x_2 + 16x_1 + 9x_2 + 12 = 12x_1 x_2 + 16x_2 + 9x_1 + 12$$ Subtracting identical terms from both sides: $$16x_1 + 9x_2 = 16x_2 + 9x_1$$ $$16x_1 - 9x_1 = 16x_2 - 9x_2 \implies 7x_1 = 7x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Thus, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, so $f$ is injective.

2. Proof of Surjectivity (Onto):
Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} - \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\}$. We seek an element $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. $$y = \frac{4x + 3}{3x + 4} \implies y(3x + 4) = 4x + 3$$ $$3xy + 4y = 4x + 3 \implies 3xy - 4x = 3 - 4y$$ $$x(3y - 4) = 3 - 4y \implies x = \frac{3 - 4y}{3y - 4} = \frac{4y - 3}{4 - 3y}$$ We check whether this $x$ is valid:
- The denominator $4 - 3y = 0 \iff y = \frac{4}{3}$. Since $y \in B$, $y \neq \frac{4}{3}$, so $x$ is well-defined and real for all $y \in B$.
- We verify that $x \neq -\frac{4}{3}$: $$\frac{4y - 3}{4 - 3y} = -\frac{4}{3} \implies 3(4y - 3) = -4(4 - 3y) \implies 12y - 9 = -16 + 12y \implies -9 = -16$$ This is a contradiction, so $x \neq -\frac{4}{3}$ for any real $y$.
Thus, for every $y \in B$, there exists $x = \frac{4y - 3}{4 - 3y} \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Therefore, $f$ is surjective.

Conclusion & Inverse:
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore invertible.
The inverse function $f^{-1}: \mathbb{R} - \left\{\frac{4}{3}\right\} \to \mathbb{R} - \left\{-\frac{4}{3}\right\}$ is given by: $$f^{-1}(x) = \frac{4x - 3}{4 - 3x}$$

13. Common Traps, Fallacies, and Edge Cases in CBSE Examinations

Board exam evaluators frequently test subtle definitions, edge cases, and common misconceptions. Below is an overview of critical pitfalls to watch for:

  • The “Divisible By” Zero Pitfall:
    Consider a relation defined by $a \, R \, b \iff a - b$ is divisible by $n$. When checking reflexivity, $a - a = 0$. Remember that $0$ is divisible by any non-zero integer $n$ because $0 = 0 \cdot n$. Thus, reflexivity holds.
  • Reflexivity on Restricted Sets:
    A relation $R$ defined as “is parallel to” ($\parallel$) on the set of all straight lines in a Euclidean plane is an equivalence relation, because every line is parallel to itself (direction vectors are collinear: $\vec{d} \times \vec{d} = \vec{0}$). However, the relation “is perpendicular to” ($\perp$) is not an equivalence relation:
    • It is not reflexive, since no line is perpendicular to itself ($L \not\perp L$).
    • It is symmetric ($L_1 \perp L_2 \implies L_2 \perp L_1$).
    • It is not transitive ($L_1 \perp L_2 \land L_2 \perp L_3 \implies L_1 \parallel L_3$, not $L_1 \perp L_3$).
  • The Real Codomain Assumption in Injectivity vs. Surjectivity:
    Consider $f(x) = x^2$.
    • If $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f$ is neither injective ($f(-2) = f(2) = 4$) nor surjective ($\operatorname{Ran}(f) = [0, \infty) \neq \mathbb{R}$).
    • If $f: [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)$, $f$ is both injective and surjective, making it bijective with inverse $f^{-1}(x) = \sqrt{x}$.
    Crucial Rule: Never classify a function solely by its formula. The domain and codomain are fundamental to its definition and character.
  • Transitivity Under Incomplete Hypotheses:
    A relation is transitive unless there exist pairs $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ such that $(a, c) \notin R$. If no such pairs exist, transitivity holds vacuously. For instance, in $R = \{(1, 2), (3, 4)\}$, no two pairs can be chained, so $R$ is transitive.
  • Distinction Between Identity and Reflexive Relations:
    On $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, the relation $R = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2)\}$ is reflexive, but it is not an identity relation because $(1, 2) \in R$ with $1 \neq 2$.

Derivation 1: Combinatorial Formulas for Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

Objective: Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. Derive the formulas for the total number of reflexive relations, symmetric relations, and relations that are simultaneously reflexive and symmetric on $A$.

Step 1: Structural Representation of $A \times A$

The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $|A \times A| = n^2$ ordered pairs. Represent $A \times A$ as an $n \times n$ coordinate grid or boolean matrix $M = [m_{ij}]$, where $m_{ij} \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes whether $(a_i, a_j) \in R$:

$$A \times A = \underbrace{\{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\}}_{\text{Diagonal pairs } (D)} \cup \underbrace{\{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j\}}_{\text{Off-diagonal pairs } (O)}$$ $$\text{Count of diagonal elements: } |D| = n$$ $$\text{Count of off-diagonal elements: } |O| = n^2 - n = n(n-1)$$

Step 2: Number of Reflexive Relations

A relation $R$ is reflexive if and only if $\forall a_i \in A, \, (a_i, a_i) \in R$.

  • For every diagonal pair $(a_i, a_i) \in D$, there is exactly $1$ choice: it must be included in $R$.
  • For every off-diagonal pair $(a_i, a_j) \in O$, there are $2$ independent choices: it may either be included or excluded.
$$\text{Number of off-diagonal pairs} = n^2 - n$$ $$N_{\text{ref}} = 1^n \times 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

Step 3: Number of Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$.

  • The $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ can be chosen independently: $2$ choices each $\implies 2^n$ combinations.
  • The $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal elements form unordered pairs of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ with $i < j$. The number of such pairs is: $$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$
  • For each pair $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, symmetry dictates that either both are in $R$ or neither is in $R$. This yields $2$ choices per pair.
$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Step 4: Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

Combining both constraints:

  • Diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ must all be included $\implies 1$ choice for each of the $n$ elements.
  • Off-diagonal symmetric pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ have $2$ choices each (both in or both out).
$$N_{\text{ref}\cap\text{sym}} = 1^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Classes (Partition Theorem)

Objective: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Prove that the family of equivalence classes partitions $A$, meaning:
(i) $\forall a \in A, \, [a] \neq \emptyset$ and $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$,
(ii) For any $a, b \in A$, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$,
(iii) $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.

Step 1: Non-emptiness and Exhaustion of Set $A$

By definition, an equivalence class is given by $[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$.

  • Since $R$ is reflexive, $(a, a) \in R$ for every $a \in A$.
  • Therefore, $a \in [a]$, which proves $[a] \neq \emptyset$.
  • Since $a \in [a]$ for all $a \in A$, we have: $$A = \bigcup_{a \in A} \{a\} \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$$
  • By definition, $[a] \subseteq A$ for every $a \in A$, so $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A$.
  • Consequently: $$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$

Step 2: Proof of Lemma: $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$

Assume $(a, b) \in R$. We show $[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a]$.

  1. Let $x \in [a]$. By definition, $(x, a) \in R$. $$\text{Given } (x, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (a, b) \in R$$ By transitivity of $R$: $$(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$ Hence, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
  2. Conversely, let $y \in [b] \implies (y, b) \in R$. $$\text{Since } (a, b) \in R, \text{ by symmetry we have } (b, a) \in R.$$ By transitivity applied to $(y, b) \in R$ and $(b, a) \in R$: $$(y, a) \in R \implies y \in [a]$$ Hence, $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
$$\text{Therefore, } [a] \subseteq [b] \text{ and } [b] \subseteq [a] \implies [a] = [b]$$

Step 3: Proof of Pairwise Disjointness ($[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$)

Let $[a]$ and $[b]$ be two equivalence classes such that $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. We show that they must be identical.

  • Let $c \in [a] \cap [b]$. Then $c \in [a]$ and $c \in [b]$.
  • $c \in [a] \implies (c, a) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$ (by symmetry).
  • $c \in [b] \implies (c, b) \in R$.
  • By transitivity: $$(a, c) \in R \land (c, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$$
  • By the Lemma proved in Step 2, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$.

Contrapositively, if $[a] \neq [b]$, then $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. Thus, any two distinct equivalence classes are mutually disjoint, and their union is $A$. Hence, the set of equivalence classes forms a partition of $A$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: Composition Theorems for Injective and Surjective Functions

Objective: Given mappings $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$:

  • Part A: Prove that if $f$ and $g$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
  • Part B: Prove that if $f$ and $g$ are surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
  • Part C: Prove that if $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ is injective; if $g \circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ is surjective.

Part A: Injectivity of $g \circ f$

Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that:

$$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$ $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$

Since $g$ is injective, $\forall u, v \in B, \, g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$. Applying this with $u = f(x_1)$ and $v = f(x_2)$:

$$f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$

Since $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$.

$$\text{Thus, } (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Hence, $g \circ f$ is injective.

Part B: Surjectivity of $g \circ f$

Let $z \in C$ be an arbitrary element.

  • Since $g: B \to C$ is surjective, there exists $y \in B$ such that: $$g(y) = z$$
  • Since $f: A \to B$ is surjective, for this $y \in B$, there exists $x \in A$ such that: $$f(x) = y$$
  • Substitute $f(x)$ for $y$ in $g(y) = z$: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = z$$

Thus, for every $z \in C$, there exists $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is surjective.

Part C: Deconstructing Injective and Surjective Compositions

  1. Assume $g \circ f$ is injective: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. $$\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$ Since $g \circ f$ is injective, this implies $x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ must be injective.
  2. Assume $g \circ f$ is surjective: Let $z \in C$. $$\implies \exists x \in A \text{ such that } (g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z$$ Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have $g(y) = z$. Thus, every $z \in C$ has a pre-image $y \in B$, proving $g$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Characterization of Invertibility ($f$ is Invertible $\iff f$ is Bijective)

Objective: Prove that a function $f: A \to B$ possesses a two-sided inverse $g: B \to A$ (such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$) if and only if $f$ is both injective and surjective.

Step 1: Necessity ($\implies$)

Assume $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.

  • Injectivity: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Apply $g$: $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$ Since $g \circ f = I_A$, we have $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Thus, $f$ is injective.
  • Surjectivity: Let $y \in B$. Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we evaluate at $y$: $$(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$$ Setting $x = g(y) \in A$, we have found an $x$ such that $f(x) = y$. Thus, $f$ is surjective.

Therefore, invertibility implies that $f$ is bijective.

Step 2: Sufficiency ($\impliedby$)

Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.

  • Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is injective, this element $x$ is strictly unique. If $f(x_1) = y$ and $f(x_2) = y$, then $x_1 = x_2$.
  • We can therefore define a well-defined mapping $g: B \to A$ by the rule: $$g(y) = x \iff f(x) = y$$

Verify identity compositions:

  1. For any $x \in A$, let $y = f(x)$. Then by definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x) \implies g \circ f = I_A$$
  2. For any $y \in B$, let $x = g(y)$. Then by definition of $g$, $f(x) = y$. $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y) \implies f \circ g = I_B$$

Thus, $g$ is the two-sided inverse of $f$, denoted $g = f^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Reversal Law for the Inverse of a Composition

Objective: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible and that its inverse satisfies: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Step 1: Well-Definedness of Constituent Inverses

Since $f$ and $g$ are invertible, both are bijections, and their inverses exist:

$$f^{-1}: B \to A \quad \text{such that} \quad f^{-1} \circ f = I_A, \quad f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$$ $$g^{-1}: C \to B \quad \text{such that} \quad g^{-1} \circ g = I_B, \quad g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

The candidate inverse function is $h = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}: C \to A$.

Step 2: Verification of the Left Identity Condition

Compute $h \circ (g \circ f) = (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)$:

$$h \circ (g \circ f) = \left(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}\right) \circ (g \circ f)$$

Applying the associativity of function composition:

$$= f^{-1} \circ \left(g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)\right)$$ $$= f^{-1} \circ \left((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f\right)$$

Substitute $g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$:

$$= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f)$$

Since $I_B \circ f = f$:

$$= f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$

Step 3: Verification of the Right Identity Condition

Compute $(g \circ f) \circ h = (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$:

$$(g \circ f) \circ h = (g \circ f) \circ \left(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}\right)$$

Applying associativity:

$$= g \circ \left(f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})\right)$$ $$= g \circ \left((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}\right)$$

Substitute $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$:

$$= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1})$$

Since $I_B \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}$:

$$= g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

Step 4: Uniqueness Conclusion

Since $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$ and $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$, the inverse of $g \circ f$ is unique and satisfies:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$
