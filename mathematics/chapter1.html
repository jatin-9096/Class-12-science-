1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Set-Theoretic Mechanics & Cartesian Architecture

The formal architecture of higher mathematics, specifically the analysis of real and abstract systems in CBSE Class 12, rests upon the scaffolding of set theory. A rigorous treatment of relations and functions requires understanding sets not merely as static collections of objects, but as spaces upon which structural linkages are constructed. Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets defined within a universal discourse $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian product, denoted by $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate belongs strictly to the antecedent set $A$ and the second coordinate belongs to the consequent set $B$. Formally, this is stated as:

$$A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$

When the two sets are identical, the product $A \times A$ is written as $A^2$, representing the Cartesian square of $A$. Geometrically, if $A = \mathbb{R}$, then $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$ represents the two-dimensional Euclidean continuum. The ordered nature of the pair is fundamental: $(a, b) = (c, d) \iff a = c \land b = d$. Consequently, unless $A = B$ or at least one of the sets is empty, the Cartesian product is non-commutative, meaning $A \times B \neq B \times A$.

From a combinatorial perspective, if set $A$ contains $|A| = m$ elements and set $B$ contains $|B| = n$ elements, the cardinality of their Cartesian product is given by:

$$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = m \cdot n$$

Any subset $R \subseteq A \times B$ is formally defined as a binary relation from $A$ to $B$. If $(a, b) \in R$, we write $a \, R \, b$, which is read as "$a$ is related to $b$ under relation $R$." Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, we state that $a$ is not related to $b$ ($a \not R \, b$). If $A = B$, $R \subseteq A \times A$ is called a binary relation on the set $A$. Because the power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ consists of all possible subsets of $A \times B$, the total number of distinct relations that can be constructed from $A$ to $B$ is given by:

$$|\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{|A \times B|} = 2^{mn}$$

For a relation $R$ defined on a single finite set $A$ containing $n$ elements, the total number of possible relations is $2^{n^2}$. This exponential growth illustrates the vast spectrum of relational structures that can be imposed on even the most elementary discrete sets.

Associated with every relation $R \subseteq A \times B$ are three fundamental constructs:

  • Domain of $R$ ($\operatorname{Dom}(R)$): The set of all first coordinates of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$: $$\operatorname{Dom}(R) = \{a \in A : \exists b \in B \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq A$$
  • Range of $R$ ($\operatorname{Ran}(R)$): The set of all second coordinates of the ordered pairs belonging to $R$: $$\operatorname{Ran}(R) = \{b \in B : \exists a \in A \text{ such that } (a, b) \in R\} \subseteq B$$
  • Codomain of $R$: The entire set $B$ within which the relation targets its outputs. It is critical to distinguish between the range and the codomain; the range is always a subset of the codomain ($\operatorname{Ran}(R) \subseteq B$), but they are not necessarily identical.

2. Exhaustive Typology of Binary Relations

In Class 12 advanced mathematics, relations defined on a single set $A$ are classified according to specific structural symmetries and relational axioms. The basic classifications encompass the following categories:

2.1 Trivial and Universal Relations

Let $A$ be any set. The empty set $\emptyset$ is a subset of $A \times A$ ($\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$). This is known as the empty relation or the void relation on $A$. Under an empty relation, no element of $A$ is related to any element of $A$:

$$R = \emptyset \implies \forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \notin R$$

At the opposite extreme, the complete Cartesian product $A \times A$ is trivially a subset of itself ($A \times A \subseteq A \times A$). This is called the universal relation on $A$. Under a universal relation, every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$:

$$R = A \times A \implies \forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R$$

Both the empty relation and the universal relation are often referred to as the trivial relations on $A$.

2.2 The Identity Relation

The identity relation on a set $A$, denoted by $I_A$, is the relation wherein every element of $A$ is related exclusively to itself:

$$I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$$

The identity relation must not be confused with a reflexive relation. In an identity relation, an element is related only to itself; no additional ordered pairs of the form $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$ may exist.

2.3 The Reflexive Relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is defined as reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. Formally:

$$\forall a \in A \implies (a, a) \in R$$

In the language of set inclusion, $R$ is reflexive if and only if $I_A \subseteq R$. Thus, while the identity relation requires strict exclusivity ($(a, b) \in I_A \implies a = b$), reflexivity merely requires that all self-pairs $(a, a)$ are present, permitting additional pairs $(a, b)$ where $a \neq b$.

Combinatorial Derivation of Reflexive Relations: Consider a set $A$ with $|A| = n$. The Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $n^2$ total pairs. These pairs can be partitioned into two distinct subsets:

  1. The diagonal elements: $D = \{(a_i, a_i) : i = 1, 2, \dots, n\}$, with $|D| = n$.
  2. The non-diagonal elements: $N = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j\}$, with $|N| = n^2 - n$.

For a relation $R$ to be reflexive, every element of $D$ must be included in $R$. Therefore, there is only $1$ choice for each of the $n$ diagonal pairs. For the remaining $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal pairs, each pair may be either included or excluded independently (2 choices per pair). Hence, the total number of reflexive relations on a set of cardinality $n$ is:

$$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \cdot 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$

2.4 The Symmetric Relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is defined as symmetric if the presence of any ordered pair implies the presence of its reversed counterpart:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$$

Using the concept of the inverse relation $R^{-1} = \{(b, a) : (a, b) \in R\}$, a relation is symmetric if and only if:

$$R = R^{-1}$$

Combinatorial Derivation of Symmetric Relations: Again partition $A \times A$ into the $n$ diagonal pairs and the $n^2 - n$ non-diagonal pairs. The non-diagonal pairs can be grouped into $\frac{n^2 - n}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$ unordered pairs of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ with $i < j$. For symmetry:

  • Each of the $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ can be independently included or excluded: $2^n$ choices.
  • For each of the $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs of symmetric off-diagonal coordinates, there are only 2 choices: either both $(a_i, a_j)$ and $(a_j, a_i)$ are in $R$, or neither is in $R$. (Including only one violates symmetry).

Consequently, the total number of symmetric relations on a set of cardinality $n$ is:

$$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

2.5 The Transitive Relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is defined as transitive if whenever an element $a$ is related to $b$ and $b$ is related to $c$, it follows that $a$ is related to $c$:

$$\forall a, b, c \in A, \, [(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$$

Transitivity is often misunderstood regarding its conditional premise. The implication $[(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$ is fundamentally an implication ($P \implies Q$). Under the rules of classical mathematical logic, an implication is automatically true whenever its hypothesis ($P$) is false. Therefore, if there are no pairs in $R$ satisfying the compound antecedent $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ with the intermediate element $b$ matching, the condition is satisfied vacuously.

Critical Example of Vacuous Transitivity: Consider the set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and the relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$. Here, $(1, 2) \in R$. However, there is no pair in $R$ starting with 2. The premise $(1, 2) \in R \land (2, c) \in R$ can never be fulfilled. Thus, the implication is never violated, and $R$ is transitive.

2.6 The Anti-Symmetric Relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is defined as anti-symmetric if:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \, [(a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R] \implies a = b$$

Equivalently, if $a \neq b$, then both $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ cannot belong to $R$ simultaneously. Anti-symmetry is not the simple negation of symmetry. A relation can be both symmetric and anti-symmetric (e.g., the identity relation $I_A$), or it can be neither.

3. Equivalence Relations, Partitions, and Modular Arithmetic

3.1 The Equivalence Axioms

A relation $R$ defined on a non-empty set $A$ is an equivalence relation if and only if it satisfies three properties simultaneously:

  1. Reflexivity: $\forall a \in A, \, (a, a) \in R$.
  2. Symmetry: $\forall a, b \in A, \, (a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$.
  3. Transitivity: $\forall a, b, c \in A, \, [(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R] \implies (a, c) \in R$.

Equivalence relations generalize the concept of equality. While equality ($=$) is strict identity, an equivalence relation groups elements that share a designated mathematical property.

3.2 Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any element $a \in A$, the equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$ or $\operatorname{cl}(a)$, is the set of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:

$$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\} = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$$

The element $a$ is known as the representative of the equivalence class $[a]$.

3.3 Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. The set of all equivalence classes forms a partition of $A$. That is:

  1. Every equivalence class is a non-empty subset of $A$: $\forall a \in A, \, [a] \neq \emptyset$ (since $a \in [a]$ by reflexivity).
  2. Any two equivalence classes are either identical or disjoint: $$\forall a, b \in A, \, \text{either } [a] = [b] \text{ or } [a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$$
  3. The union of all equivalence classes recovers the entire set $A$: $$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$

Proof of Property 2 (Disjointness or Identity):
Assume $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. We must demonstrate that this implies $[a] = [b]$.
Since the intersection is non-empty, there exists some element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$.
By definition of equivalence classes:

$$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R \quad \text{(by symmetry)}$$ $$x \in [b] \implies (x, b) \in R$$

Applying transitivity to $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$:

$$(a, b) \in R$$

Now, let $y$ be any arbitrary element of $[a]$. Then $(y, a) \in R$. Since $(y, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$, by transitivity we obtain $(y, b) \in R$, which implies $y \in [b]$. Therefore, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
By identical logic, starting with $(b, a) \in R$ (from symmetry of $(a, b) \in R$), let $z \in [b]$, then $(z, b) \in R \implies (z, a) \in R \implies z \in [a]$, proving $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
By mutual subset inclusion, $[a] = [b]$. Hence, two equivalence classes with at least one common element are identical, meaning distinct equivalence classes must be completely disjoint. $\blacksquare$

3.4 Congruence Modulo $m$ ($\mathbb{Z}_m$)

A foundational example of an equivalence relation in number theory and the CBSE syllabus is congruence modulo $m$. Let $\mathbb{Z}$ be the set of integers and let $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ be a fixed positive integer. A relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is defined by:

$$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b) \iff a - b = km \quad \text{for some } k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

Let us rigorously verify the three equivalence axioms:

  • Reflexivity: For any $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot m$. Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $m \mid (a - a)$, and thus $a \equiv a \pmod m$. The relation is reflexive.
  • Symmetry: Let $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $a \equiv b \pmod m$. Then $a - b = k_1 m$ for some $k_1 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiplying both sides by $-1$, we obtain $b - a = (-k_1)m$. Since $k_1 \in \mathbb{Z} \implies -k_1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $m \mid (b - a)$, so $b \equiv a \pmod m$. The relation is symmetric.
  • Transitivity: Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $a \equiv b \pmod m$ and $b \equiv c \pmod m$. This implies $a - b = k_1 m$ and $b - c = k_2 m$ for some integers $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding these two linear equations yields: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 m + k_2 m \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)m$$ Since the integers are closed under addition, $(k_1 + k_2) \in \mathbb{Z}$. Thus, $m \mid (a - c)$, which means $a \equiv c \pmod m$. The relation is transitive.

Since all three axioms hold, congruence modulo $m$ is an equivalence relation. It partitions the infinite set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ into exactly $m$ pairwise disjoint equivalence classes, called residue classes modulo $m$:

$$[0] = \{\dots, -2m, -m, 0, m, 2m, \dots\}$$ $$[1] = \{\dots, -2m+1, -m+1, 1, m+1, 2m+1, \dots\}$$ $$\vdots$$ $$[m - 1] = \{\dots, -m-1, -1, m-1, 2m-1, 3m-1, \dots\}$$

Their union is $\bigcup_{r=0}^{m-1} [r] = \mathbb{Z}$, and $[r_1] \cap [r_2] = \emptyset$ whenever $r_1 \neq r_2$.

4. Rigorous Mapping Theory: Typology and Analysis of Functions

4.1 Modern Mathematical Definition of a Function

A function (or mapping) $f$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, is a specialized binary relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ satisfying two conditions:

  1. Totality: Every element of the antecedent set $A$ must be associated with an element in $B$: $$\forall x \in A, \, \exists y \in B \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Well-definedness): No element of $A$ can be associated with more than one element in $B$: $$(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f \implies y_1 = y_2$$

We write $y = f(x)$ to indicate that $y$ is the unique image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is a pre-image of $y$. The set $A$ is the domain of $f$, and the set $B$ is the codomain of $f$. The range of $f$ is:

$$f(A) = \{f(x) \in B : x \in A\} \subseteq B$$

For finite sets $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$, every element in $A$ has independently $n$ choices in $B$. Thus, the total number of functions from $A$ to $B$ is:

$$N_{\text{total mappings}} = n^m = |B|^{|A|}$$

4.2 Injective Functions (One-One Mappings)

A function $f: A \to B$ is injective (or one-to-one) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the codomain.

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \, x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$

In analytical proofs, the logically equivalent contrapositive statement is generally preferred:

$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \, f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

If a function is not injective, it is classified as many-to-one:

$$\exists x_1, x_2 \in A \text{ such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$

Analytical and Calculus-Based Tests for Injectivity:

  • The Horizontal Line Test: Let $f: D \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. The function $f$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = c$ (where $c \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the Cartesian graph of $y = f(x)$ at most once. If any horizontal line intersects the graph at two or more points, the function is many-to-one.
  • Monotonicity Criterion (Differential Calculus): Let $f$ be continuous on a real interval $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. If the first derivative $f'(x)$ is strictly positive ($\forall x \in (a, b), f'(x) > 0$) or strictly negative ($\forall x \in (a, b), f'(x) < 0$), then $f$ is strictly monotonic (strictly increasing or strictly decreasing) and is therefore injective on $[a, b]$. If $f'(x) = 0$ at discrete, isolated points, injectivity is preserved (e.g., $f(x) = x^3$, where $f'(0) = 0$, is strictly increasing and injective). However, if $f'(x) = 0$ over an entire sub-interval, the function is constant on that interval and not injective.

Combinatorics of Injective Functions:

Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$. For an injection to exist, each element of $A$ must target a unique element of $B$. By the Pigeonhole Principle, if $m > n$, at least two elements in $A$ must share an image in $B$, making an injection impossible. If $m \le n$, the first element of $A$ has $n$ choices, the second has $(n - 1)$ choices, and the $m$-th element has $(n - m + 1)$ choices. Thus:

$$N_{\text{injections}} = \begin{cases} ^n P_m = \dfrac{n!}{(n - m)!}, & \text{if } n \ge m \\ 0, & \text{if } n < m \end{cases}$$

4.3 Surjective Functions (Onto Mappings)

A function $f: A \to B$ is surjective (or onto) if every element in the codomain has at least one pre-image in the domain:

$$\forall y \in B, \, \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$

In set notation, $f$ is surjective if and only if its range equals its codomain:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = B$$

If $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subset B$ (a strict proper subset), the function is called into.

Analytical Method to Prove Surjectivity:

  1. Set up the equation $y = f(x)$, where $y \in B$ is an arbitrary representative element of the codomain.
  2. Algebraically invert the equation to express the independent variable $x$ entirely in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
  3. Verify two operational conditions:
    • Is $g(y)$ defined for every value of $y \in B$? (Check for divisions by zero, negative radicands under even roots, etc.)
    • Does the resulting value $g(y)$ lie within the specified domain $A$ for every $y \in B$?
  4. If both conditions hold, then $x = g(y) \in A$ serves as the valid pre-image: $f(g(y)) = y$, confirming surjectivity.

Combinatorics of Surjective Functions:

Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$. If $m < n$, by the Pigeonhole Principle, the range can contain at most $m$ elements, so it cannot cover all $n$ elements of $B$. Thus, no surjection exists. When $m \ge n$, the number of surjective functions can be computed using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE) or Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind $\left\{ \begin{matrix} m \\ n \end{matrix} \right\}$:

$$N_{\text{surjections}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = n! \left\{ \begin{matrix} m \\ n \end{matrix} \right\}$$

For the special case where $m = n$:

$$N_{\text{surjections}} = n!$$

4.4 Bijective Functions (One-One and Onto Mappings)

A function $f: A \to B$ is bijective (or a one-to-one correspondence) if and only if it is both injective and surjective.

$$f \text{ is bijective} \iff [f \text{ is injective}] \land [f \text{ is surjective}]$$

Bijective functions establish an exact pairing between elements of set $A$ and set $B$. For finite sets, a bijection can exist if and only if $|A| = |B| = n$. In this case, the number of possible bijections is:

$$N_{\text{bijections}} = n!$$

Important Theorem for Finite Sets: Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = |B|$. A function $f: A \to B$ is injective if and only if it is surjective. Hence, establishing either injectivity or surjectivity on equal-sized finite sets automatically confirms bijectivity. Warning: This equivalence fails for infinite sets. For instance, consider $f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ defined by $f(x) = x + 1$. Here, $f$ is injective, but it is not surjective because $1 \in \mathbb{N}$ has no pre-image ($x = 0 \notin \mathbb{N}$).

5. Composition of Functions and Algebraic Mechanics

5.1 Formal Definition and Existence Criteria

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B' \to C$ be two functions. The composite function, denoted by $g \circ f$ (read "$g$ circle $f$" or "$g$ of $f$"), is a mapping from $A$ into $C$ defined by:

$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x))$$

Strict Existence Criterion: The composite function $g \circ f$ is mathematically well-defined if and only if the range of the inner function $f$ is a subset of the domain of the outer function $g$:

$$\operatorname{Ran}(f) \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}(g)$$

If this condition is violated, there will exist elements $x \in A$ for which $f(x) \notin \operatorname{Dom}(g)$, leaving $g(f(x))$ undefined and violating the totality axiom of a function.

5.2 Algebraic Properties of Function Composition

  • Non-Commutativity: In general, function composition is non-commutative: $$g \circ f \neq f \circ g$$ Even when both composites $g \circ f$ and $f \circ g$ exist (e.g., when $f, g: A \to A$), they typically yield distinct mappings. For example, let $f, g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ where $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$. Then: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$$ $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(x^2) = 2x^2 + 1$$ Clearly, $4x^2 + 4x + 1 \neq 2x^2 + 1$ for almost all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Associativity: Function composition is associative. Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$. Then: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$ Proof: For any $x \in A$: $$(h \circ (g \circ f))(x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ $$((h \circ g) \circ f)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Because their outputs match for every $x \in A$ and their domains and codomains are identical, the two composite functions are equal. $\blacksquare$
  • Identity Preservation: Let $f: A \to B$, and let $I_A: A \to A$ and $I_B: B \to B$ be identity functions ($I_A(x) = x, I_B(y) = y$). Then: $$f \circ I_A = f \quad \text{and} \quad I_B \circ f = f$$

5.3 Composition Theorems on Injectivity and Surjectivity

Theorem 1 (Direct Preservation): Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$.

  1. If both $f$ and $g$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
    Proof: Assume $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$. Since $g$ is injective, this implies $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Since $f$ is injective, this further implies $x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$
  2. If both $f$ and $g$ are surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
    Proof: Let $z \in C$. Since $g$ is surjective, $\exists y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$. Since $f$ is surjective, for this $y \in B$, $\exists x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$. Substituting yields $(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = z$. Thus, every element of $C$ has a pre-image in $A$. $\blacksquare$
  3. If both $f$ and $g$ are bijective, then $g \circ f$ is bijective.

Theorem 2 (Deconstructive Inferences): Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$.

  1. If $g \circ f$ is injective, then the inner function $f$ must be injective. (The outer function $g$ is not necessarily injective).
    Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ with $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f$ is injective, it follows that $x_1 = x_2$. Thus, $f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$
  2. If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then the outer function $g$ must be surjective. (The inner function $f$ is not necessarily surjective).
    Proof: Let $z \in C$. Since $g \circ f$ is surjective, $\exists x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z$. Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have $g(y) = z$. Thus, for any $z \in C$, there exists $y \in B$ such that $g(y) = z$, proving that $g$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

6. Invertibility and Inverse Functions

6.1 Axiomatic Definition of Invertibility

A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be invertible if there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that:

$$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$

Where $I_A$ is the identity function on $A$ ($I_A(x) = x, \forall x \in A$) and $I_B$ is the identity function on $B$ ($I_B(y) = y, \forall y \in B$). The function $g$ is uniquely determined and is called the inverse of $f$, denoted by $f^{-1}$.

6.2 The Fundamental Characterization Theorem

Theorem: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is bijective.

Proof ($\implies$ Direction): Assume $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.

  • Since $g \circ f = I_A$ is the identity function (which is injective), by Theorem 2.1, $f$ must be injective.
  • Since $f \circ g = I_B$ is the identity function (which is surjective), by Theorem 2.2, $f$ must be surjective.

Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Proof ($\impliedby$ Direction): Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.

  • Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is injective, this pre-image $x$ is unique.

We can therefore define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element in $A$ such that $f(x) = y$.

  • For any $x \in A$, let $y = f(x)$. Then $g(f(x)) = g(y) = x$. Thus, $(g \circ f)(x) = x \implies g \circ f = I_A$.
  • For any $y \in B$, let $x = g(y)$. Then $f(x) = y$, meaning $f(g(y)) = y$. Thus, $(f \circ g)(y) = y \implies f \circ g = I_B$.

Thus, $g$ satisfies the invertibility criterion, proving that $f$ is invertible and $g = f^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

6.3 Properties of Inverse Functions

  • Uniqueness of Inverse: If a function has an inverse, that inverse is unique. Let $g_1$ and $g_2$ both be inverses of $f: A \to B$. Then: $$g_1 = g_1 \circ I_B = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_A \circ g_2 = g_2$$
  • Involution Property: If $f: A \to B$ is invertible, then $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is also invertible, and: $$(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$$
  • The "Shoes and Socks" Theorem (Reversal Rule): Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and its inverse is given by: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ Proof: We check the identity compositions: $$((f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ $$((g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$ By uniqueness of the inverse, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

6.4 Geometric Reflection Principle

If a point $(a, b)$ lies on the Cartesian graph of an invertible function $y = f(x)$, then by definition $b = f(a) \iff a = f^{-1}(b)$. This implies that the point $(b, a)$ lies on the graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$. In Euclidean coordinate geometry, the transformation $(a, b) \mapsto (b, a)$ represents an orthogonal reflection across the line $y = x$. Therefore:

The graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the reflection of the graph of $y = f(x)$ across the line of symmetry $y = x$.

Any intersection between the graphs of $y = f(x)$ and $y = f^{-1}(x)$ for an increasing function must occur along the diagonal axis $y = x$.

7. Binary Operations and Structural Abstract Algebra

7.1 Formal Definition and Closure

A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function that maps every ordered pair of elements in $A \times A$ to a unique element in $A$:

$$*: A \times A \to A$$

We denote the image of the pair $(a, b)$ under $*$ as $a * b$ rather than $*((a, b))$. The condition that the output must belong to $A$ for all inputs from $A \times A$ is called the closure property:

$$\forall a, b \in A \implies a * b \in A$$

Since a binary operation on $A$ is simply a function from $A \times A$ to $A$, if $|A| = n$, the cardinality of the domain is $|A \times A| = n^2$, and the cardinality of the codomain is $|A| = n$. Consequently, the total number of distinct binary operations on $A$ is:

$$N_{\text{binary operations}} = |A|^{|A \times A|} = n^{n^2}$$

7.2 Algebraic Properties of Binary Operations

1. Commutativity:

A binary operation $*$ on a set $A$ is commutative if the order of the operands does not affect the outcome:

$$\forall a, b \in A, \, a * b = b * a$$

Combinatorics of Commutative Operations: In an operation table (Cayley Table) of size $n \times n$:

  • There are $n$ diagonal entries of the form $a_i * a_i$. Each can be assigned any of the $n$ values in $A$ independently ($n^n$ choices).
  • There are $\frac{n^2 - n}{2}$ pairs of off-diagonal entries $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ with $i < j$. For commutativity, once $a_i * a_j$ is selected, $a_j * a_i$ is determined. Thus, there are $n$ choices for each of these $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ pairs.

The total number of commutative binary operations on a set of cardinality $n$ is:

$$N_{\text{commutative}} = n^n \cdot n^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = n^{n + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = n^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$

2. Associativity:

A binary operation $*$ on a set $A$ is associative if parentheses can be rearranged without altering the evaluated product:

$$\forall a, b, c \in A, \, (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$

Associativity permits the unambiguous evaluation of expressions like $a * b * c$ without grouping symbols. Standard subtraction on $\mathbb{R}$ is not associative: $(8 - 4) - 2 = 2$, but $8 - (4 - 2) = 6$.

3. Identity Element:

Let $*$ be a binary operation on $A$. An element $e \in A$ is an identity element with respect to $*$ if:

$$\forall a \in A, \, a * e = e * a = a$$

Uniqueness Theorem: If an identity element exists for a binary operation, it is unique.
Proof: Suppose $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identity elements in $A$ for $*$.

  • Treating $e_1$ as an identity element: $e_2 * e_1 = e_2$.
  • Treating $e_2$ as an identity element: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$.

If $*$ is commutative, or by applying the two-sided identity definition: $e_1 * e_2 = e_2$ and $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. Therefore:

$$e_1 = e_2$$

Hence, the identity element is unique. $\blacksquare$

4. Invertible Elements:

Let $*$ be a binary operation on $A$ with an identity element $e \in A$. An element $a \in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists an element $b \in A$ such that:

$$a * b = b * a = e$$

The element $b$ is called the inverse of $a$ and is typically denoted by $a^{-1}$.

Uniqueness of Inverse Theorem: If an associative binary operation $*$ has an identity $e$, and an element $a \in A$ is invertible, then its inverse is unique.
Proof: Suppose $b_1$ and $b_2$ are both inverses of $a$. Then $a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e$ and $a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$. Consider the product $b_1 * (a * b_2)$:

$$b_1 * (a * b_2) = b_1 * e = b_1$$

Using the associative property:

$$(b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$

Since $(b_1 * a) * b_2 = b_1 * (a * b_2)$, it follows that $b_1 = b_2$. Thus, the inverse is unique. $\blacksquare$

8. Analytical Edge Cases, Board Examination Pitfalls, and Nuances

8.1 Analysis of Piecewise Functions

Piecewise-defined functions are a common source of errors in Class 12 exams. Determining whether a piecewise function is injective or surjective requires checking not only the individual sub-functions, but also whether their ranges overlap.

Problem Case: Consider the mapping $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x + 1, & \text{if } x \ge 0 \\ x - 1, & \text{if } x < 0 \end{cases}$$

1. Injectivity Verification:

  • For $x \ge 0$, $f(x) \ge 1$. Over this domain, $f'(x) = 1 > 0$, so $f$ is strictly increasing.
  • For $x < 0$, $f(x) < -1$. Over this domain, $f'(x) = 1 > 0$, so $f$ is strictly increasing.
  • Check for overlap between the two branches: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f|_{x \ge 0}) = [1, \infty)$$ $$\operatorname{Ran}(f|_{x < 0}) = (-\infty, -1)$$ The intersection is empty: $[1, \infty) \cap (-\infty, -1) = \emptyset$. Because the branches do not share values, no two distinct inputs can yield the same output. Hence, $f$ is injective.

2. Surjectivity Verification:

  • The total range of $f$ is: $$\operatorname{Ran}(f) = (-\infty, -1) \cup [1, \infty)$$
  • The codomain is specified as $\mathbb{R}$.
  • Notice that the open interval $[-1, 1)$ is excluded from the range. For example, for $y = 0 \in \mathbb{R}$, there is no $x \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x) = 0$. Thus, $\operatorname{Ran}(f) \neq \text{Codomain}$, and $f$ is not surjective (it is an into function).

8.2 The Pitfall of the Transitivity Test

A common board examination error involves misidentifying transitivity in relations with non-overlapping pairs. Consider the following relation on the set $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$:

$$R = \{(1, 2), (3, 4)\}$$

To violate transitivity, there must exist pairs $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ such that $(a, c) \notin R$.

  • Pair $(1, 2)$ has second element $2$. There is no pair in $R$ of the form $(2, z)$.
  • Pair $(3, 4)$ has second element $4$. There is no pair in $R$ of the form $(4, z)$.

Since the hypothesis $[(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R]$ cannot be met anywhere in $R$, the transitivity implication is vacuously true. Hence, $R$ is transitive. Marking this relation as non-transitive is a frequent error.

8.3 Injectivity of Real Functions with Local Extrema

Consider a rational function such as $f: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ defined by:

$$f(x) = \frac{x - 3}{x - 2}$$

Injectivity Proof: Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ for $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$:

$$\frac{x_1 - 3}{x_1 - 2} = \frac{x_2 - 3}{x_2 - 2}$$ $$(x_1 - 3)(x_2 - 2) = (x_2 - 3)(x_1 - 2)$$ $$x_1 x_2 - 2x_1 - 3x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 2x_2 - 3x_1 + 6$$ $$-2x_1 - 3x_2 = -2x_2 - 3x_1$$ $$3x_1 - 2x_1 = 3x_2 - 2x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Thus, $f$ is strictly injective.

Surjectivity Proof: Let $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. We must find $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ such that $y = f(x)$:

$$y = \frac{x - 3}{x - 2} \implies y(x - 2) = x - 3 \implies yx - 2y = x - 3$$ $$yx - x = 2y - 3 \implies x(y - 1) = 2y - 3 \implies x = \frac{2y - 3}{y - 1}$$

Since $y \neq 1$, the denominator $y - 1 \neq 0$, so $x$ is well-defined. Next, we verify that $x \neq 2$:

$$\frac{2y - 3}{y - 1} = 2 \implies 2y - 3 = 2(y - 1) \implies 2y - 3 = 2y - 2 \implies -3 = -2 \quad \text{(Contradiction)}$$

Thus, $x$ can never equal $2$. For every $y$ in the codomain $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, there exists a valid $x = \frac{2y - 3}{y - 1}$ in the domain $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ such that $f(x) = y$. Hence, $f$ is surjective.

Because $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and invertible, with:

$$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{2y - 3}{y - 1} \quad \text{or} \quad f^{-1}(x) = \frac{2x - 3}{x - 1}$$

Derivation 1: Combinatorial Proof for Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

Objective: Let $A$ be a finite set with $|A| = n$. Derive the formulas for:

  1. The total number of relations on $A$.
  2. The total number of reflexive relations on $A$.
  3. The total number of symmetric relations on $A$.

Step 1: Total Number of Relations

A relation $R$ on $A$ is defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$:

$$R \subseteq A \times A$$

The cardinality of the Cartesian product is:

$$|A \times A| = |A| \cdot |A| = n \cdot n = n^2$$

The total number of subsets of a set of size $k$ is given by the power set size $2^k$. Substituting $k = n^2$:

$$N_R = 2^{n^2}$$

Step 2: Number of Reflexive Relations

A relation $R$ is reflexive if and only if $(a, a) \in R$ for all $a \in A$. We decompose the elements of $A \times A$ into two disjoint collections:

  1. Diagonal elements: $D = \{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\}$, where $|D| = n$.
  2. Off-diagonal elements: $O = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \ne j, 1 \le i, j \le n\}$, where $|O| = n^2 - n$.

For $R$ to be reflexive:

  • Every element in $D$ must be included in $R$. Thus, each diagonal element has exactly $1$ choice (must be present). Number of choices for diagonal elements:
    • $$\prod_{i=1}^{n} 1 = 1^n = 1$$
  • Each off-diagonal element in $O$ can either be included or excluded from $R$. Thus, each has $2$ independent choices. Number of choices:
    • $$2^{|O|} = 2^{n^2 - n} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

By the fundamental counting principle, the total number of reflexive relations is:

$$N_{\text{ref}} = 1 \times 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

Step 3: Number of Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ is symmetric if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$ for all $i, j \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$.

We partition the $n^2$ entries of $A \times A$ into independent groups:

  • The $n$ diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_i)$. Each pair can either be in $R$ or not (2 choices each). Total choices: $2^n$.
  • The remaining $n^2 - n$ off-diagonal pairs, which can be grouped into unordered pairs of symmetric coordinates:
    • $$\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\} \quad \text{for } 1 \le i < j \le n$$

    The total number of such unordered pairs is:

    $$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

    For each pair $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, by symmetry, both elements must either be simultaneously present in $R$ or simultaneously absent from $R$. Hence, there are $2$ choices per pair. Total choices for off-diagonal elements:

    $$2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$

Multiplying the independent choices:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Derivation 2: Enumeration of Injective (One-to-One) Functions

Objective: Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$. Prove that the number of injective functions $f: A \to B$ is given by:

$$N_{\text{inj}} = \begin{cases} \frac{n!}{(n-m)!} = P(n, m), & n \ge m \\ 0, & n < m \end{cases}$$

Proof:

Let $A = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\}$ and $B = \{y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n\}$. A function $f: A \to B$ is injective if:

$$x_i \ne x_j \implies f(x_i) \ne f(x_j) \quad \forall i, j \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\}$$

Case 1 ($n < m$):

By the Pigeonhole Principle, if $m$ elements are mapped to $n$ target elements with $m > n$, at least two distinct elements in $A$ must map to the same element in $B$. Thus, no injective function exists:

$$N_{\text{inj}} = 0 \quad \text{for } n < m$$

Case 2 ($n \ge m$):

We construct an injection by assigning distinct images sequentially:

  • For the element $x_1 \in A$, there are $n$ possible choices from $B$.
  • For the element $x_2 \in A$, to preserve injectivity, $f(x_2) \ne f(x_1)$. Thus, there are $(n - 1)$ remaining choices.
  • For the element $x_3 \in A$, $f(x_3) \notin \{f(x_1), f(x_2)\}$, leaving $(n - 2)$ choices.
  • Continuing this process up to the $k$-th element, $x_k$ has $(n - (k - 1)) = (n - k + 1)$ choices.
  • For the final element $x_m$, there are $(n - m + 1)$ available choices.

Applying the Multiplication Principle of counting:

$$N_{\text{inj}} = n \cdot (n - 1) \cdot (n - 2) \cdots (n - m + 1)$$

Multiplying and dividing by $(n - m)!$:

$$N_{\text{inj}} = \frac{n(n - 1)(n - 2)\cdots(n - m + 1) \cdot (n - m)!}{(n - m)!} = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!} = {}^n P_m$$

Corollary (Bijective Functions where $m = n$):

$$N_{\text{bij}} = {}^n P_n = \frac{n!}{(n - n)!} = \frac{n!}{0!} = n!$$

Derivation 3: Number of Surjective (Onto) Functions via Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

Objective: For $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ with $m \ge n$, prove that the number of surjective functions $f: A \to B$ is:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-r} \binom{n}{r} r^m = n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m - \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}1^m$$

Proof:

Let $U$ be the universal set of all functions from $A$ to $B$. The total cardinality is:

$$|U| = n^m$$

Let $B = \{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n\}$. For each $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, define the condition $P_i$ that the element $b_i$ is not in the range of $f$. Let $A_i$ be the set of functions missing element $b_i$:

$$A_i = \{f: A \to B \mid b_i \notin \text{Range}(f)\}$$

A function $f$ is surjective if and only if it satisfies none of the properties $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$. Therefore, the number of surjective functions is:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = |A_1^c \cap A_2^c \cap \dots \cap A_n^c| = |U| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right|$$

By the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE):

$$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right| = \sum_{1 \le i \le n} |A_i| - \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} |A_i \cap A_j| + \sum_{1 \le i < j < k \le n} |A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k| - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} |A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_n|$$

Evaluating each term:

  • For $|A_i|$, the target set excludes $b_i$, leaving $(n - 1)$ choices for each of the $m$ elements of $A$. Hence, $|A_i| = (n - 1)^m$. There are $\binom{n}{1}$ such sets:
    • $$\sum |A_i| = \binom{n}{1} (n - 1)^m$$
  • For $|A_i \cap A_j|$, the target set excludes 2 specific elements, leaving $(n - 2)$ choices. There are $\binom{n}{2}$ such intersections:
    • $$\sum |A_i \cap A_j| = \binom{n}{2} (n - 2)^m$$
  • In general, for $k$ excluded elements, the functions map into $n - k$ elements, with $\binom{n}{k}$ combinations:
    • $$S_k = \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Substituting these back into the PIE expression:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = n^m - \left[ \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m - \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m + \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}1^m \right]$$ $$N_{\text{surj}} = \binom{n}{0}n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m - \dots + (-1)^n \binom{n}{n}(0)^m$$

Expressing in compact summation form by setting $r = n - k$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-r} \binom{n}{r} r^m$$

Derivation 4: Invertibility Criterion and the Reverse Order Law $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be invertible functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and its inverse is given by:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Proof:

A mapping $h: X \to Y$ is invertible with inverse $h^{-1}: Y \to X$ if and only if:

$$h^{-1} \circ h = I_X \quad \text{and} \quad h \circ h^{-1} = I_Y$$

Here, $f$ and $g$ are invertible, meaning there exist unique mappings $f^{-1}: B \to A$ and $g^{-1}: C \to B$ such that:

$$f^{-1} \circ f = I_A, \quad f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$$ $$g^{-1} \circ g = I_B, \quad g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

We propose that the inverse candidate for $h = (g \circ f)$ is $k = (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}): C \to A$. We must prove two conditions:

Condition 1: Left Inverse Verification ($k \circ h = I_A$)

$$k \circ h = (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)$$

By associativity of function composition:

$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ \left( g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f) \right)$$ $$= f^{-1} \circ \left( (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f \right)$$

Substitute $g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$:

$$= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f)$$

Since $I_B \circ f = f$ (identity property):

$$= f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$

Condition 2: Right Inverse Verification ($h \circ k = I_C$)

$$h \circ k = (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})$$

By associativity:

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ \left( f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \right)$$ $$= g \circ \left( (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} \right)$$

Substitute $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$:

$$= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1})$$

Since $I_B \circ g^{-1} = g^{-1}$:

$$= g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$

Conclusion: Since $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$ and $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$, the inverse of $(g \circ f)$ exists and is uniquely given by:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Derivation 5: Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations (Partition Theorem)

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. Prove that:

  1. For any $a, b \in A$, $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
  2. Any two equivalence classes are either identical or disjoint: $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$ or $[a] = [b]$.
  3. The collection of all equivalence classes forms a partition of $A$: $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$.

Proof of Part 1 ($[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$):

($\implies$) Assume $[a] = [b]$. By reflexivity of $R$, $(a, a) \in R \implies a \in [a]$. Since $[a] = [b]$, it follows that $a \in [b]$. By definition of $[b]$, this implies $(a, b) \in R$.

($\impliedby$) Assume $(a, b) \in R$. Let $x \in [a]$ be arbitrary.

$$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R$$

Since $(x, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$, by transitivity of $R$:

$$(x, b) \in R \implies x \in [b]$$

Therefore, $[a] \subseteq [b]$. Conversely, because $(a, b) \in R$, symmetry gives $(b, a) \in R$. By symmetric reasoning, if $y \in [b]$, then $(y, b) \in R$ and $(b, a) \in R \implies (y, a) \in R \implies y \in [a]$, so $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Mutual inclusion proves:

$$[a] = [b]$$

Proof of Part 2 (Disjoint or Identical):

Let $[a]$ and $[b]$ be two equivalence classes. We show that if $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$, then $[a] = [b]$.

Assume $[a] \cap [b] \ne \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $c \in A$ such that:

$$c \in [a] \cap [b] \implies c \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad c \in [b]$$ $$\implies (c, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (c, b) \in R$$

By symmetry of $R$, $(c, a) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$. Now apply transitivity:

$$(a, c) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (c, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R$$

From Part 1, $(a, b) \in R \implies [a] = [b]$. Therefore, two equivalence classes cannot partially overlap: they are either strictly disjoint or identical.

Proof of Part 3 (Union Exhausts Set $A$):

For every $a \in A$, by reflexivity, $(a, a) \in R$, which implies $a \in [a]$. Therefore:

$$A = \bigcup_{a \in A} \{a\} \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a]$$

Furthermore, since every equivalence class $[a]$ is defined as a subset of $A$, $[a] \subseteq A$ for all $a \in A$, which implies $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A$. By double containment:

$$\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$

Hence, the family of distinct equivalence classes forms a complete partition of $A$. $\blacksquare$
