Comprehensive Theory

1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Epistemology & Cartesian Set Theory The systematic formalization of modern mathematics rests upon axiomatic set theory. Within the senior secondary CBSE curriculum, Chapter 1 (“Relations and Functions”) represents the structural bridge between elementary discrete set operations and the continuous machinery of real and functional analysis. To rigorously comprehend the behavioral taxonomy of mappings, one must first construct the primary space upon which binary associations exist: the Cartesian Product of sets.

Let $A$ and $B$ be two non-empty sets defined within a universal universe of discourse $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian Product, denoted by $A \times B$, is defined as the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate belongs to $A$ and the second coordinate belongs to $B$. Formally:
$$A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$ The “ordered” nature of the pair $(a, b)$ is essential: $(a, b) = (c, d) \iff (a = c \land b = d)$. Consequently, if $A \neq B$, then $A \times B \neq B \times A$, demonstrating that the Cartesian product is non-commutative. From a combinatorial standpoint, if the cardinalities of the sets are finite, denoted by $|A| = n(A) = p$ and $|B| = n(B) = q$, the cardinality of their Cartesian space is multiplicative:
$$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = p \cdot q$$ The power set $\mathcal{P}(A \times B)$ represents the collection of all subsets of $A \times B$. By definition, any arbitrary subset $R \subseteq A \times B$ constitutes a binary relation from $A$ to $B$. When the source set and the target set coincide, that is, $R \subseteq A \times A$, we state that $R$ is a binary relation on the set $A$ (or an endo-relation on $A$). The total number of distinct relations that can be defined on a finite set $A$ with $|A| = n$ corresponds directly to the cardinality of the power set of $A \times A$:
$$N_{\text{rel}} = |\mathcal{P}(A \times A)| = 2^{|A \times A|} = 2^{n^2}$$ This exponential explosion reveals the vast structural diversity of relations, ranging from completely unstructured collections of ordered pairs to highly constrained structural isomorphisms that preserve topological, algebraic, or geometric invariants.

2. The Tripartite Taxonomy of Binary Relations Let $A$ be a non-empty set and $R \subseteq A \times A$. The elements of $R$ are denoted interchangeably by the set-membership syntax $(x, y) \in R$ or the infix relational notation $x R y$. Conversely, $(x, y) \notin R \iff x \not R y$. Before establishing the core equivalence triad, we demarcate the trivial and identity relations:

I. The Empty (Void) Relation: The empty set $\emptyset$ is a subset of every set, hence $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $\Phi = \emptyset$ is termed the empty relation on $A$. It possesses zero elements: $\forall x, y \in A, (x, y) \notin \Phi$.

II. The Universal Relation: The full Cartesian product $A \times A$ is trivially a subset of itself: $(A \times A) \subseteq A \times A$. The relation $U = A \times A$ is the universal relation on $A$. Every element in $A$ is related to every element in $A$: $\forall x, y \in A, (x, y) \in U$. Both the empty and universal relations are collectively termed trivial relations.

III. The Identity Relation: Denoted by $I_A$ (or $\Delta_A$, the diagonal relation), this is defined strictly as:
$$I_A = \{(x, x) : x \in A\}$$ In $I_A$, every element is related solely and exclusively to itself. If an element $a \in A$ is related to any distinct element $b \neq a$, the relation ceases to be an identity relation.

To establish algebraic equivalence and order structures, Class 12 mathematics focuses on three core axiomatic properties of a relation $R$ on a set $A$:

Axiom 1: Reflexivity
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is said to be reflexive if every element of $A$ is related to itself. Formally:
$$\forall x \in A \implies (x, x) \in R$$ Critical Examiner Distinction: Contrast the identity relation $I_A$ with reflexivity. For $R$ to be reflexive, the diagonal pairs must be present: $I_A \subseteq R$. However, $R$ may contain non-diagonal pairs $(x, y)$ with $x \neq y$ without violating reflexivity. Identity requires $R = I_A$, whereas reflexivity requires $I_A \subseteq R$. Hence, every identity relation is reflexive, but a reflexive relation is not necessarily the identity relation.

Axiom 2: Symmetry
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is said to be symmetric if the existence of an ordered pair implies the existence of its transposed counterpart. Formally:
$$\forall x, y \in A, \quad (x, y) \in R \implies (y, x) \in R$$ In terms of the inverse relation $R^{-1} = \{(y, x) : (x, y) \in R\}$, the condition of symmetry is succinctly represented by the equality:
$$R = R^{-1}$$ If there exists even a single pair $(a, b) \in R$ such that $(b, a) \notin R$, the symmetry condition is immediately falsified. Note that symmetry is an implication ($P \implies Q$); if the antecedent $(x, y) \in R$ is never triggered for distinct elements, the condition holds vacuously.

Axiom 3: Transitivity
A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is said to be transitive if, whenever an element $x$ is related to $y$ and that same $y$ is related to $z$, it must follow that $x$ is related to $z$. Formally:
$$\forall x, y, z \in A, \quad [(x, y) \in R \land (y, z) \in R] \implies (x, z) \in R$$ The Analytical Pitfall of Vacuous Transitivity: Many candidates fail examination questions regarding transitivity because they misunderstand the conditional logic of the material conditional $(P \land Q) \implies S$. The requirement $(x, z) \in R$ is activated only when the compound antecedent $[(x, y) \in R \text{ and } (y, z) \in R]$ is simultaneously satisfied. If there exist no pairs $(x, y)$ and $(y, z)$ sharing an intermediate element $y$ with $x \neq y$ or $y \neq z$, the antecedent is false, rendering the conditional statement logically true. For instance, consider $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $R = \{(1, 2)\}$. Here, $(1, 2) \in R$, but there is no pair of the form $(2, z)$ in $R$. Hence, the antecedent condition is never satisfied, and the relation $R$ is vacuously transitive.

Definition of an Equivalence Relation:
A relation $R$ defined on a non-empty set $A$ is classified as an Equivalence Relation if and only if it simultaneously satisfies all three axioms:
$$\text{Equivalence}(R) \iff \text{Reflexive}(R) \land \text{Symmetric}(R) \land \text{Transitive}(R)$$
Theorem 1.1: Let $R$ and $S$ be two equivalence relations on a set $A$. Prove that the intersection $R \cap S$ is also an equivalence relation on $A$.

Proof:
1. Reflexivity: Let $a \in A$ be arbitrary. Since $R$ is an equivalence relation on $A$, $R$ is reflexive, which implies $(a, a) \in R$. Similarly, since $S$ is an equivalence relation on $A$, $S$ is reflexive, which implies $(a, a) \in S$. By the set-theoretic definition of intersection, $(a, a) \in R \land (a, a) \in S \implies (a, a) \in R \cap S$. Thus, $R \cap S$ is reflexive on $A$.

2. Symmetry: Let $(a, b) \in R \cap S$. This implies that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in S$. Since $R$ is symmetric, $(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R$. Since $S$ is symmetric, $(a, b) \in S \implies (b, a) \in S$. Consequently, $(b, a) \in R \land (b, a) \in S \implies (b, a) \in R \cap S$. Therefore, $R \cap S$ is symmetric.

3. Transitivity: Let $(a, b) \in R \cap S$ and $(b, c) \in R \cap S$. By definition of intersection: $[(a, b) \in R \land (a, b) \in S]$ and $[(b, c) \in R \land (b, c) \in S]$. Rearranging via logical conjunction commutativity gives: $[(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R]$ and $[(a, b) \in S \land (b, c) \in S]$. Because $R$ is transitive, $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R$. Because $S$ is transitive, $(a, b) \in S \land (b, c) \in S \implies (a, c) \in S$. Since $(a, c) \in R$ and $(a, c) \in S$, it follows that $(a, c) \in R \cap S$. Hence, $R \cap S$ is transitive.

Since $R \cap S$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, it is an equivalence relation on $A$. $\blacksquare$
Crucial Counterexample on Union: The union of two equivalence relations $R \cup S$ is not necessarily an equivalence relation. Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Define $R = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2), (2, 1)\}$ and $S = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (2, 3), (3, 2)\}$. Both $R$ and $S$ are equivalence relations. Then:
$$R \cup S = \{(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2)\}$$ Observe that $(1, 2) \in R \cup S$ and $(2, 3) \in R \cup S$. If $R \cup S$ were transitive, it would require $(1, 3) \in R \cup S$. However, $(1, 3) \notin R \cup S$. Hence, transitivity fails, and $R \cup S$ is not an equivalence relation.

3. Equivalence Classes, Partitions, and the Fundamental Theorem Equivalence relations serve as an abstract mathematical framework for categorizing objects by identifying distinct elements that share common structural properties. This classification process is formalized through Equivalence Classes.

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation defined on a non-empty set $A$. Let $a \in A$. The equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$, or $cl(a)$, or $\bar{a}$, is the subset of $A$ consisting of all elements $x \in A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$:
$$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$ Because $R$ is symmetric, $(x, a) \in R \iff (a, x) \in R$, so $[a] = \{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

Fundamental Properties of Equivalence Classes:
1. Non-Emptiness: For every $a \in A$, $[a] \neq \emptyset$, because reflexivity guarantees $(a, a) \in R \implies a \in [a]$.
2. Identity of Classes: For any $a, b \in A$, $[a] = [b] \iff (a, b) \in R$.
3. Disjointness: For any $a, b \in A$, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. Two equivalence classes are either identical or completely disjoint.
4. Exhaustive Union: The union of all equivalence classes reconstructs the original set $A$: $\bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$.

Theorem 1.2: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on $A$. Prove that any two equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either disjoint or identical, i.e., $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \implies [a] = [b]$.

Proof: Assume $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists at least one element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a] \cap [b]$.
By definition of intersection: $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$.
$x \in [a] \implies (x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$ (by symmetry).
$x \in [b] \implies (x, b) \in R$.
Since $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, by transitivity of $R$, we obtain:
$$(a, b) \in R$$ Now, let $y \in [a]$ be an arbitrary element.
$y \in [a] \implies (y, a) \in R$.
We already established $(a, b) \in R$. By transitivity, $(y, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (y, b) \in R$.
$(y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$. Since $y$ was arbitrary, $[a] \subseteq [b]$.

Conversely, let $z \in [b]$ be an arbitrary element.
$z \in [b] \implies (z, b) \in R$.
Since $(a, b) \in R$, by symmetry, $(b, a) \in R$.
Applying transitivity: $(z, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R \implies (z, a) \in R$.
$(z, a) \in R \implies z \in [a]$. Since $z$ was arbitrary, $[b] \subseteq [a]$.

By mutual subset inclusion ($[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a]$), we conclude $[a] = [b]$. Thus, if two equivalence classes share even a single element, they are identical. $\blacksquare$
The Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations:
An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ partitions $A$ into a family of mutually disjoint subsets $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ (where each $A_i$ is an equivalence class) such that:
(i) $A_i \neq \emptyset \quad \forall i \in I$,
(ii) $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset \quad \forall i \neq j$, and
(iii) $\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = A$.
Conversely, any arbitrary partition $\mathcal{P} = \{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of $A$ uniquely induces an equivalence relation $R$ on $A$ defined by $(x, y) \in R \iff \exists i \in I \text{ such that } x, y \in A_i$.

Canonical Example (Congruence Modulo $m$): Let $m \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. Define a relation on $\mathbb{Z}$ by:
$$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b) \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } a - b = k \cdot m$$ This relation is reflexive ($a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot m$), symmetric ($a - b = km \implies b - a = (-k)m$), and transitive ($a - b = k_1 m \land b - c = k_2 m \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)m$). The resulting equivalence classes are the residue classes modulo $m$:
$$[0] = \{\dots, -2m, -m, 0, m, 2m, \dots\}$$ $$[1] = \{\dots, -2m+1, -m+1, 1, m+1, 2m+1, \dots\}$$ $$\dots$$ $$[m-1] = \{\dots, -m-1, -1, m-1, 2m-1, \dots\}$$ These $m$ distinct sets are pairwise disjoint, and their union is $\mathbb{Z}$, forming the quotient set $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$.

4. Combinatorics of Relations on Finite Sets Let $A$ be a finite set of cardinality $|A| = n$. The total number of elements in the Cartesian product $A \times A$ is $n^2$. These $n^2$ pairs can be partitioned into:
- Diagonal pairs: $\{(a_i, a_i) : i = 1, 2, \dots, n\}$, exactly $n$ pairs.
- Non-diagonal pairs: $\{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j\}$, exactly $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$ pairs.

I. Total Number of Relations: Each of the $n^2$ pairs can either be included or excluded:
$$N_{\text{total}} = 2^{n^2}$$
II. Number of Reflexive Relations: For a relation to be reflexive, all $n$ diagonal pairs $(a_i, a_i)$ must be included (1 choice each). The remaining $n(n - 1)$ non-diagonal pairs can be independently included or excluded (2 choices each):
$$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 1^n \times 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n^2 - n}$$
III. Number of Symmetric Relations: The $n$ diagonal pairs can be included or excluded arbitrarily ($2^n$ choices). The $n(n - 1)$ off-diagonal elements form $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ unordered pairs of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$. For symmetry, both must be present or both must be absent (2 choices per pair):
$$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$
IV. Number of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations: All $n$ diagonal pairs must be present (1 choice). The $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ symmetric pairs off the diagonal have 2 choices each:
$$N_{\text{ref} \land \text{sym}} = 1^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$
V. Number of Equivalence Relations (Bell Numbers): The enumeration of equivalence relations on a set of size $n$ equals the number of partitions of an $n$-element set, given by the $n$-th Bell Number $B_n$, which satisfies the recurrence:
$$B_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} B_k, \quad \text{with } B_0 = 1$$ For small values: $B_1 = 1$, $B_2 = 2$, $B_3 = 5$, $B_4 = 15$, $B_5 = 52$. For $n = 3$ with $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, the 5 equivalence relations correspond to partitions: $1 \text{ partition of type } \{\{1, 2, 3\}\}$, $3 \text{ partitions of type } \{\{a, b\}, \{c\}\}$, and $1 \text{ partition of type } \{\{1\}, \{2\}, \{3\}\}$.

5. The Theory of Functions: Injective, Surjective, and Bijective Mappings A function (or mapping) $f$ from a set $A$ into a set $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, is a specialized binary relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ governed by two strict existence and uniqueness conditions:
1. Totality (Domain Exhaustion): Every element of the domain must possess an image:
$$\forall x \in A, \quad \exists y \in B \quad \text{such that } (x, y) \in f$$ 2. Uniqueness (Well-Definedness): No element of the domain can map to multiple distinct elements in the codomain:
$$\forall x \in A \text{ and } \forall y_1, y_2 \in B, \quad [(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f] \implies y_1 = y_2$$ When $(x, y) \in f$, we write $y = f(x)$, where $y$ is the image of $x$ under $f$, and $x$ is a pre-image of $y$. $A$ is the Domain, $B$ is the Codomain, and the set of all evaluated images forms the Range:
$$\text{Range}(f) = f(A) = \{f(x) : x \in A\} \subseteq B$$
Classification of Functions:

I. Injective (One-to-One) Mappings:
A function $f: A \to B$ is termed injective (or an injection) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the codomain. Formally:
$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$ In analytical practice, proofs employ the contrapositive formulation:
$$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$ If a function is not injective, it is classified as many-to-one:
$$\exists x_1, x_2 \in A \quad \text{such that } x_1 \neq x_2 \land f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$
Calculus-Based Injection Criterion: Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a differentiable function defined on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. If the derivative $f'(x)$ satisfies either:
(i) $f'(x) > 0 \quad \forall x \in I$ (strictly monotonically increasing), or
(ii) $f'(x) < 0 \quad \forall x \in I$ (strictly monotonically decreasing),
then $f$ is strictly monotonic on $I$, and therefore $f$ is injective on $I$. (Isolated points where $f'(x) = 0$ do not compromise injectivity provided the derivative does not vanish identically across any sub-interval).

The Horizontal Line Test (Geometric Interpretation): Let $y = f(x)$ be graphed in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. The function $f$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = k$ (where $k \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the curve at most once. If any horizontal line intersects the curve at two or more points, those intersection points share the same output $y$ for multiple inputs $x$, violating injectivity.

II. Surjective (Onto) Mappings:
A function $f: A \to B$ is termed surjective (or a surjection) if every element of the codomain possesses at least one pre-image in the domain. Formally:
$$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \quad \text{such that } f(x) = y$$ In set-theoretic terms, surjectivity is equivalent to:
$$\text{Range}(f) = B$$ If $\text{Range}(f) \subsetneq B$, the function is termed into. An into function leaves at least one element $y \in B$ with no pre-image: $\exists y \in B$ such that $f^{-1}(\{y\}) = \emptyset$.

Analytical Procedure for Proving Surjectivity:
Step 1: Set $y = f(x)$, where $y$ represents an arbitrary element of the codomain $B$.
Step 2: Algebraically solve this equation for $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
Step 3: Rigorously verify that for every $y \in B$, the value $g(y)$ is defined and $g(y) \in A$.
Step 4: Substitute $g(y)$ back into $f$: $f(g(y))$ must evaluate identically to $y$. This confirms that $x = g(y)$ is a valid pre-image.

III. Bijective (One-to-One and Onto) Mappings:
A function $f: A \to B$ is termed bijective (or a bijection, or a one-to-one correspondence) if and only if it is simultaneously injective and surjective:
$$\text{Bijective}(f) \iff \text{Injective}(f) \land \text{Surjective}(f)$$ A bijection establishes a perfect pairing between the elements of $A$ and $B$.

Cardinality Constraints for Finite Sets:
Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.
- If there exists an injection $f: A \to B$, then $m \le n$. (Pigeonhole Principle).
- If there exists a surjection $f: A \to B$, then $m \ge n$.
- If there exists a bijection $f: A \to B$, then $m = n$.

6. Combinatorial Enumeration of Mappings Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

1. Total Number of Functions: Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ can be mapped independently to any of the $n$ elements in $B$:
$$N_{\text{functions}} = n^m$$
2. Number of Injective Functions:
- If $m > n$, no injection can exist: $N_{\text{injective}} = 0$.
- If $m \le n$, the first element of $A$ has $n$ choices, the second has $n - 1$ choices, and the $m$-th has $n - m + 1$ choices:
$$N_{\text{injective}} = P(n, m) = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}$$
3. Number of Bijective Functions:
- If $m \neq n$, no bijection can exist: $N_{\text{bijective}} = 0$.
- If $m = n$, the number of bijections corresponds to permutations of $n$ elements:
$$N_{\text{bijective}} = n!$$
4. Number of Surjective Functions:
- If $m < n$, no surjection can exist: $N_{\text{surjective}} = 0$.
- If $m \ge n$, we count surjections using the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE) or Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind $S(m, n)$:
$$N_{\text{surjective}} = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{k} \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$ Concrete Application: Count the number of onto functions from $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ ($m = 4$) to $B = \{a, b, 3\}$ ($n = 3$):
$$N_{\text{surjective}} = \binom{3}{0}3^4 - \binom{3}{1}2^4 + \binom{3}{2}1^4 - \binom{3}{3}0^4 = 1(81) - 3(16) + 3(1) - 0 = 81 - 48 + 3 = 36$$
7. Algebra and Composition of Functions Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions such that the codomain of $f$ matches the domain of $g$ (or more generally, $\text{Range}(f) \subseteq \text{Domain}(g)$). The composite function, denoted by $g \circ f: A \to C$ (read “$g$ circle $f$” or “$g$ composed with $f$”), is defined by:
$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$
Core Theorems on Composition:

Theorem 1.3 (Associativity of Function Composition): Let $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$ be functions. Prove that $h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$.

Proof: First, observe the domain and codomain structures:
$g \circ f: A \to C \implies h \circ (g \circ f): A \to D$.
$h \circ g: B \to D \implies (h \circ g) \circ f: A \to D$.
Both composite functions have the same domain $A$ and codomain $D$.
Now, let $x \in A$ be arbitrary:
$$[h \circ (g \circ f)](x) = h((g \circ f)(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ $$[(h \circ g) \circ f](x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h(g(f(x)))$$ Since $[h \circ (g \circ f)](x) = [(h \circ g) \circ f](x)$ for all $x \in A$, the mappings are identical:
$$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f \quad \blacksquare$$
Non-Commutativity of Composition: In general, composition is non-commutative: $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$. In fact, if $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ with $A \neq C$, the expression $f \circ g$ is not even defined, since the domain of $f$ ($A$) does not equal the codomain of $g$ ($C$). Even when $A = B = C$, commutativity fails in general. For example, let $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$:
$$(g \circ f)(x) = g(2x + 1) = (2x + 1)^2 = 4x^2 + 4x + 1$$ $$(f \circ g)(x) = f(x^2) = 2x^2 + 1$$ Clearly, $4x^2 + 4x + 1 \neq 2x^2 + 1$, confirming $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$.

Theorem 1.4: Preservation and Deduction of Mapping Characteristics:
Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$.
(a) If $f$ and $g$ are injective, then $g \circ f$ is injective.
(b) If $f$ and $g$ are surjective, then $g \circ f$ is surjective.
(c) If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ must be injective (though $g$ may not be).
(d) If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ must be surjective (though $f$ may not be).

Proof of (a): Assume $f, g$ are injective. Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
$\implies g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$.
Since $g$ is injective, $g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$. Hence:
$f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
Since $f$ is injective, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$.
Therefore, $g \circ f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

Proof of (c): Assume $g \circ f$ is injective. Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$.
$\implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
Because $g \circ f$ is injective, this implies $x_1 = x_2$.
Thus, $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, so $f$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

Counterexample illustrating that $g$ need not be injective in (c): Let $A = \{1, 2\}$, $B = \{a, b, c\}$, $C = \{x, y\}$. Define $f = \{(1, a), (2, b)\}$ and $g = \{(a, x), (b, y), (c, y)\}$. Then $g \circ f = \{(1, x), (2, y)\}$, which is clearly injective. However, $g(b) = g(c) = y$ with $b \neq c$, showing that $g$ is not injective.
8. Invertible Functions & The Inversion Algorithm A function $f: A \to B$ is said to be invertible if there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that:
$$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$ where $I_A: A \to A$ ($I_A(x) = x$) and $I_B: B \to B$ ($I_B(y) = y$) are the identity mappings on $A$ and $B$, respectively. The function $g$ is called the inverse of $f$ and is uniquely denoted by:
$$g = f^{-1}$$
Theorem 1.5 (The Fundamental Characterization of Invertibility):
A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijection (both injective and surjective).

Proof ($\implies$ Part: Invertibility implies Bijectivity):
Assume $f: A \to B$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.
1. Injectivity of $f$: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
Apply $g$ to both sides: $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \implies (g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$.
Since $g \circ f = I_A$, we have $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ is injective.
2. Surjectivity of $f$: Let $y \in B$ be arbitrary. We must find $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we evaluate at $y$: $(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$.
Let $x = g(y)$. Since $g: B \to A$, $x \in A$.
Then $f(x) = f(g(y)) = y$. Thus, a pre-image $x$ exists for every $y \in B$, so $f$ is surjective.
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Proof ($\impliedby$ Part: Bijectivity implies Invertibility):
Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.
Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
Since $f$ is injective, this element $x \in A$ is unique: if $f(x_1) = y$ and $f(x_2) = y$, then $x_1 = x_2$.
Thus, we can define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element in $A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
Now, verify the identity conditions:
- For any $x \in A$, let $y = f(x)$. By definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$.
Substituting gives $g(f(x)) = x \implies (g \circ f)(x) = x = I_A(x) \implies g \circ f = I_A$.
- For any $y \in B$, let $x = g(y)$. By definition of $g$, this means $f(x) = y$.
Substituting gives $f(g(y)) = y \implies (f \circ g)(y) = y = I_B(y) \implies f \circ g = I_B$.
Therefore, $g$ is the inverse of $f$, and $f$ is invertible with $g = f^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$
Uniqueness and Inversion Laws:
1. Uniqueness: The inverse of a bijective function is unique. If $g_1$ and $g_2$ both satisfy the definition of an inverse for $f$, then:
$$g_1 = g_1 \circ I_B = g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_A \circ g_2 = g_2$$ 2. Involution: $(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$.
3. Reversal Law: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are invertible, then $g \circ f$ is invertible, and:
$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ Proof of Reversal Law:
$$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ $$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$ Since $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ acts as the two-sided identity inverse for $g \circ f$, by uniqueness of the inverse, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

9. Theory of Binary Operations A binary operation $*$ on a non-empty set $A$ is a function that maps every ordered pair of elements from $A \times A$ to a unique element in $A$:
$$*: A \times A \to A, \quad \text{denoted by } *(a, b) = a * b$$ The condition that $a * b \in A$ for all $a, b \in A$ represents the Closure Property. If the output falls outside $A$, $*$ is not a valid binary operation on $A$.

Structural Axioms:

I. Commutativity: A binary operation $*$ on $A$ is commutative if:
$$\forall a, b \in A, \quad a * b = b * a$$ II. Associativity: A binary operation $*$ on $A$ is associative if:
$$\forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$$ Associativity ensures that parenthesization is unambiguous, allowing expressions like $a * b * c$ to be written without parentheses.

III. Existence of Identity Element: An element $e \in A$ is an identity element for the binary operation $*$ if:
$$\forall a \in A, \quad a * e = e * a = a$$ Uniqueness Theorem: If an identity element exists for $*$, it is unique.
Proof: Assume $e_1$ and $e_2$ are both identity elements for $*$ on $A$. Since $e_1$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_2$. Since $e_2$ is an identity: $e_1 * e_2 = e_1$. Therefore, $e_1 = e_2$.

IV. Existence of Inverse Element: Let $*$ have an identity element $e \in A$. An element $b \in A$ is called the inverse of an element $a \in A$ with respect to $*$ if:
$$a * b = b * a = e$$ If such a $b$ exists, $a$ is termed invertible, and we write $b = a^{-1}$.
Uniqueness Theorem for Inverses: If $*$ is associative and an element $a \in A$ has an inverse, this inverse is unique.
Proof: Suppose $b_1$ and $b_2$ are both inverses of $a$. Then $a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e$ and $a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$. Using associativity:
$$b_1 = b_1 * e = b_1 * (a * b_2) = (b_1 * a) * b_2 = e * b_2 = b_2$$ Thus, $b_1 = b_2$.

Cayley Tables (Composition Tables):
For a finite set $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n\}$, a binary operation $*$ can be represented by an $n \times n$ matrix whose $(i, j)$-th entry is $a_i * a_j$.
- Commutativity Criterion: The operation $*$ is commutative if and only if the Cayley table is symmetric about its main diagonal (i.e., the table equals its transpose).
- Identity Criterion: An element $a_k$ is the identity if its corresponding row matches the top header row and its column matches the left header column.
- Invertibility Criterion: If an identity $e$ exists, an element $a_i$ is invertible if and only if the symbol $e$ appears in row $i$ and column $i$ symmetrically.

Exemplar Board Problem 1.1: Let $A = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{3\}$ and $B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. Consider the function $f: A \to B$ defined by:
$$f(x) = \frac{x - 2}{x - 3}$$ Prove that $f$ is bijective, and find its inverse function $f^{-1}$.

Part I: Injectivity Proof
Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$.
$$\frac{x_1 - 2}{x_1 - 3} = \frac{x_2 - 2}{x_2 - 3}$$ Cross-multiplying across non-zero denominators ($x_1 \neq 3, x_2 \neq 3$):
$$(x_1 - 2)(x_2 - 3) = (x_2 - 2)(x_1 - 3)$$ $$x_1 x_2 - 3x_1 - 2x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 3x_2 - 2x_1 + 6$$ Subtracting the common terms $(x_1 x_2 + 6)$ from both sides:
$$-3x_1 - 2x_2 = -3x_2 - 2x_1$$ Rearranging terms:
$$-3x_1 + 2x_1 = -3x_2 + 2x_2 \implies -x_1 = -x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Therefore, $f$ is injective (one-to-one).

Part II: Surjectivity Proof
Let $y \in B = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ be arbitrary. We seek an element $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$:
$$\frac{x - 2}{x - 3} = y$$ $$x - 2 = y(x - 3) = yx - 3y$$ $$x - yx = 2 - 3y \implies x(1 - y) = 2 - 3y$$ Since $y \in B$, $y \neq 1$, which guarantees $1 - y \neq 0$. Dividing both sides yields:
$$x = \frac{2 - 3y}{1 - y} = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}$$ We must verify two domain conditions for $x$:
1. Is $x$ real? Since $y \in \mathbb{R}$ and $y \neq 1$, $x$ is real.
2. Does $x \in A$? (i.e., is $x \neq 3$?)
Assume for contradiction that $x = 3$:
$$\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} = 3 \implies 3y - 2 = 3(y - 1) \implies 3y - 2 = 3y - 3 \implies -2 = -3$$ This is a contradiction. Hence, $x \neq 3$, confirming that $x \in A$.
Now, verify the image under $f$:
$$f(x) = f\left(\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}\right) = \frac{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 2}{\frac{3y - 2}{y - 1} - 3} = \frac{\frac{3y - 2 - 2(y - 1)}{y - 1}}{\frac{3y - 2 - 3(y - 1)}{y - 1}} = \frac{3y - 2 - 2y + 2}{3y - 2 - 3y + 3} = \frac{y}{1} = y$$ Thus, for every $y \in B$, there exists a valid pre-image $x \in A$. Hence, $f$ is surjective (onto).

Part III: Invertibility and Inverse Formulation
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective, and therefore invertible. Its inverse $f^{-1}: B \to A$ is defined by:
$$f^{-1}(y) = \frac{3y - 2}{y - 1}, \quad \text{or equivalently: } f^{-1}(x) = \frac{3x - 2}{x - 1}$$
Exemplar Board Problem 1.2: Let $\mathbb{N}$ denote the set of all natural numbers. Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ by:
$$(a, b) R (c, d) \iff a + d = b + c$$ Prove that $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$, and determine the equivalence class $[(2, 5)]$.

Proof of Equivalence:

1. Reflexivity: Let $(a, b) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ be arbitrary. By commutativity of addition in $\mathbb{N}$:
$$a + b = b + a$$ By definition of the relation, this implies:
$$(a, b) R (a, b)$$ Hence, $R$ is reflexive on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.

2. Symmetry: Let $(a, b), (c, d) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that $(a, b) R (c, d)$.
$$\implies a + d = b + c$$ Using symmetry and commutativity of equality and addition:
$$c + b = d + a$$ By definition of $R$, this directly gives:
$$(c, d) R (a, b)$$ Hence, $R$ is symmetric.

3. Transitivity: Let $(a, b), (c, d), (e, f) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ such that:
$$(a, b) R (c, d) \quad \text{and} \quad (c, d) R (e, f)$$ By definition of $R$:
(1) $a + d = b + c$
(2) $c + f = d + e$
Adding equations (1) and (2):
$$(a + d) + (c + f) = (b + c) + (d + e)$$ Rearranging via associativity and commutativity:
$$(a + f) + (c + d) = (b + e) + (c + d)$$ Applying the cancellation law for addition in $\mathbb{N}$:
$$a + f = b + e$$ By definition of $R$, this implies:
$$(a, b) R (e, f)$$ Hence, $R$ is transitive.

Since $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, $R$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$.

Determination of Equivalence Class $[(2, 5)]$:
By definition:
$$[(2, 5)] = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} : (x, y) R (2, 5)\}$$ $$(x, y) R (2, 5) \iff x + 5 = y + 2 \iff y - x = 3 \iff y = x + 3$$ Since the underlying set is $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$:
$$[(2, 5)] = \{(x, x + 3) : x \in \mathbb{N}\} = \{(1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6), (4, 7), (5, 8), \dots\}$$ Geometrically, this equivalence class corresponds to the discrete set of lattice points lying on the line $y - x = 3$ in the first quadrant. $\blacksquare$
Analytical Summary of Examiner Expectations:
In CBSE Class 12 board evaluations, arguments for relation and function properties must be stated with full deductive rigor:
1. Disproving vs. Proving: To prove a universal property (e.g., injectivity, symmetry, transitivity), you must use general variables ($x_1, x_2$ or $a, b, c$). To disprove a property, a single concrete numerical counterexample is sufficient and expected.
2. Equivalence Class Specification: Equivalence classes must be written in explicit set notation, and their elements must satisfy the domain bounds of the underlying set (e.g., verifying natural vs. integer bounds).
3. Composite Domain Verification: For a composite function $g \circ f$ to be valid, explicitly check that the range of the interior function $f$ is a subset of the domain of the exterior function $g$.

Derivation 1: Fundamental Partitioning Theorem of Equivalence Classes

Theorem: Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on a non-empty set $A$. For any $a, b \in A$, the equivalence classes $[a]$ and $[b]$ are either identical or mutually disjoint. That is, either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.

Proof:

Let $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. We must establish that $[a] = [b]$.

Step 1: Establishing intersection element properties.
Since $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$, there exists an element $x \in A$ such that: $$x \in [a] \quad \text{and} \quad x \in [b]$$ By the definition of an equivalence class: $$(x, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (x, b) \in R$$

Step 2: Applying Symmetry and Transitivity.
Since $R$ is symmetric: $$(x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$$ Now, applying transitivity to $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$: $$(a, x) \in R \land (x, b) \in R \implies (a, b) \in R \tag{1}$$

Step 3: Proving $[a] \subseteq [b]$.
Let $y$ be an arbitrary element of $[a]$. Then: $$y \in [a] \implies (y, a) \in R$$ Using (1) and transitivity of $R$: $$(y, a) \in R \land (a, b) \in R \implies (y, b) \in R$$ By definition of $[b]$, $(y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$. Therefore: $$[a] \subseteq [b] \tag{2}$$

Step 4: Proving $[b] \subseteq [a]$.
Applying symmetry to (1): $$(a, b) \in R \implies (b, a) \in R \tag{3}$$ Let $z$ be an arbitrary element of $[b]$. Then: $$z \in [b] \implies (z, b) \in R$$ Using (3) and transitivity of $R$: $$(z, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R \implies (z, a) \in R$$ Hence, $z \in [a]$, which proves: $$[b] \subseteq [a] \tag{4}$$

Conclusion:
From equations (2) and (4), $[a] \subseteq [b]$ and $[b] \subseteq [a] \implies [a] = [b]$.
Consequently, if $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$, then $[a] = [b]$. By contrapositive logic, if $[a] \neq [b]$, then $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Preservation of Injectivity under Function Composition

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two injective (one-one) functions. Then the composite mapping $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also injective.

Proof:

Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that: $$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$

Step 1: Expanding functional application.
By definition of functional composition: $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$

Step 2: Injectivity of the outer function $g$.
Define intermediate elements $u_1 = f(x_1) \in B$ and $u_2 = f(x_2) \in B$. Thus: $$g(u_1) = g(u_2)$$ Since $g: B \to C$ is given to be injective ($g(y_1) = g(y_2) \implies y_1 = y_2$): $$u_1 = u_2 \implies f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$

Step 3: Injectivity of the inner function $f$.
Since $f: A \to B$ is given to be injective ($f(a_1) = f(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2$): $$f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Conclusion:
We have demonstrated that: $$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2 \quad \forall x_1, x_2 \in A$$ Therefore, $g \circ f$ is strictly injective. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: Preservation of Surjectivity under Function Composition

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two surjective (onto) functions. Then the composite mapping $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also surjective.

Proof:

To prove that $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective, we must prove that for an arbitrary element $z \in C$, there exists an element $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$.

Step 1: Utilizing surjectivity of outer function $g$.
Let $z \in C$. Since $g: B \to C$ is surjective: $$\exists y \in B \quad \text{such that} \quad g(y) = z \tag{1}$$

Step 2: Utilizing surjectivity of inner function $f$.
For this element $y \in B$, since $f: A \to B$ is surjective: $$\exists x \in A \quad \text{such that} \quad f(x) = y \tag{2}$$

Step 3: Evaluating the composite map at $x$.
Substitute equation (2) into equation (1): $$g(f(x)) = z$$ By the standard definition of functional composition: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = z$$

Conclusion:
For every $z \in C$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$. Thus, $\text{Range}(g \circ f) = C$, confirming that $g \circ f$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Uniqueness of Inverse Functions

Theorem: If a function $f: X \to Y$ is invertible, its inverse mapping is unique.

Proof:

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exist two distinct inverse functions $g_1: Y \to X$ and $g_2: Y \to X$ for the function $f$.

Step 1: Applying invertibility equations for $g_1$ and $g_2$.
Since $g_1$ is an inverse of $f$: $$f \circ g_1 = I_Y \quad \text{and} \quad g_1 \circ f = I_X \tag{1}$$ Similarly, since $g_2$ is an inverse of $f$: $$f \circ g_2 = I_Y \quad \text{and} \quad g_2 \circ f = I_X \tag{2}$$

Step 2: Operating via identity mappings and associativity.
Consider the composition $g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2)$: $$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = g_1 \circ I_Y = g_1 \tag{3}$$ By the associative property of function composition: $$g_1 \circ (f \circ g_2) = (g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 \tag{4}$$ Substitute $(g_1 \circ f) = I_X$ from equation (1) into equation (4): $$(g_1 \circ f) \circ g_2 = I_X \circ g_2 = g_2 \tag{5}$$

Conclusion:
Equating statements (3) and (5): $$g_1 = g_2$$ This contradicts the assumption that $g_1$ and $g_2$ are distinct. Hence, the inverse of an invertible function is strictly unique. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Reversal Law for the Inverse of Composition

Theorem: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible, and its inverse is given by: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Proof:

Since $f$ and $g$ are invertible, both are bijections, and their individual inverses exist: $$f^{-1}: B \to A \quad \text{such that} \quad f \circ f^{-1} = I_B, \; f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ $$g^{-1}: C \to B \quad \text{such that} \quad g \circ g^{-1} = I_C, \; g^{-1} \circ g = I_B$$ To prove that $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$, we must verify two operational conditions: $$\text{(i) } (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{(ii) } (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$$

Step 1: Verification of left identity composition.
Using the associative property of composition: $$\begin{aligned} (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f)) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ ((g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ f \\ &= I_A \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: Verification of right identity composition.
Similarly, applying associativity: $$\begin{aligned} (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ (f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1})) \\ &= g \circ ((f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1}) \\ &= g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) \\ &= g \circ g^{-1} \\ &= I_C \end{aligned}$$

Conclusion:
Since $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$ and $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$, by the uniqueness of inverse functions: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1} \quad \blacksquare$$

Derivation 6: Combinatorial Derivation of Total Reflexive & Symmetric Relations

Theorem: For a finite set $A$ with $|A| = n$, determine the number of: (a) Reflexive relations, (b) Symmetric relations, and (c) Reflexive and symmetric relations simultaneously.

Proof:

The total Cartesian product $A \times A$ contains $n^2$ ordered pairs $(a_i, a_j)$. These can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets: $$\text{Diagonal Elements: } D = \{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\}, \quad |D| = n$$ $$\text{Off-Diagonal Elements: } O = \{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j\}, \quad |O| = n^2 - n = n(n-1)$$

Part (a): Total Number of Reflexive Relations.
A relation $R$ is reflexive if and only if $D \subseteq R$.

  1. For each diagonal element $(a_i, a_i) \in D$, there is exactly $1$ choice (it must belong to $R$).
  2. For each of the $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal elements, there are $2$ independent choices (it can either belong to $R$ or not belong to $R$).
Using the multiplication principle: $$N_{\text{ref}} = 1^n \times 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

Part (b): Total Number of Symmetric Relations.
A relation $R$ is symmetric if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \iff (a_j, a_i) \in R$.

  1. The $n$ diagonal elements $(a_i, a_i)$ can either be included or excluded independently: $2^n$ choices.
  2. The $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal elements form $\frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \binom{n}{2}$ non-ordered pairs of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$. For each pair, there are only $2$ valid symmetric choices: either both are in $R$, or neither is in $R$.
Total symmetric relations: $$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Part (c): Total Relations that are both Reflexive and Symmetric.

  1. Diagonal elements: Since $R$ is reflexive, all $n$ diagonal pairs must be present $\implies 1$ choice.
  2. Off-diagonal pairs: By symmetry, each of the $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ symmetric pairs has $2$ choices.
Total relations: $$N_{\text{ref} \cap \text{sym}} = 1 \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} \quad \blacksquare$$
