1. Complete Conceptual Framework & Definitions

Fundamental Theoretical Principles

1. Foundational Set-Theoretic Architecture

The rigorous formulation of relations and functions constitutes the bedrock of contemporary mathematical analysis. Within the epistemological framework of the CBSE Senior School Certificate Examination and advanced foundational calculus, these constructs are not merely computational mechanics, but formal morphisms mapping between structured sets.

Let $A$ and $B$ be arbitrary non-empty sets defined within a universal set $\mathcal{U}$. The Cartesian Product, denoted by $A \times B$, is the set of all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ such that the first coordinate is an element of $A$ and the second coordinate is an element of $B$: $$\mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{B} = \{(a, b) : a \in A \land b \in B\}$$ If $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$, where $|\cdot|$ denotes the cardinality of a finite set, then the cardinality of the product space is governed by the fundamental counting principle: $$|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B| = mn$$ The total number of distinct subsets that can be formed from $A \times B$ is given by the power set cardinality $|\mathcal{P}(A \times B)| = 2^{mn}$. Each of these subsets defines a distinct mathematical relation from $A$ to $B$.

2. Formal Theory of Relations on a Set

A relation $R$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ is formally defined as any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times B$. Symbolically, $R \subseteq A \times B$. If $(a, b) \in R$, we denote this state of relational association as $a\,R\,b$ (read: "$a$ is $R$-related to $b$"). Conversely, if $(a, b) \notin R$, the elements do not satisfy the relational predicate, denoted $a \!\not R\, b$.

When $B = A$, $R$ is termed an endorelation or simply a relation on set $A$ ($R \subseteq A \times A$). For a finite set $A$ with $|A| = n$, the total number of distinct relations definable on $A$ is precisely $2^{n^2}$.

2.1 Trivial and Universal Extremities

Within the Boolean lattice of subsets $\mathcal{P}(A \times A)$, two boundary cases manifest:

  • The Empty (or Void) Relation: Denoted by $\emptyset$. Since the null set is a subset of every set, $\emptyset \subseteq A \times A$. In this relation, no element of $A$ is related to any element of $A$: $$\emptyset = \{\} \subset A \times A$$
  • The Universal Relation: Denoted by $U = A \times A$. In this relation, every element of $A$ is related to every element of $A$. Both the empty and universal relations are colloquially designated as trivial relations.

2.2 The Identity Relation versus Reflexivity

Precision in distinguishing the Identity Relation from a Reflexive Relation is a principal criterion for high-level board assessments.

The identity relation on a set $A$, denoted $I_A$, is defined strictly as: $$I_A = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$$ Under $I_A$, an element $a \in A$ is related exclusively to itself and to no other element.

By contrast, a relation $R$ on set $A$ is classified as Reflexive if and only if: $$\forall a \in A, \quad (a, a) \in R$$ Thus, while $I_A$ enforces $(a, b) \in I_A \implies a = b$, a reflexive relation merely mandates that the diagonal elements $\Delta = \{(a, a) : a \in A\}$ form a subset of $R$. That is: $$I_A \subseteq R$$ Consequently, every identity relation is reflexive, but a reflexive relation is not necessarily the identity relation, as it may contain non-diagonal ordered pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$.

3. The Triad of Equivalence Relations

The classification of relational structures depends on three axiomatic properties: reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity.

3.1 Reflexivity

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is reflexive if every element of $A$ maps to itself under $R$: $$\forall x \in A \implies (x, x) \in R$$ Analytical Proof Strategy: To establish reflexivity, choose an arbitrary element $x \in A$ without assuming any special characteristics, and demonstrate analytically through the governing algebraic or geometric rule that $(x, x)$ satisfies the condition.

Counterexample Mechanism: To disprove reflexivity, one must isolate a single element $x_0 \in A$ such that $(x_0, x_0) \notin R$.

3.2 Symmetry

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is symmetric if the validity of the relation in one direction guarantees its validity in the reverse direction: $$\forall x, y \in A, \quad (x, y) \in R \implies (y, x) \in R$$ In terms of the inverse relation $R^{-1} = \{(y, x) : (x, y) \in R\}$, symmetry is defined by the set identity: $$R = R^{-1}$$ Analytical Caution: Symmetry is a conditional statement (implication). If the premise $(x, y) \in R$ is false for distinct $x$ and $y$, the implication holds vacuously. Symmetry is violated if and only if there exists a pair $(x, y) \in R$ such that $(y, x) \notin R$.

3.3 Transitivity

A relation $R$ on set $A$ is transitive if the relational chaining through an intermediate element transfers directly between the terminal elements: $$\forall x, y, z \in A, \quad \Big((x, y) \in R \land (y, z) \in R\Big) \implies (x, z) \in R$$ The Vacuous Truth Nuance: Transitivity fails if and only if there exist elements $a, b, c \in A$ such that $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$, but $(a, c) \notin R$. If there are no pairs of the form $(a, b)$ and $(b, c)$ with $a \neq b$ in $R$, the antecedent is perpetually false, rendering the conditional statement trivially true.

For example, consider the relation $R = \{(1, 2)\}$ on the set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$. The condition $(a, b) \in R \land (b, c) \in R$ requires an ordered pair starting with $2$. No such pair exists in $R$. Thus, the hypothesis is never satisfied, the implication is never breached, and $R$ is strictly transitive.

3.4 Combinatorial Enumeration of Relations on Finite Sets

Let $|A| = n$. The total number of pairs in $A \times A$ is $n^2$. These pairs can be partitioned into:

  • $n$ diagonal pairs of the form $(a_i, a_i)$.
  • $n^2 - n = n(n - 1)$ non-diagonal pairs, which can be grouped into $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ symmetric pairings of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ where $i < j$.

Using these partitions, we determine the number of specific relations on $A$:

  • Total Reflexive Relations: All $n$ diagonal pairs must be present. The remaining $n(n - 1)$ non-diagonal pairs may be chosen independently: $$N_{\text{reflexive}} = 2^{n(n - 1)}$$
  • Total Symmetric Relations: Each of the $n$ diagonal pairs can be independently included or excluded ($2^n$ possibilities). For each of the $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ pairs of symmetric off-diagonal elements $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, there are 2 choices (both are present, or both are absent): $$N_{\text{symmetric}} = 2^n \cdot 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}}$$
  • Total Reflexive and Symmetric Relations: All $n$ diagonal pairs must be present ($1$ choice). The $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ symmetric off-diagonal pairs can be chosen independently ($2$ choices each): $$N_{\text{ref} \cap \text{sym}} = 2^{\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}}$$

4. Equivalence Relations, Partitions, and Equivalence Classes

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed an Equivalence Relation if and only if it is simultaneously reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

4.1 Equivalence Classes

Let $R$ be an equivalence relation on set $A$, and let $a \in A$. The equivalence class of $a$, denoted by $[a]$, $cl(a)$, or $\bar{a}$, is the subset of all elements in $A$ that are related to $a$ under $R$: $$[a] = \{x \in A : (x, a) \in R\}$$ By symmetry, this can also be written as $\{x \in A : (a, x) \in R\}$.

4.2 Fundamental Theorem of Equivalence Relations

Theorem: An equivalence relation $R$ on a set $A$ partitions $A$ into a family of mutually disjoint subsets whose union is $A$. Conversely, any partition of $A$ uniquely induces an equivalence relation on $A$.

Formal Proof:

  1. Non-emptiness: Since $R$ is reflexive, for all $a \in A$, we have $(a, a) \in R$. Therefore, $a \in [a]$. Thus, no equivalence class is empty: $$\forall a \in A, \quad [a] \neq \emptyset$$
  2. Exhaustive Coverage: Since every $a \in A$ belongs to its own equivalence class $[a]$: $$A = \bigcup_{a \in A} \{a\} \subseteq \bigcup_{a \in A} [a] \subseteq A \implies \bigcup_{a \in A} [a] = A$$
  3. Pairwise Disjointness: Let $a, b \in A$. We must prove that either $[a] = [b]$ or $[a] \cap [b] = \emptyset$.
    Assume $[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset$. Then there exists an element $x \in A$ such that $x \in [a]$ and $x \in [b]$.
    By definition of equivalence classes: $$(x, a) \in R \quad \text{and} \quad (x, b) \in R$$ By symmetry, $(x, a) \in R \implies (a, x) \in R$.
    Now, using transitivity with $(a, x) \in R$ and $(x, b) \in R$, we find: $$(a, b) \in R$$ Now let $y$ be any element in $[a]$. Then $(y, a) \in R$.
    Applying transitivity to $(y, a) \in R$ and $(a, b) \in R$: $$(y, b) \in R \implies y \in [b]$$ Therefore, $[a] \subseteq [b]$. By reversing the argument, $[b] \subseteq [a]$. Hence, $[a] = [b]$.
    Thus, two equivalence classes are either strictly identical or completely disjoint: $$[a] \cap [b] \neq \emptyset \iff [a] = [b]$$

The set of all such equivalence classes forms the quotient set, denoted $A/R = \{[a] : a \in A\}$.

5. Advanced Mathematical Treatment of Functions

A function (or mapping) $f$ from set $A$ to set $B$, denoted $f: A \to B$, is a specific type of relation $f \subseteq A \times B$ satisfying two conditions:

  1. Totality: Every element of the domain must be mapped: $$\forall x \in A, \quad \exists y \in B \text{ such that } (x, y) \in f$$
  2. Uniqueness (Well-definedness): No element of the domain can map to more than one element in the codomain: $$(x, y_1) \in f \land (x, y_2) \in f \implies y_1 = y_2$$

Here, $A$ is the domain ($\text{dom}(f)$), $B$ is the codomain ($\text{codom}(f)$), and the set of actual outputs is the range: $$\text{range}(f) = f(A) = \{f(x) \in B : x \in A\} \subseteq B$$

5.1 Injective (One-to-One) Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is injective (or one-to-one) if distinct elements in the domain map to distinct elements in the codomain: $$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$$ Contrapositively, injection is verified algebraically using: $$\forall x_1, x_2 \in A, \quad f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$$

Analytical Calculus Criterion: Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be a differentiable function on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. If its derivative $f'(x)$ is strictly positive ($f'(x) > 0$) or strictly negative ($f'(x) < 0$) everywhere on $I$ (except possibly at isolated points where $f'(x) = 0$ without changing sign), then $f$ is strictly monotonic on $I$, which guarantees that $f$ is injective on $I$.

Geometric Criterion (Horizontal Line Test): A function $y = f(x)$ is injective if and only if every horizontal line $y = k$ ($k \in \mathbb{R}$) intersects the graph of $f$ at most once.

5.2 Surjective (Onto) Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is surjective (or onto) if every element of the codomain is mapped to by at least one element of the domain: $$\forall y \in B, \quad \exists x \in A \text{ such that } f(x) = y$$ Equivalently, a function is surjective if and only if its range equals its codomain: $$\text{range}(f) = B$$

Analytical Strategy for CBSE Examinations:

  1. Set $y = f(x)$, where $y$ represents an arbitrary element of the codomain $B$.
  2. Solve this equation algebraically to express the independent variable $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = g(y)$.
  3. Verify two properties:
    • That $g(y)$ exists and is real for all values of $y \in B$.
    • That the resulting $x = g(y)$ lies within the specified domain $A$ for every $y \in B$.
  4. Conclude that $f(g(y)) = y$, confirming surjectivity.

5.3 Bijective Functions

A function $f: A \to B$ is bijective (or an isomorphism of sets) if it is both injective and surjective. Bijective functions establish an exact, invertible correspondence between sets.

5.4 Cardinality Constraints and Enumerative Analysis

Let $A$ and $B$ be finite sets with $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$.

  • Total Functions: Each of the $m$ elements in $A$ can be mapped independently to any of the $n$ elements in $B$: $$N_{\text{total}} = n^m$$
  • Injections:
    • If $m > n$, no injection can exist by the Pigeonhole Principle ($N_{\text{inj}} = 0$).
    • If $m \le n$, the first element has $n$ choices, the second has $n-1$, and so on: $$N_{\text{inj}} = P(n, m) = \frac{n!}{(n - m)!}$$
  • Surjections:
    • If $m < n$, no surjection can exist ($N_{\text{surj}} = 0$).
    • If $m \ge n$, the number of surjective functions is determined by the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE): $$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^{n - k} \binom{n}{k} k^m = n! \cdot S(m, n)$$ where $S(m, n)$ represents the Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind.
  • Bijections:
    • If $m \neq n$, no bijection can exist ($N_{\text{bij}} = 0$).
    • If $m = n$, the number of bijections is the number of permutations of $n$ elements: $$N_{\text{bij}} = n!$$

6. Composition of Functions and Invertibility

6.1 Composition Mechanics

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two functions such that the codomain of $f$ matches the domain of $g$ (or more generally, $\text{range}(f) \subseteq \text{dom}(g)$). The composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is defined by: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)), \quad \forall x \in A$$

Properties of Function Composition:

  • Non-Commutativity: In general, composition is non-commutative: $g \circ f \neq f \circ g$. Even when both are defined (e.g., $f, g: A \to A$), equality rarely holds.
  • Associativity: If $f: A \to B$, $g: B \to C$, and $h: C \to D$, then: $$h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f$$ Proof: For any $x \in A$: $$\Big(h \circ (g \circ f)\Big)(x) = h\big((g \circ f)(x)\big) = h\big(g(f(x))\big)$$ $$\Big((h \circ g) \circ f\Big)(x) = (h \circ g)(f(x)) = h\big(g(f(x))\big)$$ Both sides yield the same output for all $x \in A$.

6.2 Composition Preservation Theorems

Theorem A: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are injective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is injective.
Proof: Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. $$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ Since $g$ is injective, $g(u) = g(v) \implies u = v$. Setting $u = f(x_1)$ and $v = f(x_2)$: $$f(x_1) = f(x_2)$$ Since $f$ is injective, this implies: $$x_1 = x_2$$ Hence, $g \circ f$ is injective.

Theorem B: If $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are surjective, then $g \circ f: A \to C$ is surjective.
Proof: Let $z$ be an arbitrary element of $C$. Since $g: B \to C$ is surjective: $$\exists y \in B \quad \text{such that} \quad g(y) = z$$ Similarly, since $y \in B$ and $f: A \to B$ is surjective: $$\exists x \in A \quad \text{such that} \quad f(x) = y$$ Substituting $y$ into the first expression: $$g(f(x)) = z \implies (g \circ f)(x) = z$$ Thus, for every $z \in C$, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z$. Hence, $g \circ f$ is surjective.

6.3 Structural Deductions from Composite Functions

Standard board examinations frequently test deductions where the properties of the composite function are known, but the component functions must be analyzed:

  • If $g \circ f$ is injective, then $f$ must be injective.
    Proof: Assume $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides gives $g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$, which means $(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$. Since $g \circ f$ is injective, this implies $x_1 = x_2$. Hence, $f$ is injective. Note that $g$ need not be injective.
  • If $g \circ f$ is surjective, then $g$ must be surjective.
    Proof: For any $z \in C$, since $g \circ f$ is surjective, there exists an $x \in A$ such that $(g \circ f)(x) = z \implies g(f(x)) = z$. Setting $y = f(x) \in B$, we have $g(y) = z$. Thus, every $z \in C$ has a pre-image in $B$ under $g$. Hence, $g$ is surjective. Note that $f$ need not be surjective.

6.4 Invertibility and Bijective Equivalence

A function $f: A \to B$ is defined to be invertible if there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that: $$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$ where $I_A$ and $I_B$ are the identity functions on sets $A$ and $B$, respectively. In this case, the function $g$ is the inverse of $f$ and is denoted $f^{-1}$.

Fundamental Theorem of Invertibility: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is bijective.

Forward Direction ($\implies$): Assume $f$ is invertible. Then there exists $g: B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$.

  • Since $I_A$ is injective and $g \circ f = I_A$, the function $f$ must be injective (by Section 6.3).
  • Since $I_B$ is surjective and $f \circ g = I_B$, the function $f$ must be surjective (by Section 6.3).

Because $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective.

Reverse Direction ($\impliedby$): Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.

  • Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  • Since $f$ is injective, this pre-image $x$ is unique.

We can therefore define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element in $A$ such that $f(x) = y$.

  • For any $x \in A$: let $y = f(x)$. By definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. Thus, $g(f(x)) = x$, meaning $(g \circ f)(x) = I_A(x)$. Hence, $g \circ f = I_A$.
  • For any $y \in B$: let $x = g(y)$. Then $f(x) = y$. Thus, $f(g(y)) = y$, meaning $(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y)$. Hence, $f \circ g = I_B$.

Thus, $g$ is the inverse of $f$, proving that $f$ is invertible.

6.5 The "Shoes and Socks" Theorem

Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be two invertible functions. Then the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is also invertible, and its inverse is: $$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$ Proof: To verify that $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ is the inverse of $g \circ f$, we compute their compositions in both directions: $$(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = f^{-1} \circ (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f = f^{-1} \circ I_B \circ f = f^{-1} \circ f = I_A$$ $$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = g \circ (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} = g \circ I_B \circ g^{-1} = g \circ g^{-1} = I_C$$ By the uniqueness of inverses, $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$.

7. Classical Board Exemplars and Analytical Edge Cases

7.1 The Divisibility Relation on $\mathbb{Z}$

Let $R$ be defined on the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ by $a\,R\,b \iff a \mid b$ ($a$ divides $b$, i.e., $b = ka$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$).

  • Reflexivity: For all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a = 1 \cdot a$. Thus, $a \mid a$, so $(a, a) \in R$. The relation is reflexive.
  • Symmetry: Consider $a = 2, b = 4$. Here $2 \mid 4$ because $4 = 2(2)$, so $(2, 4) \in R$. However, $4 \nmid 2$ since $2/4 = 0.5 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, so $(4, 2) \notin R$. Thus, the relation is not symmetric.
  • Transitivity: Assume $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. Then $b = k_1 a$ and $c = k_2 b$ for some $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Substituting the first equation into the second gives $c = k_2 (k_1 a) = (k_1 k_2) a$. Since the product of two integers is an integer ($k_1 k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$), $a \mid c$, which implies $(a, c) \in R$. Thus, the relation is transitive.

7.2 The Congruence Modulo $n$ Relation

Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ be a fixed positive integer. Define a relation $R$ on $\mathbb{Z}$ by: $$a \equiv b \pmod n \iff n \mid (a - b) \iff a - b = kn \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z})$$

  • Reflexivity: For any $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a - a = 0 = 0 \cdot n$. Since $0 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mid (a - a)$, so $(a, a) \in R$.
  • Symmetry: Let $(a, b) \in R$. Then $a - b = kn$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiplying both sides by $-1$ yields $b - a = (-k)n$. Since $k \in \mathbb{Z} \implies -k \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $n \mid (b - a)$, so $(b, a) \in R$.
  • Transitivity: Let $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$. Then $a - b = k_1 n$ and $b - c = k_2 n$ for some $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding these two equations gives: $$(a - b) + (b - c) = k_1 n + k_2 n \implies a - c = (k_1 + k_2)n$$ Since $k_1 + k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, it follows that $n \mid (a - c)$, so $(a, c) \in R$.

Because $R$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, congruence modulo $n$ is an equivalence relation. It partitions the set $\mathbb{Z}$ into $n$ distinct equivalence classes, called residue classes modulo $n$: $$[0], [1], [2], \dots, [n-1]$$ where $[r] = \{qn + r : q \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.

7.3 Real Analysis: The Critical Role of Domain and Codomain

A common source of errors in board examinations is analyzing an algebraic expression without considering its domain and codomain. A function's properties depend entirely on all three components:

Function Mapping Injective? Surjective? Bijective? Mathematical Justification
$f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R},\; f(x) = x^2$ No No No $f(-2) = f(2) = 4$; negative numbers lack real pre-images ($x^2 = -1 \implies x \notin \mathbb{R}$).
$f: [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R},\; f(x) = x^2$ Yes No No Restricted domain prevents sign cancellation; negative codomain values still have no pre-images.
$f: \mathbb{R} \to [0, \infty),\; f(x) = x^2$ No Yes No Fails injection via $f(-x)=f(x)$; surjection holds because for any $y \ge 0$, $x = \sqrt{y} \in \mathbb{R}$.
$f: [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty),\; f(x) = x^2$ Yes Yes Yes Strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$ with non-negative inputs and outputs; inverse is $f^{-1}(y) = \sqrt{y}$.

8. Step-by-Step Exemplar: Proving Bijectivity and Computing Inverses

Consider the rational function: $$f: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}, \quad f(x) = \frac{x - 3}{x - 2}$$

Step 1: Injectivity Test
Let $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$: $$\frac{x_1 - 3}{x_1 - 2} = \frac{x_2 - 3}{x_2 - 2}$$ Cross-multiplying gives: $$(x_1 - 3)(x_2 - 2) = (x_2 - 3)(x_1 - 2)$$ Expanding both products: $$x_1 x_2 - 2x_1 - 3x_2 + 6 = x_1 x_2 - 2x_2 - 3x_1 + 6$$ Subtracting common terms ($x_1 x_2 + 6$) from both sides: $$-2x_1 - 3x_2 = -2x_2 - 3x_1$$ Rearranging terms: $$-2x_1 + 3x_1 = -2x_2 + 3x_2 \implies x_1 = x_2$$ Since $f(x_1) = f(x_2) \implies x_1 = x_2$, the function $f$ is strictly injective.

Step 2: Surjectivity Test
Let $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$ be an arbitrary element of the codomain. We determine whether there exists an $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ such that $f(x) = y$: $$y = \frac{x - 3}{x - 2}$$ Multiplying by $(x - 2)$: $$y(x - 2) = x - 3 \implies xy - 2y = x - 3$$ Rearranging to isolate terms with $x$: $$xy - x = 2y - 3 \implies x(y - 1) = 2y - 3$$ Since $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, $y - 1 \neq 0$. Dividing by $(y - 1)$: $$x = \frac{2y - 3}{y - 1}$$ Next, verify whether $x$ can equal $2$: $$\frac{2y - 3}{y - 1} = 2 \implies 2y - 3 = 2(y - 1) \implies 2y - 3 = 2y - 2 \implies -3 = -2$$ This is a contradiction. Hence, $x \neq 2$ for any real $y \neq 1$.
Therefore, for every $y \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, there exists a valid $x = \frac{2y - 3}{y - 1} \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ such that $f(x) = y$. Thus, $f$ is surjective.

Step 3: Bijectivity and Inverse Inversion
Since $f$ is both injective and surjective, it is bijective and therefore invertible. Its inverse function $f^{-1}: \mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\} \to \mathbb{R} \setminus \{2\}$ is obtained by swapping variables: $$f^{-1}(x) = \frac{2x - 3}{x - 1}$$

9. Analytical Summary of Common Errors

  • Confusing Non-Symmetry with Antisymmetry: A relation is antisymmetric if $(a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R \implies a = b$. Antisymmetry is not the opposite of symmetry. A relation can be both symmetric and antisymmetric (e.g., the identity relation $I_A$), or neither (e.g., $R = \{(1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 3)\}$ on $\{1, 2, 3\}$).
  • Assuming a Symmetric and Transitive Relation Must Be Reflexive: A classic false argument claims that if $(a, b) \in R$, then by symmetry $(b, a) \in R$, and by transitivity $(a, b) \in R \land (b, a) \in R \implies (a, a) \in R$, concluding that $R$ is always reflexive. The error: This deduction relies on the assumption that for every element $a \in A$, there exists some $b \in A$ such that $(a, b) \in R$. If an element $a_0$ is completely isolated—meaning there is no $x$ such that $(a_0, x) \in R$—then $(a_0, a_0)$ will never be generated. For example, on the set $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$, the relation $R = \{(1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 1), (2, 2)\}$ is symmetric and transitive, but it is not reflexive because $(3, 3) \notin R$.
  • Checking Only the Interior of the Codomain: When evaluating surjectivity, it is critical to confirm that $x = g(y)$ is defined for every point in the codomain, and that its outputs always lie within the specified domain. Overlooking domain exclusions is one of the most frequent sources of mark deductions in board examinations.

Derivation 1: Enumeration of Reflexive and Symmetric Relations on Finite Sets

Statement: Let $A$ be a finite set such that $|A| = n$. Prove that:
(i) The total number of reflexive relations on $A$ is $2^{n(n-1)}$.
(ii) The total number of symmetric relations on $A$ is $2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$.
(iii) The total number of relations that are both reflexive and symmetric on $A$ is $2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$.

Proof:

Step 1: Coordinate/Matrix representation of $A \times A$

A relation $R$ on $A$ is any subset of the Cartesian product $A \times A$. The set $A \times A$ contains $|A \times A| = n^2$ ordered pairs. We can represent $A \times A$ as an $n \times n$ grid of pairs $(a_i, a_j)$ for $1 \le i, j \le n$:

$$A \times A = \{(a_i, a_i) : 1 \le i \le n\} \cup \{(a_i, a_j) : i \neq j, \, 1 \le i, j \le n\}$$

The total number of pairs decomposes into:

  • Diagonal elements: $D = \{(a_i, a_i) \mid 1 \le i \le n\}$, where $|D| = n$.
  • Off-diagonal elements: $O = \{(a_i, a_j) \mid i \ne j\}$, where $|O| = n^2 - n = n(n-1)$.

Step 2: Proof of (i) — Reflexive Relations

By definition, a relation $R$ on $A$ is reflexive if and only if $\forall a_i \in A$, $(a_i, a_i) \in R$.

Thus, all $n$ diagonal pairs in $D$ must be included in $R$. For each diagonal pair, there is only $1$ choice (mandatory inclusion).

The remaining $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal pairs in $O$ can be independently chosen to be in $R$ or not in $R$ ($2$ choices per pair):

$$N_{\text{ref}} = 1^n \times 2^{n(n-1)} = 2^{n(n-1)}$$

Step 3: Proof of (ii) — Symmetric Relations

A relation $R$ is symmetric if and only if $(a_i, a_j) \in R \implies (a_j, a_i) \in R$.

The off-diagonal pairs partition into disjoint unordered pairs of the form $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ with $i < j$. The number of such pairs is:

$$\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

For each pair $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$, symmetry imposes that either both pairs are included in $R$, or neither is included ($2$ choices per symmetric pair).

For each diagonal element $(a_i, a_i)$, symmetry is trivially satisfied: $(a_i, a_i) \in R \implies (a_i, a_i) \in R$. Hence, each of the $n$ diagonal elements can be independently present or absent ($2$ choices each).

The total number of independent binary choices is:

$$N_{\text{sym}} = 2^n \times 2^{\binom{n}{2}} = 2^{n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{2n + n^2 - n}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}$$

Step 4: Proof of (iii) — Reflexive and Symmetric Relations

If $R$ is both reflexive and symmetric:

  1. All $n$ diagonal pairs must be in $R \implies 1^n = 1$ choice.
  2. The $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ off-diagonal symmetric pairs $\{(a_i, a_j), (a_j, a_i)\}$ each have $2$ choices.
$$N_{\text{ref}\cap\text{sym}} = 1 \times 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}} = 2^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 2: Theorem on Invertibility and Bijectivity Equivalence

Statement: A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if $f$ is a bijective function (one-one and onto).

Proof:

Part 1: If $f$ is invertible, then $f$ is bijective.

Let $f: A \to B$ be invertible. By definition, there exists a function $g: B \to A$ such that:

$$g \circ f = I_A \quad \text{and} \quad f \circ g = I_B$$

(a) Injectivity of $f$:

Let $x_1, x_2 \in A$ such that $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$. Applying $g$ to both sides:

$$g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2))$$ $$(g \circ f)(x_1) = (g \circ f)(x_2)$$

Since $g \circ f = I_A$, we have $I_A(x_1) = I_A(x_2)$, which implies:

$$x_1 = x_2$$

Thus, $f$ is injective (one-one).

(b) Surjectivity of $f$:

Let $y$ be an arbitrary element in $B$. Since $f \circ g = I_B$, we evaluate at $y$:

$$(f \circ g)(y) = I_B(y) \implies f(g(y)) = y$$

Let $x = g(y)$. Since $g: B \to A$, we have $x \in A$. Therefore, for every $y \in B$, there exists $x = g(y) \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.

Thus, $f$ is surjective (onto). Being both injective and surjective, $f$ is bijective.

Part 2: If $f$ is bijective, then $f$ is invertible.

Assume $f: A \to B$ is bijective.

  1. Since $f$ is surjective, for every $y \in B$, there exists at least one $x \in A$ such that $f(x) = y$.
  2. Since $f$ is injective, this element $x \in A$ is unique. For if $f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y$, then $x_1 = x_2$.

We define a rule $g: B \to A$ by setting $g(y) = x$, where $x$ is the unique element in $A$ satisfying $f(x) = y$.

Now evaluate the compositions:

  1. For any $x \in A$: let $y = f(x)$. Then by definition of $g$, $g(y) = x$. Hence: $$(g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(y) = x = I_A(x) \implies g \circ f = I_A$$
  2. For any $y \in B$: let $x = g(y)$, so that $f(x) = y$. Hence: $$(f \circ g)(y) = f(g(y)) = f(x) = y = I_B(y) \implies f \circ g = I_B$$

Since both conditions $g \circ f = I_A$ and $f \circ g = I_B$ are satisfied, $g$ is the two-sided inverse of $f$, and $f$ is invertible with $f^{-1} = g$.

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 3: The Reversal Rule for the Inverse of a Composition

Statement: Let $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ be invertible functions. Prove that the composite function $g \circ f: A \to C$ is invertible and that:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

Proof:

Step 1: Check domains and codomains

Since $f: A \to B$ and $g: B \to C$ are bijections, their inverses exist:

$$f^{-1}: B \to A \quad \text{and} \quad g^{-1}: C \to B$$

The composition $f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$ maps elements from $C \to A$. The composition $g \circ f$ maps $A \to C$.

Step 2: Right Identity Verification

To prove $(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$, we must establish that:

$$(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$$

Using the associative property of function composition:

$$\begin{aligned} (g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ \left( f \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \right) \\ &= g \circ \left( (f \circ f^{-1}) \circ g^{-1} \right) \end{aligned}$$

Since $f$ is invertible, $f \circ f^{-1} = I_B$. Substituting this gives:

$$\begin{aligned} g \circ (I_B \circ g^{-1}) &= g \circ g^{-1} \quad (\text{since } I_B \circ h = h) \\ &= I_C \quad (\text{since } g \text{ is invertible}) \end{aligned}$$

Step 3: Left Identity Verification

Similarly, compute $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f)$:

$$\begin{aligned} (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) &= f^{-1} \circ \left( g^{-1} \circ (g \circ f) \right) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ \left( (g^{-1} \circ g) \circ f \right) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ (I_B \circ f) \\ &= f^{-1} \circ f \\ &= I_A \end{aligned}$$

Step 4: Conclusion

Because $(g \circ f) \circ (f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) = I_C$ and $(f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}) \circ (g \circ f) = I_A$, by definition of the inverse function, $g \circ f$ is invertible, and its unique inverse is:

$$(g \circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1} \circ g^{-1}$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 4: Closed Formula for the Number of Surjective (Onto) Functions

Statement: Let $|A| = m$ and $|B| = n$ where $m \ge n \ge 1$. The total number of surjective functions from $A$ to $B$ is:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-r} \binom{n}{r} r^m = n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} \binom{n}{n-1} 1^m$$

Proof:

Step 1: Set identification and Universal Set

Let $A = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m\}$ and $B = \{y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n\}$.

Let $U$ be the set of all possible functions from $A$ to $B$. For each of the $m$ elements in $A$, there are $n$ independent target choices in $B$:

$$|U| = n^m$$

Step 2: Defining the Violation Properties

A function $f: A \to B$ is not surjective if its image omits at least one element of $B$.

For each $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, let $P_i$ denote the property that the element $y_i \in B$ is not in the range of $f$. Define the set:

$$A_i = \{f \in U \mid y_i \notin f(A)\}$$

Thus, $f$ is surjective if and only if $f$ satisfies none of the properties $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$. That is:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \left| \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i^c \right| = |U| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right|$$

Step 3: Applying Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion (PIE)

By the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion:

$$\left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right| = S_1 - S_2 + S_3 - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} S_n$$

where $S_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k \le n} |A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}|$.

The term $|A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}|$ represents the number of functions whose range excludes specific elements $\{y_{i_1}, y_{i_2}, \dots, y_{i_k}\}$. The targets for all $m$ elements must come from the remaining $(n - k)$ elements of $B$:

$$|A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}| = (n - k)^m$$

The number of ways to choose $k$ excluded elements out of $n$ is $\binom{n}{k}$. Therefore:

$$S_k = \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m$$

Step 4: Summation Formulation

$$\begin{aligned} N_{\text{surj}} &= |U| - \left| \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i \right| \\ &= n^m - \left[ \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m - \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m + \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n}(0)^m \right] \\ &= \binom{n}{0}n^m - \binom{n}{1}(n-1)^m + \binom{n}{2}(n-2)^m - \dots + (-1)^{n-1}\binom{n}{n-1}1^m \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} (n - k)^m \end{aligned}$$

Let $r = n - k$. When $k = 0 \implies r = n$, and when $k = n \implies r = 0$. Using $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-r} = \binom{n}{r}$ and $(-1)^k = (-1)^{n-r}$:

$$N_{\text{surj}} = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^{n-r} \binom{n}{r} r^m$$

$\blacksquare$

Derivation 5: Uniqueness of Identity and Inverse for a Binary Operation

Statement: Let $*$ be a binary operation on a non-empty set $S$. Prove that:
(i) If an identity element exists, it is unique.
(ii) If $*$ is associative and an element $a \in S$ possesses an inverse, this inverse is unique.

Proof:

Part (i): Uniqueness of Identity Element

Suppose $e_1, e_2 \in S$ are two identity elements for the binary operation $*$ on $S$.

  1. Since $e_1$ is an identity element, for any $x \in S$: $$e_1 * x = x * e_1 = x$$ Setting $x = e_2$ gives: $$e_1 * e_2 = e_2 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$
  2. Since $e_2$ is an identity element, for any $x \in S$: $$e_2 * x = x * e_2 = x$$ Setting $x = e_1$ gives: $$e_1 * e_2 = e_1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Equating Equation 1 and Equation 2:

$$e_1 = e_1 * e_2 = e_2 \implies e_1 = e_2$$

Hence, the identity element is unique.

Part (ii): Uniqueness of Inverse Element

Assume $*$ is associative: $(x * y) * z = x * (y * z)$ for all $x, y, z \in S$. Let $e$ be the unique identity in $S$.

Let $a \in S$ be an invertible element. Suppose $b_1, b_2 \in S$ are both inverses of $a$.

By definition of an inverse:

$$a * b_1 = b_1 * a = e \quad \text{and} \quad a * b_2 = b_2 * a = e$$

Now evaluate the product expression $b_1 * (a * b_2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} b_1 * (a * b_2) &= b_1 * e \quad (\text{since } a * b_2 = e) \\ &= b_1 \quad (\text{since } e \text{ is identity}) \quad \text{--- (Equation 3)} \end{aligned}$$

By associativity of $*$:

$$\begin{aligned} b_1 * (a * b_2) &= (b_1 * a) * b_2 \\ &= e * b_2 \quad (\text{since } b_1 * a = e) \\ &= b_2 \quad (\text{since } e \text{ is identity}) \quad \text{--- (Equation 4)} \end{aligned}$$

Equating Equation 3 and Equation 4:

$$b_1 = b_2$$

Therefore, the inverse of $a$ is uniquely determined, denoted $a^{-1}$.

$\blacksquare$
