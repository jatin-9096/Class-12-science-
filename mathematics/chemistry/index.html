Concept Core

1. Theoretical Principles & Definitions

§
Solutions: Colligative Properties

Colligative properties are properties of solutions that depend solely on the number of solute particles in the solution, not on their nature. Key colligative properties include:

  • Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure
  • Elevation in Boiling Point
  • Depression in Freezing Point
  • Osmotic Pressure
These properties are fundamental for determining the molecular mass of non-volatile solutes. For ideal solutions, these properties follow simple linear relationships with solute concentration (molality or mole fraction).

§
Electrochemistry: Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy (Galvanic/Voltaic cell) or vice versa (Electrolytic cell).

  • Galvanic Cell: A spontaneous redox reaction generates electric current. Anode (oxidation, negative electrode), Cathode (reduction, positive electrode). A salt bridge maintains electrical neutrality.
  • Electrolytic Cell: External electrical energy drives a non-spontaneous redox reaction. Anode (oxidation, positive electrode), Cathode (reduction, negative electrode).
Standard electrode potential ($E^0$) measures the tendency of an electrode to gain or lose electrons. The Nernst equation relates cell potential to reactant and product concentrations.

§
Chemical Kinetics: Order and Molecularity

Order of Reaction: The sum of the powers of the concentration terms of the reactants in the experimentally determined rate law. It can be a whole number, zero, or fractional.
Molecularity of Reaction: The number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that collide simultaneously in an elementary reaction to bring about a chemical reaction. It is always a whole number and never zero or fractional.
For elementary reactions, order equals molecularity. For complex reactions, molecularity is defined for each elementary step, while order refers to the overall reaction.

§
Organic Chemistry: Nucleophilic Substitution

Nucleophilic substitution reactions ($S_N1$ and $S_N2$) are central to haloalkane chemistry.

  • $S_N1$ (Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution): Occurs in two steps (formation of carbocation, then nucleophilic attack). Favored by tertiary haloalkanes, polar protic solvents, and weak nucleophiles. Racemization is observed.
  • $S_N2$ (Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution): Occurs in a single concerted step (transition state formation). Favored by primary haloalkanes, polar aprotic solvents, and strong nucleophiles. Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion) occurs.
Stereochemistry plays a vital role in determining product distribution.

High Yield Formulas

2. Essential Equations & Derivations

Raoult's Law (for non-volatile solute)

The relative lowering of vapor pressure of a dilute solution containing a non-volatile solute is equal to the mole fraction of the solute.

$$ \frac{P^0 - P_s}{P^0} = X_B $$ Where $P^0$ is vapor pressure of pure solvent, $P_s$ is vapor pressure of solution, and $X_B$ is the mole fraction of the solute. Also, for determining molar mass ($M_B$): $$ \frac{P^0 - P_s}{P^0} = \frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B} \approx \frac{n_B}{n_A} = \frac{w_B/M_B}{w_A/M_A} $$ Here, $n_A, n_B$ are moles of solvent and solute, $w_A, w_B$ are their masses, and $M_A, M_B$ are their molar masses.

Nernst Equation

Relates the standard electrode potential ($E^0$) to the cell potential ($E$) under non-standard conditions, considering concentrations of reactants and products.

For a general reaction: $aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD$

$$ E_{cell} = E^0_{cell} - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln Q $$ or at 298 K: $$ E_{cell} = E^0_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{n} \log Q $$ Where $Q = \frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}$ is the reaction quotient, $R$ is gas constant, $T$ is temperature in Kelvin, $n$ is number of electrons exchanged, $F$ is Faraday constant (96485 C/mol).

First Order Integrated Rate Law

For a reaction $A \rightarrow Products$, where the rate depends only on the concentration of A raised to the power one.

$$ \text{Rate} = k[A] $$ Integrated form: $$ \ln[A]_t = -kt + \ln[A]_0 $$ or $$ k = \frac{2.303}{t} \log \frac{[A]_0}{[A]_t} $$ Where $[A]_0$ is initial concentration, $[A]_t$ is concentration at time $t$, and $k$ is the rate constant. Half-life ($t_{1/2}$): $$ t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k} $$ The half-life for a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration.

Arrhenius Equation

Quantifies the dependence of the rate constant ($k$) on temperature ($T$) and activation energy ($E_a$).

$$ k = A e^{-E_a/RT} $$ Where $A$ is the Arrhenius pre-exponential factor (frequency factor), $E_a$ is the activation energy, and $R$ is the gas constant. In logarithmic form: $$ \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{RT} $$ For two different temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ with rate constants $k_1$ and $k_2$: $$ \ln \left( \frac{k_2}{k_1} \right) = \frac{E_a}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2} \right) $$

Board Examination Questions

3. Solved High-Yield CBSE PYQs

Q1

Calculate Molar Mass from Elevation in Boiling Point

Problem: 1.00 g of a non-electrolyte solute was dissolved in 50 g of benzene. The freezing point of benzene is 5.51°C and the freezing point of the solution is 5.08°C. The cryoscopic constant ($K_f$) for benzene is 5.12 K kg mol-1. Calculate the molar mass of the solute.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Calculate the depression in freezing point ($\Delta T_f$): $$\Delta T_f = \text{Freezing point of pure solvent} - \text{Freezing point of solution}$$ $$\Delta T_f = 5.51^\circ C - 5.08^\circ C = 0.43^\circ C = 0.43 K$$
  2. Use the formula for depression in freezing point: $$\Delta T_f = K_f \cdot m$$ Where $m$ is molality of the solution.
  3. Rearrange to find molality ($m$): $$m = \frac{\Delta T_f}{K_f} = \frac{0.43 K}{5.12 K kg mol^{-1}} = 0.08398 \text{ mol kg}^{-1}$$
  4. Calculate molality using mass of solute and solvent: $$m = \frac{\text{Moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}} = \frac{w_B/M_B}{W_A (\text{in kg})}$$ Given $w_B = 1.00$ g, $W_A = 50$ g $= 0.050$ kg.
  5. Substitute and solve for molar mass ($M_B$): $$0.08398 = \frac{1.00 \text{ g} / M_B}{0.050 \text{ kg}}$$ $$M_B = \frac{1.00 \text{ g}}{0.08398 \text{ mol kg}^{-1} \times 0.050 \text{ kg}}$$ $$M_B = \frac{1.00}{0.004199} \approx 238.15 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$$
  6. Therefore, the molar mass of the solute is approximately 238.15 g mol-1.

Q2

Distinguish Between Organic Compounds

Problem: Give simple chemical tests to distinguish between:

  1. Propanal and Propanone
  2. Phenol and Benzoic acid

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Propanal (aldehyde) and Propanone (ketone):
    • Tollens' Test (Silver Mirror Test):
      • Procedure: Add Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal silver nitrate solution) to both compounds and warm gently.
      • Observation: Propanal, being an aldehyde, will reduce Tollens' reagent to give a bright silver mirror on the inner walls of the test tube. Propanone, being a ketone, will not react.
      • Reaction (Propanal): $CH_3CH_2CHO + 2[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+ + 3OH^- \rightarrow CH_3CH_2COO^- + 2Ag \downarrow + 4NH_3 + 2H_2O$
    • Fehling's Test:
      • Procedure: Add Fehling's A and Fehling's B solutions (mixed just before use) to both compounds and warm.
      • Observation: Propanal will give a red-brown precipitate of cuprous oxide ($Cu_2O$). Propanone will not react.
  2. Phenol and Benzoic acid:
    • Sodium Bicarbonate Test:
      • Procedure: Add a saturated solution of sodium bicarbonate ($NaHCO_3$) to both compounds.
      • Observation: Benzoic acid, being a stronger acid than phenol, will react with sodium bicarbonate to produce brisk effervescence due to the evolution of carbon dioxide gas. Phenol will not react.
      • Reaction (Benzoic acid): $C_6H_5COOH + NaHCO_3 \rightarrow C_6H_5COONa + H_2O + CO_2 \uparrow$
    • Ferric Chloride Test:
      • Procedure: Add a few drops of neutral ferric chloride solution ($FeCl_3$) to both compounds.
      • Observation: Phenol will give a characteristic violet, blue, or green coloration due to the formation of a complex. Benzoic acid will not give this test.
      • Reaction (Phenol): $6C_6H_5OH + FeCl_3 \rightarrow [Fe(OC_6H_5)_6]^{3-} + 3H^+ + 3HCl$ (Complex formation)

Fast Revision

4. Quick Mind-Map & Summary

Core Rule 1 (Solutions): Colligative properties are for non-volatile, non-dissociating/associating solutes. Apply van't Hoff factor 'i' for electrolytes.
Core Rule 2 (Electrochemistry): Anode is where oxidation occurs (electron loss), Cathode is where reduction occurs (electron gain). Remember "AN OX, RED CAT".
Core Rule 3 (Kinetics): Integrated rate laws help predict concentration at time 't' and half-life. Know units of rate constants for different orders.
Core Rule 4 (Organic Rxns): Master named reactions (e.g., Aldol, Cannizzaro, Sandmeyer, Hoffmann bromamide) and their mechanisms/reagents.
Board Tip (d- & f-block): Remember unique properties like variable oxidation states, paramagnetism, color, and catalytic activity for d-block elements.
Board Tip (Biomolecules): Focus on structures and functions of carbohydrates (glucose, fructose), proteins (amino acids, peptide bond), and nucleic acids (DNA, RNA bases).
Common Mistake: Confusing $S_N1$ and $S_N2$ reaction conditions and stereochemical outcomes. Tertiary alkyl halides favour $S_N1$, primary favour $S_N2$.
Important Distinction: Differentiating between adsorption and absorption (surface vs. bulk phenomenon) in Surface Chemistry.
