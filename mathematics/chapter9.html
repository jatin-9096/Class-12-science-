1. Conceptual Framework

Definition & Scope
What is a Differential Equation?

A differential equation (DE) is an equation that relates an unknown function with its derivatives. In mathematical notation a DE can be expressed as $$F\bigl(x, y, y', y'',\dots , y^{(n)}\bigr)=0,$$ where $y=y(x)$ is the unknown function of the independent variable $x$, and $y',y'',\dots ,y^{(n)}$ denote its first, second … $n^{\text{th}}$ derivatives respectively. The highest order derivative appearing in the equation determines the order of the DE, while the highest power (after clearing fractions and radicals) of the highest order derivative determines its degree. Differential equations arise naturally when a physical law involves rates of change – for example, Newton’s second law $F=ma$ can be written as $m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=F(t)$, a second‑order DE. The central aim of the theory is to obtain the general solution (containing arbitrary constants) or a particular solution that satisfies given initial or boundary conditions. Understanding the classification of DEs (order, degree, linearity, homogeneity) is essential because each class admits specific solution techniques.

Classification
Order, Degree, Linearity & Homogeneity

The order of a DE is the highest derivative present. For example, $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}+3\frac{dy}{dx}+2y=0$$ is a second‑order DE. The degree is the exponent of the highest order derivative after the equation is made free from radicals and fractions; the same equation has degree 1 because the highest derivative appears linearly. A DE is called linear if it can be written in the form $$a_n(x)y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}(x)y^{(n-1)}+\dots +a_1(x)y'+a_0(x)y = g(x),$$ where $a_i(x)$ and $g(x)$ are functions of $x$ alone. If $g(x)=0$, the equation is homogeneous; otherwise it is non‑homogeneous. Non‑linear DEs involve products or nonlinear functions of $y$ and its derivatives, e.g., $$\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 + y = \sin x.$$ Understanding these categories guides the choice of solution method: separable, exact, integrating factor, characteristic equation, etc. Moreover, DEs can be classified as ordinary (only one independent variable) or partial (more than one independent variable). The present chapter deals exclusively with ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of order up to three, which are most relevant for the CBSE curriculum.

First‑Order Techniques
Solving First‑Order ODEs: Separable, Linear & Exact Forms

First‑order ODEs ($y'=f(x,y)$) constitute the foundation of the subject. A separable equation can be expressed as $$\frac{dy}{dx}=g(x)h(y),$$ allowing variables to be isolated: $$\frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx.$$ Integrating both sides yields $$\int\frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = \int g(x)\,dx + C,$$ where $C$ is the constant of integration. Example: $$\frac{dy}{dx}=xy^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{y^2}=x\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; -\frac{1}{y}= \frac{x^2}{2}+C.$$

A linear first‑order DE has the standard form $$\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)y = Q(x).$$ Multiplying by the integrating factor $\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$ converts it into an exact differential: $$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\mu(x)y\bigr]=\mu(x)Q(x).$$ Integration then gives $$y = \frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left(\int \mu(x)Q(x)\,dx + C\right).$$

An exact equation is written as $$M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0,$$ where $M_y = N_x$ (partial derivatives) ensures exactness. The solution is obtained by finding a potential function $\psi(x,y)$ such that $\psi_x = M$ and $\psi_y = N$, leading to $\psi(x,y)=C$. If the condition $M_y=N_x$ fails, an integrating factor (often a function of $x$ or $y$ alone) can be sought to restore exactness. Mastery of these three techniques equips students to tackle a wide variety of first‑order problems encountered in physics, biology, and economics.

Higher‑Order Linear ODEs
Constant‑Coefficient Equations & Method of Undetermined Coefficients

A linear ODE of order $n$ with constant coefficients has the generic form $$a_n\frac{d^ny}{dx^n}+a_{n-1}\frac{d^{n-1}y}{dx^{n-1}}+\dots +a_1\frac{dy}{dx}+a_0y = f(x).$$ The associated homogeneous equation ($f(x)=0$) is solved by assuming a trial solution $y=e^{rx}$, which leads to the characteristic polynomial $$a_nr^n+a_{n-1}r^{n-1}+\dots +a_1r+a_0=0.$$ Roots $r_1,r_2,\dots$ determine the complementary function (CF):
• Distinct real roots → $y_{CF}=C_1e^{r_1x}+C_2e^{r_2x}+\dots$
• Repeated root $r$ of multiplicity $m$ → $y_{CF}=(C_1+C_2x+\dots +C_mx^{m-1})e^{rx}$
• Complex conjugate pair $α±βi$ → $y_{CF}=e^{αx}(C_1\cosβx+C_2\sinβx).$

For the non‑homogeneous part, the method of undetermined coefficients proposes a particular integral (PI) whose form mirrors $f(x)$. If $f(x)$ is a polynomial, exponential, sine or cosine, or a product thereof, a trial PI is chosen with unknown constants, substituted back, and solved for those constants. When $f(x)$ shares a term with the CF, the trial is multiplied by $x$ enough times to ensure linear independence. An alternative, more universal technique is the variation of parameters, which constructs the PI using the CF and integrals involving $f(x)$. These systematic procedures enable students to solve mechanical vibration problems, electrical circuit equations, and many other engineering models.

Real‑World Modelling
Applications of Differential Equations in Science & Technology

Differential equations serve as the mathematical backbone of dynamic modelling.

Population dynamics: The logistic growth model assumes that the rate of change of a population $P(t)$ is proportional both to the existing population and to the shortage of resources. This yields $$\frac{dP}{dt}=rP\left(1-\frac{P}{K}\right),$$ a separable first‑order DE whose solution $P(t)=\frac{K}{1+Ae^{-rt}}$ captures the characteristic S‑shaped curve observed in ecology.

Newton’s law of cooling: If an object at temperature $T(t)$ exchanges heat with an ambient medium of constant temperature $T_a$, the rate of cooling is proportional to the temperature difference: $$\frac{dT}{dt}= -k\bigl(T-T_a\bigr).$$ Solving the linear first‑order DE gives $T(t)=T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{-kt}$, a formula widely used in forensic science and engineering heat‑transfer problems.

Simple harmonic motion (SHM): A mass‑spring system obeys $m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+kx=0$, a second‑order homogeneous linear DE with constant coefficients. The characteristic equation $mr^2+k=0$ yields imaginary roots $r=±i\sqrt{k/m}$, leading to the solution $x(t)=A\cos(\omega t)+B\sin(\omega t)$ where $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. This model underpins the analysis of oscillatory circuits, pendulums, and even quantum mechanical wave functions.

Electrical RLC circuits: The current $i(t)$ in a series resistor‑inductor‑capacitor circuit satisfies $$L\frac{d^2i}{dt^2}+R\frac{di}{dt}+\frac{i}{C}=V(t),$$ a second‑order linear DE. Depending on the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial, the circuit exhibits under‑damped, critically damped, or over‑damped responses—concepts crucial for signal processing.

These examples illustrate how the abstract machinery of differential equations translates directly into predictive tools across biology, physics, and engineering. Mastery of the theory thus equips students not only for examinations but also for real‑world problem solving.

Derivation of the Feasible Region for a Linear Programming Problem

Consider a linear programming problem (LPP) in two variables $x$ and $y$ with the objective function

$$\text{Maximise (or Minimise)}\; Z = c_1x + c_2y,$$

subject to a set of linear inequalities

$$\begin{aligned} a_{11}x + a_{12}y &\le b_1,\\ a_{21}x + a_{22}y &\le b_2,\\ &\vdots\\ a_{m1}x + a_{m2}y &\le b_m,\\ x &\ge 0,\; y \ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

Each inequality represents a half‑plane in the $xy$‑plane. The intersection of all these half‑planes, together with the non‑negativity constraints, yields the feasible region $R$. To derive $R$ algebraically, we proceed as follows:

  1. Convert each inequality to an equality. For the $i^{\text{th}}$ constraint, write $a_{i1}x + a_{i2}y = b_i$ and denote the line as $L_i$.
  2. Identify the side of $L_i$ that satisfies the original inequality. Substitute a test point, usually the origin $(0,0)$, into the left‑hand side (LHS). If $a_{i1}(0)+a_{i2}(0) \le b_i$ holds, the region containing the origin is retained; otherwise, the opposite side is taken.
  3. Determine the intersection points of the boundary lines. Solve the simultaneous equations $L_i$ and $L_j$ for all $1\le i
  4. Discard points that violate any inequality. Substitute each candidate into all original inequalities; retain only those that satisfy every constraint.
  5. Include the intercepts on the axes. For each constraint, set $x=0$ to find the $y$‑intercept $y_i = b_i/a_{i2}$ (provided $a_{i2}\neq0$) and set $y=0$ to find the $x$‑intercept $x_i = b_i/a_{i1}$ (provided $a_{i1}\neq0$). These intercepts become part of the boundary when they satisfy the remaining constraints.
  6. Construct the polygon. Connect the retained points in a cyclic order (usually clockwise) to form a convex polygon. This polygon is the feasible region $R$.

The convexity of $R$ follows from the fact that each inequality defines a convex half‑plane, and the intersection of convex sets is convex. This property guarantees that the optimal value of $Z$ occurs at one of the extreme points (corner points) of $R$, a result known as the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming.

Derivation of the Corner‑Point (Extreme‑Point) Theorem

Let $R$ be a non‑empty, bounded, convex feasible region defined by a finite set of linear inequalities. The objective function $Z=c_1x+c_2y$ is a linear functional on $R$. To prove that the maximum (or minimum) of $Z$ over $R$ occurs at a corner point, we use the following argument.

Assume, for contradiction, that the optimum occurs at an interior point $P=(x_0,y_0)$ of $R$. Because $R$ is convex, there exists a line segment $PQ$ lying entirely inside $R$, where $Q$ is any other point of $R$ distinct from $P$. Since $Z$ is linear, its value along $PQ$ varies linearly:

$$Z(t)=Z(P)+t\bigl[Z(Q)-Z(P)\bigr],\qquad 0\le t\le1.$$

If $Z(Q)>Z(P)$, then $Z(t)$ exceeds $Z(P)$ for some $t\in(0,1)$, contradicting the optimality of $P$. Similarly, if $Z(Q)

When $P$ lies on a face, the face itself is a line segment joining two extreme points $V_1$ and $V_2$. Because $Z$ is constant on the face, $Z(V_1)=Z(V_2)=Z(P)$. Therefore an extreme point (corner point) attains the same optimal value as $P$. Consequently, at least one corner point yields the optimum.

This theorem justifies the graphical method: evaluating $Z$ at each corner point and selecting the largest (or smallest) value gives the solution.

Derivation of the Simplex Tableau for Two‑Variable Problems (Graphical Method)

Although the graphical method does not require a tableau, constructing a tableau helps in systematic bookkeeping, especially when extending to higher dimensions. For a two‑variable LPP, the standard form is

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Maximise}\; Z &= c_1x_1 + c_2x_2,\\ \text{subject to}\;& a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 + s_i = b_i,\quad i=1,2,\dots,m,\\ &x_1,x_2,s_1,\dots,s_m \ge 0, \end{aligned}$$

where $s_i$ are slack variables introduced to convert $\le$ constraints into equalities. The initial simplex tableau is formed as follows:

To perform the simplex iteration:

  1. Identify the most negative coefficient in the $Z$‑row (entering variable). Suppose $-c_j$ is the most negative.
  2. Compute the minimum ratio $\displaystyle \frac{b_i}{a_{ij}}$ for all $i$ with $a_{ij}>0$. The row giving the smallest ratio determines the leaving variable (basic variable to be replaced).
  3. Pivot on the element $a_{kj}$ (intersection of leaving row $k$ and entering column $j$) to make it unity and all other entries in column $j$ zero, using elementary row operations.
  4. Repeat steps 1–3 until no negative coefficient remains in the $Z$‑row. The RHS entries of the basic variables then give the optimal solution.

For a two‑variable problem, the tableau reduces to a handful of rows, making the graphical interpretation straightforward: each pivot corresponds to moving from one corner point to an adjacent one along an edge of the feasible polygon.
