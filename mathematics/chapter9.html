Definition, order and degree of a differential equation

Definition: A differential equation is an equation that relates an unknown function $y(x)$ (or $y$) and its derivatives $\\frac{dy}{dx}, \\frac{d^2y}{dx^2},\\dots$.

  • Order: The highest order derivative present in the equation.
  • Degree: The highest power (positive integer) to which the highest order derivative is raised, after the equation is made free from radicals and fractions involving derivatives.

For a differential equation written in the form $F\\big(x, y, y', y'', \\dots, y^{(n)}\\big)=0$, the order is $n$ and the degree is the exponent of $y^{(n)}$ after clearing fractions and radicals.

Order $n$Highest derivative
$1$$y'$
$2$$y''$
$\\dots$$y^{(n)}$

SI Units: If $x$ is measured in metres (m) and $y$ in seconds (s), then $y'$ has unit $\\frac{s}{m}$, $y''$ has unit $\\frac{s}{m^2}$, etc.

Example: Solve $y' = 3x^2$, with $y(0)=5$.

  1. Integrate: $\\int y'\\,dx = \\int 3x^2\\,dx \Rightarrow y = x^3 + C$.
  2. Apply the condition $y(0)=5$: $5 = 0 + C \Rightarrow C = 5$.
  3. Solution: $y = x^3 + 5$.

Formation of differential equations from a given family of curves

Definition: A differential equation is an equation involving an unknown function and its derivatives. The order is the highest derivative; the degree (when defined) is the power of the highest derivative after clearing fractions and radicals.

Formation from a family of curves:

  • Identify the given family with one or more arbitrary constants.
  • Differentiate successively enough times to obtain as many equations as there are constants.
  • Eliminate the constants between the original equation and the differentiated ones.
  • The resulting relation between $x$, $y$, and derivatives is the required differential equation.
StepAction
1Write the family, e.g. $y = c x^2 + 5$.
2Differentiate: $\frac{dy}{dx}=2c x$.
3Express the constant: $c = \frac{1}{2x}\frac{dy}{dx}$.
4Substitute $c$ back: $y = \frac{x}{2}\frac{dy}{dx}+5$.
5Arrange: $2y - x\frac{dy}{dx}=10$ – the required DE (first order, degree 1).

Key Formula: For a family containing $n$ arbitrary constants, the resulting differential equation will be of order $n$.

Example: Find the differential equation of the family $y = a\sin x + b\cos x$.

  • Differentiate once: $\frac{dy}{dx}=a\cos x - b\sin x$.
  • Differentiate again: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}= -a\sin x - b\cos x = -y$.
  • Eliminate $a$ and $b$ to obtain $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}+y=0$ (second order, degree 1).

SI Unit: Since the equation relates dimensionless ratios of derivatives, the standard unit is $\text{unitless}$ (or consistent with the variables involved).

Solution of first‑order differential equations – separable variables ($\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y)$)

Definition: A first‑order differential equation is called separable if it can be written as $\\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y)$, i.e., the right‑hand side is a product of a function of $x$ only and a function of $y$ only.

Key idea: Bring all $y$‑terms (including $dy$) to one side and all $x$‑terms (including $dx$) to the other side, then integrate.

StepOperation
1Write $\\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y)$ as $\\frac{1}{g(y)}\\,dy = f(x)\\,dx$.
2Integrate both sides: $\\int \\frac{1}{g(y)}\\,dy = \\int f(x)\\,dx + C$.
3Solve the resulting implicit relation for $y$ if possible.

General solution: $\\int \\frac{1}{g(y)}\\,dy = \\int f(x)\\,dx + C$.

Units: If $y$ and $x$ have units $U_y$ and $U_x$, then $\\frac{dy}{dx}$ has unit $U_y/U_x$. The functions $f$ and $g$ must be dimensionally consistent so that both sides share the same unit.

Example: Solve $\\frac{dy}{dx}=x y$ with $y(0)=2$.

  • Rewrite: $\\frac{1}{y}\\,dy = x\\,dx$.
  • Integrate: $\\int \\frac{1}{y}\\,dy = \\int x\\,dx \Rightarrow \\ln|y| = \\frac{x^{2}}{2}+C$.
  • Exponentiate: $y = C_1 e^{x^{2}/2}$, where $C_1=e^{C}$.
  • Apply $y(0)=2$: $2 = C_1 e^{0} \Rightarrow C_1 = 2$.
  • Final solution: $y = 2 e^{x^{2}/2}$.

Solution of first‑order linear differential equations – integrating factor ($y'+P(x)y=Q(x)$)

Definition: A first‑order linear differential equation is of the form $y'+P(x)y=Q(x)$ where $P$ and $Q$ are continuous functions on an interval $I$.

Integrating factor (IF): $\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$.

Derivation:

  • Multiply the equation by $\mu(x)$: $\mu y'+\mu P y=\mu Q$.
  • Since $\mu'=\mu P$, the left side becomes $(\mu y)'$.
  • Integrate: $\int (\mu y)'dx = \int \mu Q dx \;\Rightarrow\; \mu y = \int \mu Q dx + C$.
  • Hence the solution $y = \frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left(\int \mu(x) Q(x)dx + C\right)$.

General formula: $$y(x)=e^{-\int P(x)dx}\left(\int e^{\int P(x)dx}Q(x)dx + C\right)$$

Units: If $x$ has unit $[X]$ and $y$ has unit $[Y]$, then $P(x)$ has unit $[X]^{-1}$ and $Q(x)$ has unit $[Y][X]^{-1}$ so that each term \in the equation has unit $[Y][X]^{-1}$.

Example:

Solve $y' - \frac{2}{x}y = x^2$, $x>0$.

StepWork
1. Identify $P(x)$, $Q(x)$$P(x)=-\frac{2}{x}$, $Q(x)=x^2$
2. IF $\mu(x)=e^{\int -\frac{2}{x}dx}=e^{-2\ln x}=x^{-2}$
3. Multiply: $(x^{-2}y)' = x^{-2}\cdot x^2 =1$
4. Integrate: $x^{-2}y = \int 1dx = x + C$
5. Solve for $y$$y = x^{2}(x + C)=x^{3}+Cx^{2}$

Thus the required solution is $y = x^{3}+Cx^{2}$.

Exact differential equations and method of making them exact ($M(x,y)dx+N(x,y)dy=0$)

Exact Differential Equation – An equation of the form $M(x,y)dx+N(x,y)dy=0$ is called exact if there exists a function $F(x,y)$ such that $dF= Mdx+Ndy$. Hence $M=\\frac{\\partial F}{\\partial x}$ and $N=\\frac{\\partial F}{\\partial y}$.

Condition of Exactness – The necessary and sufficient condition is $\\frac{\\partial M}{\\partial y}=\\frac{\\partial N}{\\partial x}$ (provided $M,N$ have continuous first partial derivatives).

Solution Procedure

  • Verify exactness using the condition.
  • Integrate $M$ w.r.t. $x$: $F(x,y)=\\int M(x,y)dx+\\phi(y)$.
  • Differentiate $F$ w.r.t. $y$ and equate to $N$ to find $\\phi'(y)$.
  • Obtain $\\phi(y)$ and write the implicit solution $F(x,y)=C$.

Making a Non‑Exact Equation Exact

  • If $\\frac{\\partial M}{\\partial y}\\neq\\frac{\\partial N}{\\partial x}$, look for an integrating factor $\\mu(x)$ or $\\mu(y)$.
  • For $\\mu(x)$: $\\frac{1}{\\mu}\\frac{d\\mu}{dx}=\\frac{\\partial N/\\partial x-\\partial M/\\partial y}{M}$ must be a function of $x$ alone.
  • Similarly for $\\mu(y)$: $\\frac{1}{\\mu}\\frac{d\\mu}{dy}=\\frac{\\partial M/\\partial y-\\partial N/\\partial x}{N}$ must depend only on $y$.

Example

Solve $(2xy+3)dx+(x^2+4y)dy=0$.

Here $M=2xy+3$, $N=x^2+4y$. $\\partial M/\\partial y=2x$, $\\partial N/\\partial x=2x$ → exact.

Integrate $M$ w.r.t. $x$: $F=\\int (2xy+3)dx = x^2y+3x+\\phi(y)$.

Differentiate w.r.t. $y$: $\\partial F/\\partial y = x^2+\\phi'(y)$. Equate to $N$: $x^2+\\phi'(y)=x^2+4y \Rightarrow \\phi'(y)=4y$.

Integrate: $\\phi(y)=2y^2$.

Solution: $F(x,y)=x^2y+3x+2y^2=C$.

Bernoulli and homogeneous equations ($y'+P(x)y=Q(x)y^{n}$)

Definition: A Bernoulli differential equation is of the form $y' + P(x) y = Q(x) y^{n}$ where $n \neq 0,1$.

When $n=0$ or $1$ it reduces to linear equations. For $n\neq 0,1$ we use substitution $v = y^{1-n}$.

  • Derivation: Differentiate $v = y^{1-n}$ → $v' = (1-n) y^{-n} y'$.
  • Replace $y'$ from the original equation and simplify to obtain a linear equation in $v$: $v' + (1-n) P(x) v = (1-n) Q(x)$.

Standard Form:

OriginalSubstitutionLinear in $v$
$y' + P(x) y = Q(x) y^{n}$$v = y^{1-n}$$v' + (1-n) P(x) v = (1-n) Q(x)$

Solution Procedure

  1. Identify $P(x), Q(x), n$.
  2. Set $v = y^{1-n}$.
  3. Form the linear equation $v' + (1-n)P(x)v = (1-n)Q(x)$.
  4. Find integrating factor $I(x)=\exp\left(\int (1-n)P(x)dx\right)$.
  5. Integrate: $v I = \int (1-n)Q(x) I dx + C$.
  6. Back‑substitute $y = v^{\frac{1}{1-n}}$.

Example

Solve $y' + \frac{2}{x} y = x^{3} y^{2}$.

Here $P(x)=\frac{2}{x},\; Q(x)=x^{3},\; n=2$.

Put $v = y^{1-2}=y^{-1}$ → $v' = -y^{-2} y'$.

From the given equation $y' = -\frac{2}{x} y + x^{3} y^{2}$.

Multiply by $-y^{-2}$: $v' = \frac{2}{x} v - x^{3}$.

Linear form: $v' - \frac{2}{x} v = -x^{3}$.

Integrating factor $I = \exp\left(\int -\frac{2}{x}dx\right)=x^{-2}$.

$(v x^{-2})' = -x$ → $v x^{-2}= -\frac{x^{2}}{2}+C$.

Thus $v = -\frac{x^{4}}{2}+C x^{2}$ and $y = \frac{1}{v}=\frac{1}{C x^{2}-\frac{x^{4}}{2}}$.

Units: If $x$ is in seconds (s) and $y$ in meters (m), each term of the equation has the unit $\frac{m}{s}$, keeping the relation dimensionally consistent.

Initial value problems and existence‑uniqueness ($y(x_{0})=y_{0}$)

Definition: An initial value problem (IVP) for a differential equation is to find a function $y(x)$ that satisfies the equation together with the condition $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$, where $x_{0}$ and $y_{0}$ are given numbers (often with SI units of the independent and dependent variables).

Existence‑Uniqueness Theorem (Picard‑Lindelöf): Let $f(x,y)$ be continuous in the rectangle $R:\;|x-x_{0}|\le a,\;|y-y_{0}|\le b$ and let $\partial f/\partial y$ be continuous in $R$. Then there exists a unique solution $y(x)$ of $y'=f(x,y)$ satisfying $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$ on an interval $|x-x_{0}|

Key formulae derived from the theorem:

ConditionResult
$f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ continuous \in $R$Local existence of a solution
Same condition plus Lipschitz \in $y$Uniqueness of the solution

Typical steps \to solve an IVP:

  • Identify the type of ODE (separable, linear, exact, etc.).
  • Solve the ODE ignoring the constant of integration.
  • Apply the initial condition $y(x_{0})=y_{0}$ \to determine the constant.
  • State the interval of validity using the theorem or singular points.

Example (separable ODE): Solve $y' = y\cos x$, $y(0)=2$.

Solution: Separate variables $\frac{dy}{y}=\cos x\,dx$. Integrate: $\ln|y| = \sin x + C$. Exponentiate: $y = Ce^{\sin x}$. Use $y(0)=2$ gives $2 = C e^{\sin 0}=C$, so $C=2$. Hence $y(x)=2e^{\sin x}$. The functions $f(x,y)=y\cos x$ and $\partial f/\partial y=\cos x$ are continuous for all $x$, $y$, so by the theorem the solution exists and is unique for $-\infty

Higher‑order linear differential equations with constant coefficients – complementary function and particular integral ($y^{(n)}+a_{1}y^{(n-1)}+\dots +a_{n}y=F(x)$)

Definition: A linear differential equation of order $n$ with constant coefficients is written as $y^{(n)}+a_{1}y^{(n-1)}+\dots +a_{n}y=F(x)$, where $a_{i}$ are constants, $F(x)$ is a known function and $y$ is the dependent variable (unit of $y$ depends on the physical context; $x$ is usually in seconds (s) or meters (m)).

General solution = Complementary Function ($y_c$) + Particular Integral ($y_p$).

1. Complementary Function (CF)

  • Form the auxiliary (characteristic) equation: $$r^{n}+a_{1}r^{n-1}+\dots +a_{n}=0$$
  • Let the distinct real roots be $r_{1},r_{2},\dots ,r_{k}$ with multiplicities $m_{1},m_{2},\dots ,m_{k}$.
  • Then $$y_c=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{s=0}^{m_{j}-1}C_{j,s}x^{s}e^{r_{j}x}$$ where $C_{j,s}$ are arbitrary constants.

2. Particular Integral (PI)

  • Choose a trial function $y_p$ based on the form of $F(x)$ (method of undetermined coefficients).
  • If any term of the trial coincides with a term \in $y_c$, multiply that term by $x^{p}$ where $p$ is the smallest positive integer that removes the duplication.
  • Substitute $y_p$ into the original equation and determine the unknown coefficients.

Key formulas

Case of $F(x)$Trial $y_p$
$F(x)=P_m(x)$ (polynomial of degree $m$)$y_p=Ax^{m}+Bx^{m-1}+\dots +K$
$F(x)=e^{\alpha x}$$y_p=Ce^{\alpha x}$
$F(x)=\sin \beta x$ or $\cos \beta x$$y_p=A\sin \beta x+B\cos \beta x$

Example

Solve $$y''-5y'+6y=3e^{2x}$$.

  1. Auxiliary equation: $r^{2}-5r+6=0\Rightarrow (r-2)(r-3)=0$, so $r_{1}=2$, $r_{2}=3$.
  2. CF: $y_c=C_{1}e^{2x}+C_{2}e^{3x}$.
  3. Since the RHS is $3e^{2x}$ and $e^{2x}$ already appears \in $y_c$, multiply the trial by $x$: $y_p=Ax e^{2x}$.
  4. Compute derivatives: $y_p'=A e^{2x}+2Ax e^{2x}$, $y_p''=2A e^{2x}+2A e^{2x}+4Ax e^{2x}=4A e^{2x}+4Ax e^{2x}$.
  5. Substitute into the DE: $(4A e^{2x}+4Ax e^{2x})-5(A e^{2x}+2Ax e^{2x})+6(Ax e^{2x})=3e^{2x}$.
  6. Simplify: $(4A-5A) e^{2x}+ (4A-10A+6A) x e^{2x}=3e^{2x}\Rightarrow -A e^{2x}=3e^{2x}$, giving $A=-3$.
  7. Thus $y_p=-3x e^{2x}$ and the complete solution is $y=C_{1}e^{2x}+C_{2}e^{3x}-3x e^{2x}$.
