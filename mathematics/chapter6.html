Increasing and decreasing functions

Definition: A function $f$ is said to be increasing on an interval $I$ if for any $x_1,x_2\in I$ with $x_1f(x_2)$.

Derivative Test: If $f$ is differentiable on $I$ and $f'(x)>0$ for all $x\in I$, then $f$ is strictly increasing on $I$. If $f'(x)<0$ for all $x\in I$, then $f$ is strictly decreasing on $I$. At points where $f'(x)=0$ or $f'$ does not exist, we examine sign changes to determine monotonicity.

Critical Points and Monotonicity Intervals: Solve $f'(x)=0$ or $f'$ undefined to get critical points. Partition the domain into subintervals using these points. Test a point from each subinterval in $f'$ to decide the sign. A change from $+$ to $-$ indicates a local maximum; $-$ to $+$ indicates a local minimum.

Edge Cases: If $f'(x)=0$ over an entire interval, $f$ is constant there. If $f'$ changes sign but remains zero at isolated points, the function may still be monotonic (e.g., $f(x)=x^3$ at $x=0$).

Example:

Let $f(x)=x^3-3x^2+2$. Then $f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)$.

  • Critical points: $x=0$ and $x=2$.
  • Test intervals: $(-\infty,0)$, $(0,2)$, $(2,\infty)$.
  • Choose $x=-1$: $f'(-1)=3(-1)(-3)=9>0$ → increasing.
  • Choose $x=1$: $f'(1)=3(1)(-1)=-3<0$ → decreasing.
  • Choose $x=3$: $f'(3)=3(3)(1)=9>0$ → increasing.

Thus $f$ is increasing on $(-\infty,0)$ and $(2,\infty)$, decreasing on $(0,2)$. The function attains a local maximum at $x=0$ and a local minimum at $x=2$.

Local maxima and minima

Definition: A point $c$ in the domain of a differentiable function $f$ is called a local maximum if there exists an interval $(c-\delta,c+\delta)$ such that $f(c)\ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in that interval. Similarly, $c$ is a local minimum if $f(c)\le f(x)$ in a neighbourhood.

First‑Derivative Test:

  • Find critical points by solving $f'(x)=0$ or where $f'$ does not exist.
  • Check the sign of $f'$ on the left and right of each critical point.
  • If $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$, $c$ is a local maximum; from $-$ to $+$, $c$ is a local minimum.

Second‑Derivative Test (Sufficient Condition):

ConditionConclusion
$f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)>0$Local minimum at $c$
$f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)<0$Local maximum at $c$
$f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)=0$Test inconclusive

Edge Cases:

  • Endpoints of a closed interval are examined separately; they can be absolute extrema even if derivative does not change sign.
  • If $f''(c)=0$, higher‑order derivatives may be used: the first non‑zero derivative of order $n$ determines the nature (odd $n$ → point of inflection, even $n$ → min if positive, max if negative).
  • Functions not differentiable at $c$ (e.g., $|x|$ at $0$) can still have a local minimum; such points are also critical.

Example: Find the local extrema of $f(x)=x^{3}-3x+2$.

Step 1: $f'(x)=3x^{2}-3=3(x^{2}-1)$. Set $f'(x)=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1$ (critical points).

Step 2: $f''(x)=6x$. Evaluate:

  • At $x=1$, $f''(1)=6>0$ ⇒ local minimum. $f(1)=1-3+2=0$.
  • At $x=-1$, $f''(-1)=-6<0$ ⇒ local maximum. $f(-1)=-1+3+2=4$.

Thus $f$ has a local maximum $4$ at $x=-1$ and a local minimum $0$ at $x=1$.

All maxima and minima are expressed in the same units as $f(x)$ (e.g., meters if $f$ represents height).

Points of inflection and concavity

Definition: A point $c$ on the curve $y=f(x)$ is called a point of inflection if the concavity of the curve changes as $x$ passes through $c$.

Mathematically, if $f''(x)$ exists in an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$) and changes sign at $c$, then $c$ is a point of inflection.

  • If $f''(c)=0$ and $f''(x)$ changes sign, $c$ is a point of inflection.
  • If $f''(c)$ does not exist but $f'(x)$ has a vertical tangent and the sign of $f''$ changes, $c$ is also a point of inflection.

Test for Concavity:

ConditionConclusion
$f''(x)>0$Curve is concave upward (shaped like $\\cup$)
$f''(x)<0$Curve is concave downward (shaped like $\\cap$)

Derivation of the Test: Starting from the definition of derivative, $$f'(x)=\\lim_{h\\to0}\\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$ Differentiating again, $$f''(x)=\\lim_{h\\to0}\\frac{f'(x+h)-f'(x)}{h}$$ If $f''(x)>0$, the slope $f'(x)$ is increasing, which means the graph bends upward, etc.

Edge Cases:

  • If $f''(x)=0$ but the sign does \not change, the point is \not an inflection point (e.g., $f(x)=x^4$ at $x=0$).
  • If $f''(x)$ does \not exist at $c$ but the curvature changes, $c$ is still an inflection point (e.g., $f(x)=x^{1/3}$ at $x=0$).

Example: Find the points of inflection of $f(x)=x^3-3x$.

Solution:

  1. Compute $f'(x)=3x^2-3$.
  2. Compute $f''(x)=6x$.
  3. Set $f''(x)=0\\Rightarrow6x=0\\Rightarrow x=0$.
  4. Check sign of $f''$ around $x=0$: for $x<0$, $f''(x)<0$ (concave down); for $x>0$, $f''(x)>0$ (concave up). Sign changes, so $x=0$ is a point of inflection.
  5. Corresponding $y$‑value: $f(0)=0$. Hence the inflection point is $(0,0)$.

Units: If $x$ is measured \in meters (m) and $y$ \in meters (m), then $f''(x)$ has unit $\\text{m}^{-1}$, indicating curvature per unit length.

Rate of change of quantities

Rate of Change of Quantities – CBSE Class 12

1. Definitions

  • Average rate of change of a function $f$ between $x=a$ and $x=b$ is $$\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$$. It has units of the dependent variable per unit of the independent variable.
  • Instantaneous rate of change at $x=a$ is the limit $$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$, provided the limit exists. It is denoted by $f'(a)$ and is called the derivative.

2. Derivation of the derivative as a rate of change

Starting from the definition of average rate, let $h=x-a$. Then $$\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$. Taking the limit as $h\to0$ gives the instantaneous rate. The derivative $f'(x)$ is a function that assigns \to each $x$ the rate of change of $f$ at that point.

3. SI Units

  • If $f$ represents distance \in metres and $x$ time \in seconds, then $f'(x)$ has units m/s.
  • For velocity $v(t)$ \in m/s, acceleration $a(t)=v'(t)$ has units m/s2.

4. Edge Cases

  • Vertical tangent: derivative tends \to ±∞ (e.g., $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ at $x=0$).
  • Cusp or corner: derivative does \not exist \because \left and \right limits differ (e.g., $f(x)=|x|$ at $x=0$).

5. Example

A car’s position is given by $s(t)=4t^2+3t$ metres, where $t$ is time \in seconds. Find the instantaneous speed at $t=2$ s.

First compute the derivative: $$s'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}(4t^2+3t)=8t+3$$.

Evaluate at $t=2$: $$s'(2)=8\times2+3=19\text{ m/s}$$. Thus the car’s speed at 2 s is 19 m/s.

Note: The average speed between $t=1$ s and $t=3$ s is $$\frac{s(3)-s(1)}{3-1}=\frac{(36+9)-(4+3)}{2}=\frac{42-7}{2}=17.5\text{ m/s}$$, which differs from the instantaneous speed at $t=2$ s, illustrating the distinction between average and instantaneous rates of change.

Tangents and normals to curves

Definition: For a curve $y=f(x)$, the tangent at point $P(x_{0},y_{0})$ is the straight line that touches the curve at $P$ and has the same instantaneous rate of change as the curve there. The slope of the tangent is the derivative $m_{t}=\left. rac{dy}{dx} ight|_{x=x_{0}}$. The normal is the line perpendicular to the tangent at $P$, with slope $m_{n}=- rac{1}{m_{t}}$ (provided $m_{t} eq0$).

Derivation of Tangent Equation:

  • Let $P(x_{0},y_{0})$ be on the curve. The differential $dy$ at $x_{0}$ is $dy=m_{t},dx$.
  • Using point–slope form, the tangent line is $y-y_{0}=m_{t}(x-x_{0})$.

Normal Equation: Replace $m_{t}$ by $-1/m_{t}$ in the point–slope form: $y-y_{0}=- rac{1}{m_{t}}(x-x_{0})$.

SI Units: Coordinates $(x,y)$ are measured in metres (m). The derivative $ rac{dy}{dx}$ is dimensionless (ratio of metres to metres). Hence the slope of tangent and normal is unitless.

Edge Cases:

  • Horizontal Tangent: $m_{t}=0$ → tangent is $y=y_{0}$; normal is vertical line $x=x_{0}$.
  • Vertical Tangent: $m_{t} oinfty$ (derivative undefined). Use implicit or parametric form: if $ rac{dx}{dy} eq0$, normal slope is $- rac{dx}{dy}$.

Example: Find tangent and normal to $y=x^{3}-3x^{2}+2x$ at $x=1$.

1. Compute derivative: $\frac{dy}{dx}=3x^{2}-6x+2$.

2. At $x=1$, $m_{t}=3(1)^{2}-6(1)+2=-1$.

3. Point on curve: $y(1)=1-3+2=0$ → $P(1,0)$.

4. Tangent: $y-0=-1(x-1)$ → $y=-x+1$.

5. Normal: slope $m_{n}=1$ → $y-0=1(x-1)$ → $y=x-1$.

Thus, tangent: $y=-x+1$, normal: $y=x-1$.

Optimization problems (maximum/minimum)

Definition: An optimization problem seeks the maximum or minimum value of a real‑valued function $f(x)$ subject to given constraints. The required value is called the extremum. In physics and engineering the result is expressed in SI units (e.g., metres, joules).

Key Theorem (First‑Derivative Test): If $f$ is differentiable on an open interval and $f'(c)=0$, then:

  • if $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at $c$, $f(c)$ is a local maximum;
  • if $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$ at $c$, $f(c)$ is a local minimum.

Second‑Derivative Test: For a critical point $c$ where $f'(c)=0$:

  • if $f''(c)>0$, $f(c)$ is a local minimum;
  • if $f''(c)<0$, $f(c)$ is a local maximum;
  • if $f''(c)=0$, the test is inconclusive.

General Procedure

  1. Translate the word problem into a mathematical function $f(x)$.
  2. Identify the domain $D$ from the constraints.
  3. Compute $f'(x)$ and solve $f'(x)=0$ to obtain critical points.
  4. Apply the first‑ or second‑derivative test to classify each critical point.
  5. Evaluate $f$ at critical points and at the end‑points of $D$; the largest (or smallest) value is the required optimum.

Edge Cases

  • When the domain is closed and bounded, the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees existence of global extrema.
  • If $f'(x)$ does not exist at a point, that point must be examined separately.
  • Multiple constraints may lead to a system of equations; Lagrange multipliers are used (beyond CBSE scope).

Lengthmetre (m)
Areasquare metre (m$^2$)
Volumecubic metre (m$^3$)

Example: Find the dimensions of a rectangle of maximum area that can be inscribed in a circle of radius $r=5\ \text{m}$.

Solution:

  1. Let the rectangle have half‑sides $x$ and $y$. The circle gives $x^2+y^2=r^2$.
  2. Area $A=4xy$. From the constraint $y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$, so $A(x)=4x\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$.
  3. Differentiate: $A'(x)=4\sqrt{r^2-x^2}-\frac{4x^2}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}$.
  4. Set $A'(x)=0\;\Rightarrow\; r^2-2x^2=0\;\Rightarrow\; x=\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}$.
  5. Then $y=\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}$; the rectangle is a square of side $s=\sqrt{2}\,x=r\sqrt{2}$.
  6. Maximum area $A_{\max}=2r^2=2(5^2)=50\ \text{m}^2$.

Thus the optimal rectangle is a square of side $5\sqrt{2}\ \text{m}$ with area $50\ \text{m}^2$.

Related rates problems

Related Rates – Definition

When two or more quantities are linked by an equation and each varies with time, the rate of change of one quantity with respect to time is called a related rate. If the relation is $F(x(t),y(t),dots)=0$, then differentiating implicitly with respect to $t$ gives the chain rule: $$\frac{dF}{dt}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}+\cdots=0.$$

Step‑by‑Step Procedure

  • Identify the variables and write the governing equation.
  • Differentiate implicitly with respect \to $t$ using the chain rule.
  • Substitute known rates (e.g. $\frac{dx}{dt}$) and solve for the unknown rate.
  • Check units: if $x$ is \in metres and $t$ \in seconds, then $\frac{dx}{dt}$ is \in $\mathrm{m/s}$.

SI Units & Edge Cases

  • All quantities should be expressed \in SI units before differentiation.
  • If a variable is independent of time, its derivative is zero.
  • When the denominator \in the differentiated equation becomes zero, the related rate may be undefined or infinite; interpret physically (e.g. a point moving at a cusp).

Example – Water Tank

A cylindrical tank of radius $r=2\,\mathrm{m}$ is being filled at a constant rate $\frac{dV}{dt}=5\,\mathrm{m^3/s}$. Find $\frac{dh}{dt}$ when the water depth $h=3\,\mathrm{m}$.

StepEquationSubstitution
1Volume of cylinder: $V=\pi r^2h$
2Differentiate: $\frac{dV}{dt}=\pi r^2\frac{dh}{dt}$
3Insert known values: $5=\pi(2)^2\frac{dh}{dt}$
4Solve: $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{5}{4\pi}\approx0.398\,\mathrm{m/s}$

Thus the water level rises at approximately $0.398\,\mathrm{m/s}$ when $h=3\,\mathrm{m}$.

Sketching curves using first and second derivative tests

Definition: Sketching a curve using the first and second derivative tests involves locating critical points, determining intervals of increase/decrease, and identifying concavity and points of inflection.

Key Steps & Formulas

  • Find $f'(x)$ and solve $f'(x)=0$ or $f'(x)$ undefined → critical points.
  • First–derivative test: sign of $f'(x)$ on either side of a critical point decides local maxima/minima.
  • Second derivative $f''(x)$ gives concavity: $f''(x)>0$ → concave upward, $f''(x)<0$ → concave downward.
  • Point of inflection where $f''(x)=0$ and sign of $f''(x)$ changes.
  • Units: If $y$ in meters and $x$ in seconds, $f'(x)$ in $\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}$, $f''(x)$ in $\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}$.

Derivation of Second–Derivative Test

For a critical point $c$ with $f'(c)=0$, expand $f(x)$ near $c$ using Taylor series:

$$f(x)=f(c)+\\frac{f''(c)}{2}(x-c)^2+\\dots$$

If $f''(c)>0$, the quadratic term is positive → local minimum; if $f''(c)<0$ → local maximum; if $f''(c)=0$ test is inconclusive.

Example

Sketch $f(x)=x^3-3x^2+2$.

StepComputation
First derivative$f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2)$
Critical points$f'(x)=0\\Rightarrow x=0,2$
Second derivative$f''(x)=6x-6$
Concavity$f''(0)=-6<0$ (max), $f''(2)=6>0$ (min)
Inflection$f''(x)=0\\Rightarrow x=1$, sign changes → inflection

Sign chart of $f'(x)$: $(-\\infty,0)$ (+), $(0,2)$ (–), $(2,\\infty)$ (+). Hence $x=0$ is a local maximum, $x=2$ a local minimum. Plot points $(0,2)$, $(2,-2)$, inflection at $(1,0)$. The curve rises, falls, rises again, matching the derivative analysis.

Applications of derivatives in physics and economics

Key Definitions (Physics)

  • Displacement $s(t)$ – position of a particle at time $t$ (unit: m).
  • Velocity $v(t)=\frac{ds}{dt}$ – instantaneous rate of change of displacement (unit: $\text{m\,s}^{-1}$).
  • Acceleration $a(t)=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$ – instantaneous rate of change of velocity (unit: $\text{m\,s}^{-2}$).

Key Definitions (Economics)

  • Cost function $C(q)$ – total cost for producing $q$ units (unit: $\text{₹}$).
  • Revenue function $R(q)$ – total revenue from selling $q$ units (unit: $\text{₹}$).
  • Profit $\Pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)$.
  • Marginal Cost $MC=\frac{dC}{dq}$ (₹ per unit).
  • Marginal Revenue $MR=\frac{dR}{dq}$ (₹ per unit).
  • Price Elasticity of Demand $E=\frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}$ (dimensionless).

Important Formulas

ConceptFormula
Velocity$v=\frac{ds}{dt}$
Acceleration$a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$
Marginal Cost$MC=\frac{dC}{dq}$
Marginal Revenue$MR=\frac{dR}{dq}$
Maximum/Minimum Condition$\frac{d\Pi}{dq}=0$ and $\frac{d^2\Pi}{dq^2}<0$ for maximum

Sample Problem (Profit Maximisation)

Given $C(q)=5q^{2}+200$ and $R(q)=20q-0.5q^{2}$, find the quantity $q$ that yields maximum profit.

Solution:

  1. Profit $\Pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)=\bigl(20q-0.5q^{2}\bigr)-\bigl(5q^{2}+200\bigr)=20q-5.5q^{2}-200$.
  2. First derivative $\frac{d\Pi}{dq}=20-11q$.
  3. Set $\frac{d\Pi}{dq}=0\Rightarrow20-11q=0\Rightarrow q=\frac{20}{11}\approx1.82$ units.
  4. Second derivative $\frac{d^{2}\Pi}{dq^{2}}=-11<0$, confirming a maximum.
  5. Maximum profit $\Pi_{max}=20\left(\frac{20}{11}\right)-5.5\left(\frac{20}{11}\right)^{2}-200\approx-180.36\,\text{₹}$ (negative profit indicates the given cost‑revenue structure is not viable).

This illustrates how derivatives provide instantaneous rates in physics and marginal analysis in economics, enabling optimisation of motion and business decisions.
