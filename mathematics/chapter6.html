Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Definition & Scope
Application of Derivatives – An Overview

The chapter “Application of Derivatives” deals with the utilisation of the first and second derivatives of a function to solve real‑world problems. While the preceding chapter establishes the rules of differentiation, this chapter translates those rules into tools for analysing the behaviour of curves, optimisation, and rates of change. The central concepts include monotonicity, extrema, concavity, and points of inflection. These are expressed mathematically through the sign of $f'(x)$ and $f''(x)$. For a differentiable function $f$, the following statements are fundamental:

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{If }f'(x)>0\text{ on an interval }I,\text{ then }f\text{ is increasing on }I;\\ &\text{If }f'(x)<0\text{ on }I,\text{ then }f\text{ is decreasing on }I;\\ &\text{If }f'(x)=0\text{ at }c\text{ and changes sign, }c\text{ is a local extremum.}\\ &\text{If }f''(x)>0\text{ on }I,\text{ then }f\text{ is concave upward (convex) on }I;\\ &\text{If }f''(x)<0\text{ on }I,\text{ then }f\text{ is concave downward (concave) on }I.\\ &\text{If }f''(c)=0\text{ and the sign of }f''\text{ changes at }c,\text{ then }c\text{ is a point of inflection.} \end{aligned}$$

The chapter also introduces the Mean Value Theorem (MVT) and its corollaries, which provide a bridge between average and instantaneous rates of change. The MVT states that for a function $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there exists a point $\xi\in(a,b)$ such that $$f'(\xi)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$ This theorem underpins many of the optimisation results derived later. The scope of the chapter extends to applications in physics (velocity and acceleration), economics (maximising profit, minimising cost), and geometry (tangents, normals, and curvature). Understanding these applications requires fluency in interpreting derivative information in the context of the problem statement.

Key Theorems & Formulas
First and Second Derivative Tests

The First Derivative Test provides a systematic procedure to classify stationary points. Let $c$ be a point where $f'(c)=0$ and $f$ is continuous around $c$. Examine the sign of $f'$ on intervals immediately to the left and right of $c$:

  • If $f'$ changes from positive to negative, $c$ is a local maximum.
  • If $f'$ changes from negative to positive, $c$ is a local minimum.
  • If $f'$ does not change sign, $c$ is a stationary point of inflection.

The Second Derivative Test offers a quicker alternative when $f''(c)$ exists and $f'(c)=0$: $$\begin{cases} f''(c)>0 &\Rightarrow\; c\text{ is a local minimum},\\[4pt] f''(c)<0 &\Rightarrow\; c\text{ is a local maximum},\\[4pt] f''(c)=0 &\Rightarrow\; \text{Test inconclusive; revert to First Derivative Test.} \end{cases}$$ These tests are indispensable for solving optimisation problems, where the objective is to find the maximum or minimum value of a quantity subject to given constraints.

Another pivotal result is the Rolle’s Theorem, a special case of the MVT. If $f(a)=f(b)$, then there exists at least one $\xi\in(a,b)$ such that $f'(\xi)=0$. This theorem guarantees the existence of a stationary point when the function’s values at the endpoints coincide, a fact often exploited in proving the existence of roots or extrema.

The chapter also formalises the concept of increasing/decreasing intervals using the derivative sign chart, and introduces the notion of concavity via the second derivative. These ideas culminate in the definition of a point of inflection, where the curvature of the graph changes sign, a critical feature in sketching accurate curves.

Intuitive Insight
Geometric Interpretation of Derivatives

Visualising derivatives as slopes of tangents provides an intuitive grasp of the algebraic criteria. Consider a smooth curve $y=f(x)$. At any point $(c,f(c))$, the tangent line has equation $$y-f(c)=f'(c)(x-c).$$ The sign of $f'(c)$ tells us whether the tangent tilts upward (positive slope) or downward (negative slope). When $f'(c)=0$, the tangent is horizontal, signalling a potential extremum or a point of inflection.

The second derivative $f''(c)$ measures the rate at which the slope itself changes. If $f''(c)>0$, the slope is increasing, meaning the curve bends upward – a convex shape. Conversely, $f''(c)<0$ indicates a decreasing slope and a concave shape. This curvature interpretation is crucial when sketching graphs: after locating stationary points via $f'(x)=0$, one inspects $f''(x)$ to decide whether each stationary point is a peak, a trough, or a flat inflection.

In physics, $f'(t)$ often represents velocity, while $f''(t)$ represents acceleration. A positive acceleration ($f''>0$) means the velocity is increasing – the object speeds up. A negative acceleration indicates deceleration. This direct mapping between mathematical symbols and physical quantities reinforces the practical relevance of the derivative tests.

Economically, if $P(x)$ denotes profit as a function of production level $x$, then $P'(x)$ is the marginal profit. Maximising profit requires solving $P'(x)=0$ and applying the second derivative test to ensure $P''(x)<0$, confirming a peak profit. This analogy illustrates how the abstract language of calculus translates into concrete decision‑making tools.

Exceptions & Special Cases
When Standard Tests Fail

Although the First and Second Derivative Tests are powerful, they are not universally decisive. Situations where $f'(c)=0$ but $f''(c)=0$ constitute a classic exception. In such cases, higher‑order derivatives must be examined. If the first non‑zero derivative after $f'(c)$ is of even order $k$, the point $c$ is a local extremum: a minimum if the derivative is positive, a maximum if negative. If the first non‑zero derivative is of odd order, $c$ is a point of inflection.

Example: $f(x)=x^4$ has $f'(0)=0$, $f''(0)=0$, $f^{(3)}(0)=0$, but $f^{(4)}(0)=24>0$. Since the first non‑zero derivative after the first derivative is of even order and positive, $x=0$ is a local minimum. Conversely, $g(x)=x^3$ yields $g'(0)=0$, $g''(0)=0$, $g^{(3)}(0)=6\neq0$ (odd order), indicating an inflection point at the origin.

Another subtlety arises with functions that are not differentiable at a candidate point. For instance, $h(x)=|x|$ has a cusp at $x=0$; $h'(0)$ does not exist, yet $x=0$ is a global minimum. In such cases, one must resort to the definition of minima: $h(0)\le h(x)$ for all $x$ in a neighbourhood, bypassing derivative tests entirely.

The Mean Value Theorem also has strict hypotheses: continuity on $[a,b]$ and differentiability on $(a,b)$. If either condition fails, the conclusion may not hold. For example, $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x=0$, yet the MVT still happens to hold; however, a function like $f(x)=|x|$ on $[-1,1]$ violates differentiability at $0$, and no $\xi$ satisfying $f'(\xi)=\frac{f(1)-f(-1)}{2}$ exists because $f'$ is undefined at $0$ and the slopes on either side are $\pm1$.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation of the Integration by Parts Formula

Integration by parts is a direct consequence of the product rule for differentiation. Let $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ be two differentiable functions on an interval $I$. The product rule states

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u(x)v(x)\bigr]=u'(x)v(x)+u(x)v'(x).$$

Integrating both sides with respect to $x$ over a closed interval $[a,b]\subset I$ gives

$$\int_{a}^{b}\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[u(x)v(x)\bigr]dx=\int_{a}^{b}u'(x)v(x)\,dx+\int_{a}^{b}u(x)v'(x)\,dx.$$

The left‑hand side simplifies by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)=\int_{a}^{b}u'(x)v(x)\,dx+\int_{a}^{b}u(x)v'(x)\,dx.$$

Rearranging terms yields the definite‑integral version of integration by parts:

$$\int_{a}^{b}u(x)v'(x)\,dx = u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)-\int_{a}^{b}u'(x)v(x)\,dx.$$

If we denote $u=u(x)$, $dv=v'(x)dx$, $du=u'(x)dx$, and $v=\int dv$, the compact notation becomes

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du}$$

For indefinite integrals the constant of integration $C$ is added after the final integration.

Key Conditions

  • Both $u$ and $v$ must be continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$.
  • The integral $\int v\,du$ must exist (i.e., be finite).

Reduction Formula for $\displaystyle\int \sin^{n}x\,dx$

Reduction formulas are indispensable when dealing with powers of trigonometric functions. Consider the integral

$$I_n=\int \sin^{n}x\,dx,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.$$

We separate one sine factor and rewrite the remaining power using the identity $\sin^{2}x=1-\cos^{2}x$:

$$I_n=\int \sin^{n-1}x\;\sin x\,dx =\int \sin^{n-1}x\;(1-\cos^{2}x)^{\frac12}\,dx,$$

but a more systematic approach uses integration by parts. Let

$$u=\sin^{n-1}x,\qquad dv=\sin x\,dx.$$

Then $du=(n-1)\sin^{n-2}x\cos x\,dx$ and $v=-\cos x$. Applying the integration‑by‑parts formula:

$$I_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)\int \sin^{n-2}x\cos^{2}x\,dx.$$

Replace $\cos^{2}x$ by $1-\sin^{2}x$:

$$I_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)\int \sin^{n-2}x\,(1-\sin^{2}x)\,dx$$ $$= -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)\int \sin^{n-2}x\,dx - (n-1)\int \sin^{n}x\,dx.$$

Recognising the last integral as $I_n$ and the middle one as $I_{n-2}$, we obtain

$$I_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)I_{n-2} - (n-1)I_n.$$

Collecting like terms:

$$(n)I_n = -\sin^{n-1}x\cos x + (n-1)I_{n-2}$$ $$\boxed{\displaystyle I_n = -\frac{\sin^{\,n-1}x\cos x}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\,I_{n-2}}$$

This reduction formula reduces the power $n$ by two at each step, eventually reaching the base cases $I_0=\int dx = x$ and $I_1=-\cos x$.

Variables and Units

  • $x$ – angle measured in radians (SI unit: rad).
  • $I_n$ – indefinite integral, dimensionless (since sine is dimensionless).
  • $n$ – positive integer (dimensionless).

Conditions for Validity

  • The formula holds for all integer $n\ge 2$.
  • Function $\sin x$ must be continuous on the interval of integration (always true).

Derivation of the Definite Integral as Area Under a Curve

The geometric interpretation of a definite integral $\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx$ as the signed area between the curve $y=f(x)$ and the $x$‑axis is a cornerstone of calculus. The derivation begins with a partition of $[a,b]$ into $n$ sub‑intervals of equal width $\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}$.

Choose a sample point $x_i^{*}$ in each sub‑interval $[x_{i-1},x_i]$, where $x_i=a+i\Delta x$. Form the Riemann sum

$$S_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\bigl(x_i^{*}\bigr)\,\Delta x.$$

If $f$ is bounded and integrable on $[a,b]$, the limit of $S_n$ as $n\to\infty$ exists and is independent of the choice of sample points. By definition,

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\bigl(x_i^{*}\bigr)\,\Delta x}$$

Geometrically, each term $f(x_i^{*})\Delta x$ approximates the area of a thin rectangle of width $\Delta x$ and height $f(x_i^{*})$. As $n$ grows, the rectangles become infinitesimally thin, and the sum converges to the exact signed area.

When $f(x)\ge0$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, the integral equals the ordinary (positive) area. If $f(x)$ changes sign, the integral computes the algebraic sum of areas above and below the $x$‑axis, giving a signed result.

Variables and Units

  • $x$ – independent variable (SI unit depends on context, e.g., meters, seconds).
  • $f(x)$ – dependent quantity; its unit is the product of the unit of the integral’s result divided by the unit of $x$.
  • $\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx$ – area, unit = (unit of $f$) × (unit of $x$).
  • $a,b$ – limits of integration, same unit as $x$.

  • $f$ must be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ (continuous functions satisfy this automatically).

Substitution Method (Change of Variable) Derivation

Many integrals become tractable after a suitable change of variable. Suppose we have an integral of the form

$$\int f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x)\,dx.$$

Let $u=g(x)$. Then $du=g'(x)\,dx$, and the integral transforms to

$$\int f(u)\,du,$$

which is often simpler to evaluate. To justify this rigorously, start from the differential relationship $du=g'(x)\,dx$. Solving for $dx$ gives $dx=\dfrac{du}{g'(x)}$. Substituting into the original integral:

$$\int f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x)\,\frac{du}{g'(x)} = \int f(u)\,du.$$

Thus the substitution is valid provided the mapping $u=g(x)$ is differentiable and its derivative $g'(x)$ does not vanish on the interval of interest.

For a definite integral $\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x)\,dx$, the limits must also be transformed:

$$u=g(a)\quad\text{and}\quad u=g(b).$$

The transformed integral becomes

$$\boxed{\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du}$$

**Example**: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin^{2}x\cos x\,dx$.

Set $u=\sin x$, so $du=\cos x\,dx$. When $x=0$, $u=0$; when $x=\pi/2$, $u=1$. The integral becomes

$$\int_{0}^{1}u^{2}\,du = \left.\frac{u^{3}}{3}\right|_{0}^{1}= \frac13.$$

This illustrates the power of substitution in reducing a trigonometric integral to a simple polynomial integral.
