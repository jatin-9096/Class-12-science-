1. Conceptual Framework

Definitions & Foundations
What is a Matrix?

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, symbols, or expressions arranged in rows and columns. The size of a matrix is denoted by its order $m \times n$, where $m$ is the number of rows and $n$ is the number of columns. An individual entry of a matrix $A$ is written as $a_{ij}$, where $i$ ( $1\le i\le m$ ) indicates the row and $j$ ( $1\le j\le n$ ) indicates the column. For example, $$A=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0\\ 5 & 3 & 4\\ -2 & 7 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ is a $3\times3$ matrix.

Matrices are classified according to their shape and the nature of their elements. A matrix with the same number of rows and columns ($m=n$) is called a square matrix. Special square matrices include:

  • Diagonal matrix: all non‑diagonal entries are zero, e.g., $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,\dots,d_n)$.
  • Scalar matrix: a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are all equal, i.e., $kI_n$ where $I_n$ is the identity matrix.
  • Identity matrix ($I_n$): a diagonal matrix with ones on the main diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
  • Zero (null) matrix: every entry is zero, denoted $0_{m\times n}$.
  • Symmetric matrix: $A^T=A$, meaning $a_{ij}=a_{ji}$ for all $i,j$.
  • Skew‑symmetric matrix: $A^T=-A$, implying $a_{ii}=0$ and $a_{ij}=-a_{ji}$.
  • Upper (lower) triangular matrix: all entries below (above) the main diagonal are zero.
Understanding these classifications is crucial because many algebraic properties and computational techniques depend on the type of matrix under consideration.

Matrix Operations
Addition, Subtraction, and Scalar Multiplication

Two matrices $A$ and $B$ can be added or subtracted only if they have the same order $m\times n$. The operation is performed element‑wise: $$ (A\pm B)_{ij}=a_{ij}\pm b_{ij}. $$ For example, $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix},\; B=\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; A+B=\begin{bmatrix}6&8\\10&12\end{bmatrix}.$$

Scalar multiplication involves multiplying every entry of a matrix by a real number $k$: $$ (kA)_{ij}=k\,a_{ij}. $$ If $k=3$ and $A$ is as above, then $3A=\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\9&12\end{bmatrix}$.

These operations satisfy the usual algebraic properties: commutativity of addition, associativity of both addition and scalar multiplication, and distributivity of scalar multiplication over matrix addition.

Matrix Multiplication & Transpose
Product of Matrices and Its Properties

The product $AB$ of an $m\times n$ matrix $A$ and an $n\times p$ matrix $B$ is defined only when the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$. The resulting matrix $C=AB$ has order $m\times p$, and each entry is obtained by the dot‑product of the corresponding row of $A$ with the column of $B$: $$c_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}b_{kj}.$$ For instance, $$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix},\; B=\begin{bmatrix}0&5\\6&7\end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; AB=\begin{bmatrix}1\cdot0+2\cdot6 & 1\cdot5+2\cdot7\\ 3\cdot0+4\cdot6 & 3\cdot5+4\cdot7\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}12&19\\24&43\end{bmatrix}.$$

Matrix multiplication is associative, i.e., $(AB)C=A(BC)$, but it is generally **not** commutative: $AB\neq BA$ in most cases. The transpose of a matrix, denoted $A^{T}$, flips rows into columns: $(A^{T})_{ij}=a_{ji}$. Important identities include $$ (A+B)^{T}=A^{T}+B^{T},\qquad (kA)^{T}=kA^{T},\qquad (AB)^{T}=B^{T}A^{T}. $$ The transpose of a symmetric matrix is the matrix itself, while the transpose of a skew‑symmetric matrix changes its sign.

Special Matrices & Inverses
Identity, Orthogonal, and Invertible Matrices

The identity matrix $I_n$ acts as the multiplicative identity: $AI_n=A$ and $I_nA=A$ for any $n\times n$ matrix $A$. An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is called invertible (or non‑singular) if there exists a matrix $A^{-1}$ such that $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_n$. The inverse, when it exists, is unique and can be computed by elementary row operations or by the adjoint method (using cofactors). A necessary and sufficient condition for invertibility is $\det A\neq0$ (the determinant concept is explored in Chapter 4).

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ satisfies $Q^{T}Q=QQ^{T}=I_n$, implying $Q^{-1}=Q^{T}$. Orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean length and angle, making them indispensable in geometry and computer graphics. For example, $$Q=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$$ is orthogonal because $Q^{T}Q=I_2$.

Certain matrices are never invertible: the zero matrix, any matrix with a row (or column) of zeros, and matrices with linearly dependent rows/columns. Recognising these exceptions early prevents futile attempts at finding an inverse.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivations and Formula Compendium for Chapter 3 – Matrices

In this section we present a systematic derivation of the most frequently used matrix results in the CBSE Class 12 syllabus. Each derivation follows the standard CBSE‑approved logical flow, beginning from the definition of a matrix and proceeding step‑by‑step to the final compact formula. All symbols are defined in the accompanying formula list, together with their SI units (where applicable) and the conditions under which the formula holds true.

1. Definition of a Matrix and Notation

Let $A$ be a rectangular array of real numbers (or complex numbers) arranged in $m$ rows and $n$ columns. The element situated at the intersection of the $i^{\text{th}}$ row and $j^{\text{th}}$ column is denoted by $a_{ij}$. Hence,

$$A=\begin{bmatrix} a_{11}&a_{12}&\dots&a_{1n}\\ a_{21}&a_{22}&\dots&a_{2n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ a_{m1}&a_{m2}&\dots&a_{mn} \end{bmatrix},\qquad a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}\;(\text{or }\mathbb{C}).$$

The order of $A$ is written as $m\times n$. When $m=n$, $A$ is called a square matrix. The set of all $n\times n$ real matrices is denoted by $\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R})$.

2. Matrix Addition and Scalar Multiplication

For two matrices $A=(a_{ij})$ and $B=(b_{ij})$ of the same order $m\times n$, the sum $C=A+B$ is defined element‑wise:

$$c_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij},\qquad 1\le i\le m,\;1\le j\le n.$$

Scalar multiplication by a real number $k$ is similarly defined:

$$ (kA)_{ij}=k\,a_{ij}.$$

Both operations satisfy the commutative, associative, and distributive laws, which can be proved directly from the field properties of real numbers. For instance, commutativity of addition follows because $a_{ij}+b_{ij}=b_{ij}+a_{ij}$ for each pair $(i,j)$.

3. Matrix Multiplication – Derivation from Linear Transformations

Consider two matrices $A$ of order $m\times p$ and $B$ of order $p\times n$. The product $C=AB$ is defined only when the inner dimensions match ($p$). The entry $c_{ij}$ of $C$ is obtained by taking the dot product of the $i^{\text{th}}$ row of $A$ with the $j^{\text{th}}$ column of $B$:

$$c_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_{ik}b_{kj},\qquad 1\le i\le m,\;1\le j\le n.$$

**Derivation** – Let $A$ represent a linear transformation $T_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}$ and $B$ represent $T_{B}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}$. The composition $T_{A}\circ T_{B}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}$ and is represented by the matrix product $AB$. For a vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$,

$$T_{A}(T_{B}(x))=A(Bx)=(AB)x.$$

Expanding $Bx$ yields a $p$‑dimensional vector whose $k^{\text{th}}$ component is $\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{kj}x_{j}$. Multiplying $A$ with this vector gives the $i^{\text{th}}$ component of $ABx$ as $\sum_{k=1}^{p}a_{ik}\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{kj}x_{j}\bigr)$. Re‑ordering the sums leads directly to the definition of $c_{ij}$ above.

4. Properties of Matrix Multiplication

  • Associativity: $(AB)C=A(BC)$ for conformable matrices.
  • Distributivity: $A(B+C)=AB+AC$ and $(A+B)C=AC+BC$.
  • Non‑commutativity: In general $AB\neq BA$. Equality holds only under special conditions (e.g., both matrices are diagonal and of the same order, or they are scalar multiples of the identity).

5. Transpose of a Matrix

The transpose of $A$, denoted $A^{\mathsf{T}}$, interchanges rows and columns:

$$ (A^{\mathsf{T}})_{ij}=a_{ji},\qquad 1\le i\le n,\;1\le j\le m.$$

Key properties (proved by element‑wise inspection) are:

  • $(A^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}=A$.
  • $(A+B)^{\mathsf{T}}=A^{\mathsf{T}}+B^{\mathsf{T}}$.
  • $(kA)^{\mathsf{T}}=kA^{\mathsf{T}}$ for any scalar $k$.
  • $(AB)^{\mathsf{T}}=B^{\mathsf{T}}A^{\mathsf{T}}$ (reverse order).

6. Determinant of a Square Matrix – Laplace Expansion

For a $2\times2$ matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}$, the determinant is defined as $\det A=ad-bc$. For higher orders, the determinant is defined recursively using the Laplace expansion along any row (or column). Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix and $C_{ij}$ be the cofactor of element $a_{ij}$, where

$$C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\,M_{ij},$$

and $M_{ij}$ is the minor obtained by deleting the $i^{\text{th}}$ row and $j^{\text{th}}$ column. Then the determinant is

$$\det A=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}C_{ij}\quad\text{(expansion along the $i^{\text{th}}$ row)}.$$

**Proof Sketch** – The determinant is the unique alternating multilinear function of the rows that equals $1$ for the identity matrix. By multilinearity, expanding along a row reduces the $n$‑dimensional problem to an $(n-1)$‑dimensional one, giving the recursive formula above.

7. Inverse of a Non‑Singular Square Matrix

A square matrix $A$ of order $n$ is invertible (or non‑singular) if $\det A\neq0$. The inverse $A^{-1}$ satisfies $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I_n$, where $I_n$ is the identity matrix. The classical adjoint method gives

$$A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\,\operatorname{adj}(A),$$

where $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is the adjugate (or adjoint) matrix, i.e., the transpose of the cofactor matrix:

$$\operatorname{adj}(A)=\bigl[C_{ji}\bigr]_{1\le i,j\le n}.$$

**Derivation** – Starting from the definition of cofactors, one can show that $A\,\operatorname{adj}(A)=\det A\,I_n$. Multiplying both sides by $\frac{1}{\det A}$ (possible only when $\det A\neq0$) yields the inverse formula.

8. Cramer's Rule – Solving a System of Linear Equations

Consider a system $AX=B$, where $A$ is an $n\times n$ non‑singular coefficient matrix, $X$ is the column vector of unknowns $[x_1,\dots,x_n]^{\mathsf{T}}$, and $B$ is the constant column vector. Cramer's rule states that each unknown $x_i$ is given by

$$x_i=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A},\qquad i=1,2,\dots,n,$$

where $A_i$ is obtained from $A$ by replacing its $i^{\text{th}}$ column with $B$. The proof uses the linearity of the determinant with respect to a single column and the fact that $A^{-1}B$ yields the unique solution.

9. Rank of a Matrix

The rank $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ is the maximum order of any non‑zero minor of $A$. Equivalently, it equals the dimension of the vector space spanned by the rows (row rank) or columns (column rank). The equality of row rank and column rank is a fundamental theorem proved using elementary row operations and the concept of linear independence.

10. Elementary Row Operations and Row‑Echelon Form

Three elementary row operations (EROs) preserve the solution set of a linear system:

  1. Swap two rows: $R_i\leftrightarrow R_j$.
  2. Multiply a row by a non‑zero scalar $k$: $R_i\to kR_i$, $k\neq0$.
  3. Add a multiple of one row to another: $R_i\to R_i+kR_j$.

Applying a finite sequence of EROs transforms $A$ into its row‑echelon form (REF) or reduced row‑echelon form (RREF). The number of non‑zero rows in REF equals $\operatorname{rank}(A)$.
