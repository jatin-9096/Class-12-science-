Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Definition
Continuity of a Real‑Valued Function

Let $f$ be a real‑valued function defined on an interval $I\subset\mathbb{R}$. The function $f$ is said to be continuous at a point $c\in I$ if the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ exists and equals the function value at $c$, i.e. $$\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c).$$ This condition can be unpacked into three elementary requirements: 1) $f(c)$ is defined (the point $c$ belongs to the domain of $f$); 2) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)$ exists (the left‑hand and right‑hand limits coincide); 3) the limit equals the actual function value. When the above three statements hold for every point of $I$, we say that $f$ is continuous on the interval $I$.

The notion of continuity captures the intuitive idea that the graph of $f$ can be drawn without lifting the pen. Formally, the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition provides a rigorous foundation: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a $\delta>0$ such that $$|x-c|<\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon.$$ This definition is indispensable when proving theorems that involve limits, such as the Intermediate Value Theorem.

Important subclasses of continuous functions include polynomial functions, rational functions whose denominator does not vanish, exponential and logarithmic functions, and trigonometric functions on their natural domains. Each of these satisfies the three‑step test automatically because of their algebraic or analytic construction.

Definition
Differentiability and Its Link with Continuity

A function $f$ defined on an open interval $I$ is differentiable at a point $c\in I$ if the limit $$f'(c)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}$$ exists as a finite real number. The quantity $f'(c)$ is called the derivative of $f$ at $c$ and represents the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$ at that point.

Differentiability imposes a stricter condition than continuity. In fact, if $f$ is differentiable at $c$, then $f$ must be continuous at $c$. The proof follows directly from the definition: if the difference quotient tends to a finite limit, the numerator $f(c+h)-f(c)$ must tend to $0$ as $h\to0$, which forces $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)$. The converse, however, is false; a function may be continuous at a point yet fail to possess a derivative there (e.g., $f(x)=|x|$ at $x=0$).

Higher‑order derivatives are defined recursively: if $f'$ exists on an interval and is itself differentiable, then $f''(x)=\bigl(f'(x)\bigr)'$ is the second derivative, and so on. The existence of higher‑order derivatives provides deeper insight into the curvature and concavity of the graph, which is crucial for topics such as Taylor’s series and the study of extrema.

In the CBSE curriculum, differentiability is examined not only for elementary algebraic functions but also for piecewise‑defined functions, where the left‑hand and right‑hand derivatives must coincide for differentiability at the junction point.

Theorem
Fundamental Theorems on Continuous and Differentiable Functions

Several cornerstone theorems connect continuity, differentiability, and the behavior of functions on closed intervals. The most frequently used in Class 12 are listed below, each accompanied by a concise statement and a brief discussion of its implications.

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT): If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$ and $k$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c\in(a,b)$ such that $f(c)=k$. The IVT guarantees the existence of roots for equations $f(x)=0$ when $f$ changes sign, a principle exploited in solving equations numerically.

Rolle’s Theorem: Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and satisfy $f(a)=f(b)$. Then there exists at least one $c\in(a,b)$ with $f'(c)=0$. Geometrically, the theorem asserts that a smooth curve that starts and ends at the same height must have a horizontal tangent somewhere in between.

Mean Value Theorem (MVT): If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there exists a point $c\in(a,b)$ such that $$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$ The MVT generalises Rolle’s theorem and provides a bridge between average rates of change and instantaneous rates. It is instrumental in proving inequalities, establishing monotonicity, and justifying error estimates in numerical methods.

Extreme Value Theorem (EVT): A continuous function on a closed bounded interval $[a,b]$ attains both a maximum and a minimum value on that interval. This theorem underlies the existence of global extrema, which are later examined using the first and second derivative tests.

These theorems are not merely abstract results; they form the logical scaffolding for many problem‑solving strategies in the CBSE examinations, especially in questions that ask for justification of existence or for locating critical points.

Intuition
Geometric and Physical Interpretation

The algebraic definitions of continuity and differentiability acquire vivid meaning when visualised on the Cartesian plane.

Continuity corresponds to a graph that can be traced without interruption. Any “jump” or “hole” represents a discontinuity, which mathematically translates to a failure of the limit to equal the function value. In physics, continuity of a position‑time graph ensures that an object does not teleport instantaneously; the motion is smooth.

Differentiability is linked to the existence of a unique tangent line at a point. The derivative $f'(c)$ equals the slope of this tangent. If the graph has a sharp corner (as in $y=|x|$ at $x=0$) or a vertical tangent (as in $y=\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $x=0$), the derivative fails to exist because the limit of the difference quotient does not settle to a single finite value.

From a physical perspective, $f'(t)$ represents instantaneous velocity when $f(t)$ denotes displacement. The requirement of continuity for velocity is natural: an object cannot have an infinite jump in speed without an external impulse, which would be modelled by a discontinuity in the acceleration function.

The Mean Value Theorem can be visualised as guaranteeing a point where the instantaneous slope matches the average slope over the whole interval. This is akin to saying that, during a car trip from point $A$ to $B$, there is at least one instant when the speedometer reads exactly the average speed of the journey. Such intuition helps students remember the theorem’s statement and apply it correctly in proofs.

Understanding these geometric and physical analogies not only aids memory but also deepens conceptual clarity, which is essential for tackling higher‑order problems involving curvature, optimisation, and error analysis.

2. Master Equation Bank

Standard Form of a Linear Programming Problem

In the CBSE Class‑12 syllabus a linear programming problem (LPP) is first expressed in its standard form. Let the decision variables be $x_1, x_2, \dots , x_n$. The objective is to maximize (or minimize) a linear function $$Z = c_1x_1 + c_2x_2 + \dots + c_nx_n,$$ where $c_i$ are real coefficients. The constraints are linear inequalities of the type $$a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 + \dots + a_{in}x_n \;\;(\le,\;=,\;\ge)\; b_i,\qquad i=1,2,\dots ,m,$$ together with the non‑negativity conditions $x_j \ge 0\;(j=1,\dots ,n)$.

Derivation Steps:

  1. Identify the objective. If the problem asks for a minimum, multiply the whole objective by $-1$ to convert it into a maximisation problem, because the theory of the corner‑point theorem is usually presented for maximisation.
  2. Convert all constraints to “$\le$” form.
    • If a constraint is already “$\le$”, keep it unchanged.
    • If a constraint is “$\ge$”, multiply both sides by $-1$ to obtain an equivalent “$\le$” inequality.
    • If a constraint is an equality “$=$”, replace it by two opposite inequalities: one “$\le$” and one “$\ge$”. Then treat the “$\ge$” part as in the previous bullet.
  3. Introduce slack variables. For each inequality $a_{i1}x_1+\dots +a_{in}x_n \le b_i$, add a non‑negative slack variable $s_i$ such that $$a_{i1}x_1+\dots +a_{in}x_n + s_i = b_i,\qquad s_i\ge 0.$$ Slack variables transform the system into a set of linear equations, which is essential for the simplex tableau.
  4. Write the augmented system. Collect all equations (original equalities, transformed inequalities, and slack variables) together. The resulting matrix form is $$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b},\qquad \mathbf{x}\ge 0,$$ where $\mathbf{x}$ now includes the original decision variables and the slack variables.

Graphical Method – Corner‑Point Theorem

The graphical method is applicable when there are exactly two decision variables ($x_1$ and $x_2$). The cornerstone of this method is the corner‑point theorem, which states that if an optimal solution exists, it occurs at a vertex (corner point) of the feasible region.

Derivation of the Corner‑Point Theorem:

  1. Consider the feasible region $\mathcal{F}$ defined by a finite set of linear inequalities. $\mathcal{F}$ is a convex polygon (or an unbounded convex set) in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. Let $Z = c_1x_1 + c_2x_2$ be the objective function. For any constant $k$, the set $\{(x_1,x_2)\mid Z = k\}$ is a straight line with slope $-\frac{c_1}{c_2}$.
  3. Moving this line parallel to itself in the direction of increasing $Z$ (for maximisation) will eventually touch $\mathcal{F}$ at its outermost point. Because $\mathcal{F}$ is convex, the first point of contact must be a boundary point.
  4. If the line touches $\mathcal{F}$ at a single point, that point is a vertex. If it coincides with an edge, every point on that edge yields the same optimal value; nevertheless, the extreme points (the two vertices of that edge) are also optimal.
  5. Hence, at least one optimal solution lies at a vertex of $\mathcal{F}$.

Step‑by‑Step Graphical Procedure:
  1. Plot each linear constraint as an equality line on the $x_1$–$x_2$ plane.
  2. Shade the region that satisfies each inequality. The intersection of all shaded regions gives the feasible polygon.
  3. Identify all vertices of the feasible polygon. For two constraints intersecting, solve the simultaneous equations: $$\begin{cases} a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 = b_i\\ a_{j1}x_1 + a_{j2}x_2 = b_j \end{cases}$$ to obtain the coordinates $(x_1^{(k)},x_2^{(k)})$ of the $k^{th}$ vertex.
  4. Substitute each vertex into the objective function $Z$ to compute $Z^{(k)}$.
  5. Select the vertex that gives the maximum (or minimum) $Z^{(k)}$ as the optimal solution.

Simplex Method – Basic Tableau Derivation

The simplex algorithm provides an algebraic technique to locate the optimal vertex without drawing a graph. It works on the standard form introduced earlier. Below is a step‑wise derivation of the initial simplex tableau and the pivot operation.

1. Constructing the Initial Tableau

  1. Write the objective function in the form $Z - c_1x_1 - c_2x_2 - \dots - c_nx_n = 0$.
  2. Arrange all constraint equations (including slack variables) as $$a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 + \dots + a_{in}x_n + s_i = b_i,\qquad i=1,\dots ,m.$$
  3. Place the coefficients of the decision variables, slack variables, and the RHS $b_i$ into a rectangular matrix. The bottom row holds the negative of the objective coefficients (the “$Z$‑row”).
The generic tableau looks like: $$\begin{array}{c|cccc|c} \text{BV} & x_1 & x_2 & \dots & s_m & \text{RHS}\\\hline s_1 & a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & 1 & b_1\\ s_2 & a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & 1 & b_2\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots\\ s_m & a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \dots & 1 & b_m\\\hline Z & -c_1 & -c_2 & \dots & 0 & 0 \end{array}$$ Here “BV” denotes the current basic variable for each row (initially the slack variables $s_i$).


2. Selecting the Entering Variable (Pivot Column)

Scan the $Z$‑row for the most negative coefficient (for maximisation). Let this column correspond to variable $x_p$. If all coefficients are non‑negative, the current solution is optimal.


3. Determining the Leaving Variable (Pivot Row)

For each row $i$ with $a_{ip}>0$, compute the ratio $\displaystyle \frac{b_i}{a_{ip}}$. The smallest non‑negative ratio identifies the pivot row $r$. This ensures feasibility is retained after the pivot.
