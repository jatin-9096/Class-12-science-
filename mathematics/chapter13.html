1. Conceptual Framework

Mathematics · Probability
Fundamental Definitions and Terminology

Probability theory begins with a precise description of an experiment. An experiment (or trial) is any process that yields one or several outcomes. The set of all possible outcomes is called the sample space, denoted by $S$. Each individual outcome is an element of $S$, written as $\omega\in S$. A event $E$ is any subset of $S$; it may consist of a single outcome (a simple event) or many outcomes (a compound event). The complement of an event $E$, written $E^{\prime}$, contains all outcomes in $S$ that are not in $E$, and satisfies $E\cup E^{\prime}=S$ and $E\cap E^{\prime}=\varnothing$.

Two events $A$ and $B$ are said to be mutually exclusive (or disjoint) if they cannot occur together, i.e., $A\cap B=\varnothing$. They are exhaustive if together they cover the whole sample space, i.e., $A\cup B=S$. An event $A$ is said to be a subset of $B$ (written $A\subseteq B$) when every outcome of $A$ is also an outcome of $B$; in this case $A$ occurring guarantees $B$ occurring.

The notion of equally likely outcomes is central to the classical approach. When every elementary outcome in $S$ has the same chance of occurring, the probability of an event $E$ is defined as the ratio of the number of favourable outcomes to the total number of outcomes: $$P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}.$$ Here $n(E)$ denotes the cardinality (number of elements) of $E$, and $n(S)$ that of $S$. This definition satisfies the three axioms of probability: $0\le P(E)\le1$, $P(S)=1$, and for mutually exclusive events $E_1,E_2,\dots,E_k$, $P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}E_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(E_i)$. These axioms provide the logical foundation for all subsequent results.

Mathematics · Probability
Classical Probability and Its Applications

The classical model is most useful when dealing with finite sample spaces where symmetry or combinatorial reasoning makes all elementary outcomes equally likely. Typical examples include tossing a fair coin, rolling a fair die, or drawing a card from a well‑shuffled deck. For a single fair die, $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, and the probability of obtaining an even number $E=\{2,4,6\}$ is $$P(E)=\frac{n(E)}{n(S)}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12.$$

In problems involving selection without replacement, the counting principle becomes essential. Consider drawing two cards successively from a standard $52$‑card deck without replacement. The probability that both cards are aces is $$P(\text{both aces})=\frac{4}{52}\times\frac{3}{51}=\frac{1}{221}.$$ The first factor $\frac{4}{52}$ is the chance that the first card is an ace; after removing one ace, $3$ aces remain among $51$ cards, giving the second factor.

When order is irrelevant, combinations are employed. The probability of drawing exactly two hearts when three cards are selected without replacement is $$P(\text{2 hearts})=\frac{\binom{13}{2}\binom{39}{1}}{\binom{52}{3}}.$$ Here $\binom{13}{2}$ counts the ways to choose $2$ hearts, $\binom{39}{1}$ the ways to choose the remaining non‑heart, and $\binom{52}{3}$ the total number of $3$‑card selections. Simplifying yields $P(\text{2 hearts})\approx0.0589.$

Mathematics · Probability
Conditional Probability, Independence, and Bayes’ Theorem

Conditional probability quantifies the likelihood of an event $A$ given that another event $B$ has already occurred. It is defined as $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.$$ This definition leads directly to the multiplication rule: $$P(A\cap B)=P(B)\,P(A\mid B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A).$$

Two events $A$ and $B$ are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other, i.e., $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ or equivalently $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. For example, tossing two fair coins are independent events; the probability of getting heads on both is $P(H_1\cap H_2)=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$.

Bayes’ theorem provides a systematic way to reverse conditional probabilities: $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}.$$ When $B$ can occur via several mutually exclusive and exhaustive events $A_1,\dots,A_n$, the denominator expands using the law of total probability: $$P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i).$$ As a concrete illustration, suppose a certain disease affects $1\%$ of a population. A diagnostic test is $99\%$ accurate both for detecting the disease (sensitivity) and for correctly identifying healthy individuals (specificity). If a randomly selected person tests positive, the probability that they actually have the disease is $$P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})=\frac{0.01\times0.99}{0.01\times0.99+0.99\times0.01}=0.5.$$ This counter‑intuitive result underscores the importance of prior probabilities.

Mathematics · Probability
Random Variables, Distributions, and Expectation

A random variable (r.v.) is a function $X:S\to\mathbb{R}$ that assigns a real number to each outcome of the experiment. Random variables are classified as discrete when they take a countable set of values, and continuous when they assume values in an interval. For a discrete r.v., the probability mass function (pmf) $p_X(x)=P(X=x)$ lists the probabilities of each possible value. The pmf must satisfy $\sum_x p_X(x)=1$.

The expected value (mean) of a discrete r.v. $X$ is $$E(X)=\sum_x x\,p_X(x).$$ It represents the long‑run average of repeated trials. The variance measures dispersion: $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\!\big[(X-E(X))^2\big]=\sum_x (x-\mu)^2p_X(x),$$ where $\mu=E(X)$. The standard deviation is $\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$.

One of the most important discrete distributions in the CBSE syllabus is the binomial distribution. If a Bernoulli trial (success with probability $p$, failure with probability $q=1-p$) is repeated $n$ independent times, the number of successes $X$ follows $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$. Its pmf is $$P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^{k}q^{\,n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n.$$ The mean and variance are $E(X)=np$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=npq$. For large $n$ and small $p$, the binomial distribution can be approximated by a Poisson distribution with parameter $\lambda=np$, giving $$P(X=k)\approx\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}}{k!}.$$ This approximation simplifies calculations in many real‑world contexts such as radioactive decay or call arrivals at a call centre.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In CBSE Class‑12 Mathematics, Chapter 13 – Linear Differential Equations forms a cornerstone for modelling real‑world phenomena such as population growth, electrical circuits, and mechanical vibrations. The chapter deals primarily with two categories of linear differential equations: (i) first‑order linear equations and (ii) second‑order linear equations with constant coefficients. Below we present the standard derivations adopted in the CBSE syllabus, each accompanied by a clear step‑by‑step explanation, LaTeX‑formatted mathematics, and remarks on the underlying assumptions.

1. First‑Order Linear Differential Equation

A first‑order linear differential equation is expressed as

$$\frac{dy}{dx}+P(x)\,y=Q(x)$$

where P(x) and Q(x) are continuous functions on an interval I. The solution proceeds by introducing an integrating factor μ(x) defined as

$$\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$$

**Derivation of the Integrating Factor**:

  1. Multiply the original equation by an unknown function μ(x):
    2. $$\mu(x)\frac{dy}{dx}+\mu(x)P(x)y=\mu(x)Q(x)$$
  2. Choose μ(x) such that the left‑hand side becomes the exact derivative of the product μ(x)y. This requires
    3. $$\frac{d}{dx}\big[\mu(x)y\big]=\mu(x)\frac{dy}{dx}+y\frac{d\mu}{dx}$$
  3. Comparing with the multiplied equation, we need
    4. $$y\frac{d\mu}{dx}=\mu(x)P(x)y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{d\mu}{dx}=P(x)\mu(x)$$
  4. This first‑order separable equation for μ integrates to
    5. $$\int\frac{1}{\mu}\,d\mu=\int P(x)\,dx\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ln\mu=\int P(x)\,dx$$
  5. Exponentiating both sides yields the integrating factor
    6. $$\mu(x)=e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$$

Having determined μ(x), the original equation reduces to

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[\mu(x)y\big]=\mu(x)Q(x)$$

Integrating both sides with respect to x gives

$$\mu(x)y=\int \mu(x)Q(x)\,dx+C$$

Finally, solving for y yields the general solution

$$y(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(x)}\Bigg[\int \mu(x)Q(x)\,dx+C\Bigg]$$

**Key Points**:

  • The continuity of P(x) and Q(x) guarantees the existence of an integrating factor.
  • The constant C represents the family of solutions; a particular solution is obtained by applying an initial condition y(x₀)=y₀.

2. Second‑Order Linear Differential Equation with Constant Coefficients

The canonical form of a homogeneous second‑order linear differential equation with constant coefficients is

$$a\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+b\frac{dy}{dx}+c\,y=0$$

where a, b, c are real constants and a≠0. The standard approach is to assume a trial solution of exponential type, y=e^{rx}, and substitute it into the differential equation.

Derivation of the Characteristic Equation:

  1. Assume y=e^{rx}. Then
    2. $$\frac{dy}{dx}=re^{rx},\qquad\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}=r^{2}e^{rx}$$
  2. Substituting these into the differential equation yields
    3. $$a r^{2}e^{rx}+b r e^{rx}+c e^{rx}=0$$
  3. Since e^{rx} is never zero, we can divide through by it, obtaining the algebraic equation
    4. $$a r^{2}+b r+c=0$$
  4. This quadratic in r is called the characteristic equation. Its roots determine the nature of the solution.

Depending on the discriminant Δ=b^{2}-4ac, we have three distinct cases:

Case I – Real and Distinct Roots (Δ>0)

Let the roots be r₁ and r₂ (with r₁≠r₂). The general solution is

$$y(x)=C_{1}e^{r_{1}x}+C_{2}e^{r_{2}x}$$
Case II – Real and Repeated Roots (Δ=0)

When the quadratic has a double root r, the two linearly independent solutions are e^{rx} and xe^{rx}. Hence

$$y(x)=\big(C_{1}+C_{2}x\big)e^{rx}$$
Case III – Complex Conjugate Roots (Δ<0)

If the roots are r=α±iβ, Euler’s formula $e^{iβx}=\cosβx+i\sinβx$ allows us to write the real solution as

$$y(x)=e^{\alpha x}\big(C_{1}\cos\beta x+C_{2}\sin\beta x\big)$$

These three families cover all possible homogeneous second‑order linear equations with constant coefficients.

3. Non‑Homogeneous Second‑Order Linear Equation

The general non‑homogeneous form is

$$a\frac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+b\frac{dy}{dx}+c\,y=F(x)$$

where F(x) is a known continuous function. The principle of superposition states that the complete solution y can be expressed as

$$y(x)=y_{h}(x)+y_{p}(x)$$

Here yₕ is the complementary (homogeneous) solution derived in the previous section, and yₚ is a particular integral that satisfies the full equation. Two standard techniques for finding yₚ are the Method of Undetermined Coefficients and the Method of Variation of Parameters. Both are presented below.

3.1 Method of Undetermined Coefficients

This method works when F(x) is a linear combination of functions whose derivatives are of the same type (polynomials, exponentials, sines, cosines). The steps are:

  1. Identify the form of F(x). For example, if F(x)=P_{n}(x)e^{mx} (where Pₙ is a polynomial of degree n), assume a particular solution of the same form but with undetermined coefficients.
  2. If any term of the assumed yₚ duplicates a term in yₕ, multiply the trial solution by x enough times to make it linearly independent.
  3. Substitute the trial yₚ into the differential equation, equate coefficients of like powers (or like trigonometric functions), and solve for the unknown constants.

Illustrative Example: Solve $y''-3y'+2y=e^{2x}$.

Characteristic equation: $r^{2}-3r+2=0\Rightarrow(r-1)(r-2)=0$, giving $r_{1}=1$, $r_{2}=2$. Hence $y_{h}=C_{1}e^{x}+C_{2}e^{2x}$.

Since $e^{2x}$ already appears in $y_{h}$, we multiply the trial by $x$ and set $y_{p}=Axe^{2x}$. Differentiating, substituting, and solving yields $A=\frac{1}{1}$, so $y_{p}=xe^{2x}$. The complete solution is

$$y(x)=C_{1}e^{x}+C_{2}e^{2x}+xe^{2x}$$
3.2 Method of Variation of Parameters

This method is universal; it works for any continuous F(x). Let $y_{1}(x)$ and $y_{2}(x)$ be two linearly independent solutions of the homogeneous equation. The particular integral is assumed in the form

$$y_{p}=u_{1}(x)y_{1}(x)+u_{2}(x)y_{2}(x)$$

where $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$ are functions to be determined. Imposing the auxiliary condition

$$u_{1}'y_{1}+u_{2}'y_{2}=0$$

simplifies the computation of second derivatives. Substituting $y_{p}$ and its derivatives into the original equation leads to a linear system for $u_{1}'$ and $u_{2}'$:

$$\begin{cases} u_{1}'y_{1}+u_{2}'y_{2}=0\\[4pt] u_{1}'y_{1}'+u_{2}'y_{2}'=\dfrac{F(x)}{a} \end{cases}$$

Solving for $u_{1}'$ and $u_{2}'$ using Cramer's rule gives

$$u_{1}'=-\frac{y_{2}F(x)}{aW},\qquad u_{2}'=\frac{y_{1}F(x)}{aW}$$

where $W=y_{1}y_{2}'-y_{1}'y_{2}$ is the Wronskian of $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$. Integrating $u_{1}'$ and $u_{2}'$ yields $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$, and consequently $y_{p}$.
