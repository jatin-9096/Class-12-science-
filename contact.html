Contact between metals and acids

Contact between a metal and an acid is a classic example of a redox reaction in which the metal is oxidised and the acid is reduced. The general reaction can be written as

$$\text{M} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{M}^{2+} + \text{H}_2$$

where M denotes a metal atom or ion that can lose two electrons, and H⁺ is the proton supplied by the acid. The reaction proceeds only if the metal lies above hydrogen in the electrochemical series, i.e. if the standard electrode potential for the couple M²⁺/M is more negative than that of the H⁺/H₂ couple (0.00 V). This is the fundamental law governing metal–acid reactions: a metal will displace hydrogen from an acid only if it is a stronger reducing agent.

In practice, the reaction is influenced by several conditions. The concentration of the acid must be sufficient to provide a driving force; a dilute acid may not supply enough H⁺ to sustain the reaction. Temperature also plays a crucial role: higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of the reacting species, thereby increasing the rate. The surface area of the metal is another factor; a finely divided metal reacts much faster than a bulk piece because of the larger reactive surface. In some cases, a catalyst or a promoter (e.g., a small amount of a more reactive metal) can initiate the reaction by providing a pathway for electron transfer.

There are notable exceptions to the simple displacement law. Non‑reactive metals such as gold, silver, platinum, and palladium do not react with any acid because their standard potentials are far more positive than that of hydrogen. Some metals that are reactive in the periodic table, like iron, may not react with cold acids but do so with hot acids or concentrated acids because the oxide layer on the metal surface is removed or because the acid is strong enough to break the protective film. Oxidising acids such as nitric acid (HNO₃) or perchloric acid (HClO₄) do not produce hydrogen gas; instead, they oxidise the metal to a higher oxidation state and reduce the acid to water or other reduced species. Concentrated sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) can act as a dehydrating agent and may not liberate H₂ even though it is a strong acid.

From a stoichiometric viewpoint, the reaction obeys the law of conservation of mass: the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. The law of conservation of charge is also satisfied because the electrons lost by the metal are gained by the protons. The reaction follows the law of multiple proportions in that the ratio of the masses of the metal and the acid that combine is fixed for a given metal–acid pair. The rate of reaction is governed by the law of mass action; it is proportional to the product of the concentrations of the reactants raised to their stoichiometric coefficients. Le Chatelier’s principle predicts that adding more acid will shift the equilibrium toward the products, thereby increasing the rate.

In many laboratory settings, the reaction is monitored by measuring the volume of hydrogen gas evolved. Contact between metals and oxides

Contact between a metal and its oxide is a fundamental phenomenon that underlies many industrial processes such as the extraction of metals, the functioning of thermite reactions, and the operation of certain types of batteries. When a metal (M) is brought into intimate contact with its oxide (MO), an electron transfer can occur at the interface, leading to the reduction of the oxide and oxidation of the metal. This process is governed by the relative positions of the metal’s work function, the oxide’s electron affinity, and the thermodynamic stability of the oxide. The driving force is the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for the overall redox reaction. If ΔG is negative, the reaction proceeds spontaneously under the given conditions.

Thermodynamically, the contact reaction can be expressed as:

$$\text{M (s)} + \text{MO (s)} \rightarrow \text{M}_2\text{O (s)}$$

where the metal reduces its own oxide to form a higher oxidation state oxide or a mixed oxide, depending on the stoichiometry. The reaction is highly exothermic for many metals such as aluminium, magnesium, and zinc, which possess a large negative standard enthalpy of formation for their oxides.

Key definitions:

  • Contact Potential (E_c): The potential difference that develops across the metal‑oxide interface due to the difference in Fermi levels of the metal and the oxide.
  • Work Function (Φ): Minimum energy required to remove an electron from the metal to vacuum.
  • Electron Affinity (χ): Energy released when an electron is added to the oxide’s conduction band.
  • Standard Gibbs Free Energy of Formation (ΔG_f°): Energy change when one mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states.

The Nernst equation can be adapted to describe the equilibrium potential for the contact reaction:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln\frac{a_{\text{M}}\,a_{\text{MO}}}{a_{\text{M}_2\text{O}}}$$

where $E^\circ$ is the standard electrode potential for the metal/oxide couple, $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, $n$ the number of electrons transferred, $F$ Faraday’s constant, and $a_i$ the activities of the respective species.

Several laws and principles dictate the feasibility of contact reactions:

  • Ellingham Diagram Principle: By plotting ΔG_f° of various metal oxides against temperature, one can predict which metal can reduce a given oxide. A metal whose oxide line lies below that of another oxide at a particular temperature can thermodynamically reduce the higher line oxide.
  • Hume‑Rothery Rules (applied to solid‑state diffusion): Effective contact requires lattice compatibility and sufficient diffusion rates of metal ions and oxygen ions across the interface.
  • Le Chatelier’s Principle: Increasing temperature generally favors endothermic steps such as oxide decomposition, while removal of one of the products (e.g., gaseous CO₂ in a carbonate reduction) drives the reaction forward.

Conditions necessary for a successful contact reaction include:

  • Clean, oxide‑free metal surface to ensure intimate contact.
  • Elevated temperature to overcome kinetic barriers and enhance diffusion.
  • Presence of a reducing environment (e.g., carbon, hydrogen) when the metal alone cannot provide sufficient thermodynamic drive.
  • Particle size reduction to increase surface area and contact points.

Exceptions and special cases:

  • Passivation: Certain metals like aluminium form a dense, adherent oxide layer that prevents further contact, making direct reduction difficult without mechanical disruption.
  • Mixed Oxides: In systems containing more than one metal oxide, selective reduction may occur based on relative ΔG_f° values, leading to complex phase equilibria.
  • Non‑stoichiometric Oxides: Defect structures can alter the effective electron affinity, sometimes allowing reduction at lower temperatures than predicted by ideal stoichiometry.

Derivation

Consider the generic contact reaction:

$$\text{M (s)} + \text{MO (s)} \rightarrow \text{M}_2\text{O (s)}$$

Step 1: Write the standard Gibbs free energy change for the reaction using formation values:

$$\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{O}) - \big[ \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{MO}) + \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{M})\big]$$

Since the elemental metal has $\Delta G_f^\circ(\text{M}) = 0$, the expression simplifies to:

$$\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{M}_2\text{O}) - \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{MO})$$

Step 2: Relate ΔG to the equilibrium constant $K$:

$$\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = -RT\ln K$$

Step 3: Express $K$ in terms of activities of the solid phases (≈1) and any gaseous products if present. For a purely solid system, $K \approx 1$, so the reaction proceeds only if $\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}} < 0$.

Step 4: Incorporate temperature dependence using the van ’t Hoff equation:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}}}{RT^2}$$

Integrating gives the temperature variation of $K$, allowing prediction of the temperature at which the contact reaction becomes spontaneous.

Step 5: Connect the thermodynamic result to the contact potential $E_c$ using the Nernst relation for the metal/oxide couple:

$$E_c = \frac{-\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}}}{nF}$$

Thus, a negative ΔG corresponds to a positive contact potential, driving electron flow from metal to oxide.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • $\Delta G^\circ_{\text{rxn}}$ = Standard Gibbs free energy change (J mol⁻¹)
  • $\Delta G_f^\circ$ = Standard Gibbs free energy of formation (J mol⁻¹)
  • $R$ = Universal gas constant = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
  • $T$ = Temperature (K)
  • $K$ = Equilibrium constant (dimensionless)
  • $\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}}$ = Standard enthalpy change (J mol⁻¹)
  • $n$ = Number of electrons transferred (mol e⁻)
  • $F$ = Faraday constant = 96 485 C mol⁻¹
  • $E_c$ = Contact potential (V)

Contact between metals and water

Contact between a metal and water is a classic example of a redox reaction where the metal is oxidised and water is reduced. The reaction can be represented as:

Metal (s) + H₂O (l) → Metal(II) ion (aq) + H₂ (g) + 2OH⁻ (aq).

For the reaction to proceed spontaneously, the metal must lie above water in the electrochemical series. This means that the standard electrode potential of the metal, E°(M²⁺/M), must be more negative than the potential for the reduction of water, E°(H₂O/H₂). The standard potential for the reduction of water to hydrogen gas is +0.00 V at 25 °C and 1 atm. Therefore, metals such as Li, Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, and Fe are capable of reacting with water, whereas metals like Cu, Ag, Au, Pt, and Hg are not.

Faraday’s laws of electrolysis provide a quantitative relationship between the amount of substance produced at an electrode and the quantity of electric charge passed. The first law states that the mass of substance liberated or deposited at an electrode is directly proportional to the total electric charge. The second law states that the masses of different substances liberated by the same quantity of electric charge are proportional to their equivalent weights. These laws are essential for calculating the amount of hydrogen gas evolved during the reaction of a metal with water.

The Gibbs free energy change, ΔG, determines the spontaneity of the reaction. For a galvanic cell, ΔG = -nFE, where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred, F is Faraday’s constant (96485 C mol⁻¹), and E is the cell potential. A negative ΔG indicates a spontaneous process. The temperature dependence of ΔG is given by ΔG = ΔH - TΔS, where ΔH is the enthalpy change and ΔS is the entropy change. For most metal–water reactions, ΔH is negative (exothermic) and ΔS is positive, leading to a strongly negative ΔG at room temperature.

Conditions that influence the rate and extent of the reaction include temperature, pressure, concentration of water, and the presence of catalysts or impurities. Higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of the molecules, thereby accelerating the reaction. Steam or hot water can react with metals that are otherwise unreactive at room temperature, such as iron and copper.

Exceptions to the general trend arise from kinetic barriers. For example, magnesium reacts slowly with cold water but vigorously with hot water or steam. Copper does not react with water at any temperature but reacts with steam to form cuprous oxide and hydrogen gas. The formation of a protective oxide or hydroxide layer on the metal surface can also inhibit the reaction, as seen with iron in the presence of oxygen where rust formation slows further reaction with water.

In summary, the contact between metals and water is governed by thermodynamic criteria (electrode potentials and Gibbs free energy) and kinetic factors (temperature, pressure, surface condition). Understanding these principles allows chemists to predict whether a given metal will react with water and to quantify the products formed.

Contact between metals and non‑metals

When a metal is brought into intimate contact with a non‑metal, electrons are transferred from the species having a higher Fermi level (or lower work function) to the one having a lower Fermi level (or higher work function). This transfer continues until the Fermi levels of the two materials become equal. The resulting charge separation creates an electric field and a potential difference known as the contact potential. The phenomenon is governed by the difference in work functions of the two materials and is independent of the chemical reaction between them. The contact potential is a purely electrostatic effect and does not involve any net transfer of atoms or ions across the interface.

In the context of CBSE Class 12 chemistry, the contact between a metal and a non‑metal is often discussed under the topic of “Contact” in the chapter “Contact” of the NCERT textbook. The key points to remember are:

  • Definition of Work Function (φ): The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the Fermi level of a solid to a point just outside the surface (vacuum level). It is measured in joules (J) or electron‑volts (eV).
  • Fermi Level (E_F): The chemical potential of electrons in a solid at absolute zero temperature. It is the highest occupied energy level.
  • Contact Potential (V): The potential difference that develops across the interface when the Fermi levels equalize. It is given by the difference in work functions divided by the elementary charge.
  • Conditions for Contact Potential: The two materials must be in good electrical contact, free of insulating layers, and at the same temperature. The interface should be clean and free from contaminants that could alter the work function.
  • Exceptions: If an oxide or other insulating layer forms at the interface, the contact potential may be suppressed or altered. In cases where the non‑metal is a gas, the concept of work function is not applicable, and the contact potential is negligible. Also, when both materials are metals with very similar work functions, the contact potential is very small.

In practice, the contact potential is measured using a Kelvin probe or a contact potential difference (CPD) method. The measured value is often used to infer the relative work functions of different materials and to study surface properties.

Derivation

Consider a metal (M) and a non‑metal (N) brought into contact. Let φ_M and φ_N be their work functions. The Fermi level of the metal is E_{F,M} and that of the non‑metal is E_{F,N}. Initially, before contact, the Fermi levels are not equal:

$$E_{F,M} \neq E_{F,N}$$

When contact is made, electrons flow from the material with higher E_F to the one with lower E_F until equilibrium is reached:

$$E_{F,M}^{\text{final}} = E_{F,N}^{\text{final}} = E_F^{\text{eq}}$$

The work function is related to the Fermi level by:

$$\phi = E_0 - E_F$$

where E_0 is the vacuum level. At equilibrium, the vacuum levels on both sides are the same, so:

$$\phi_M - \phi_N = (E_0 - E_{F,M}) - (E_0 - E_{F,N}) = E_{F,N} - E_{F,M}$$

Thus, the difference in work functions equals the difference in Fermi levels. The contact potential V is the potential difference that arises due to this charge separation. It is related to the difference in work functions by:

$$V = \frac{\phi_M - \phi_N}{e}$$

where e is the elementary charge (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). This equation shows that the contact potential is directly proportional to the difference in work functions and inversely proportional to the charge of an electron.

In summary, the derivation steps are:

  1. Identify work functions φ_M and φ_N.
  2. Use the relation φ = E_0 – E_F to express the difference in work functions.
  3. Recognize that the equilibrium condition forces the Fermi levels to equalize.
  4. Relate the difference in work functions to the contact potential via the elementary charge.
Formula and SI‑unit summary
  • Work function, φ: J (joule) or eV (electron‑volt)
  • Fermi level, E_F: J (joule) or eV (electron‑volt)
  • Contact potential, V: V (volt)
  • Elementary charge, e: C (coulomb)
  • Contact potential difference: $$V = \frac{\phi_M - \phi_N}{e}$$
Practice Question 1

Two metals, A and B, with work functions φ_A = 4.5 eV and φ_B = 5.2 eV, are brought into contact. Calculate the contact potential difference between them.

Solution Steps:

  1. Identify the work functions: φ_A = 4.5 eV, φ_B = 5.2 eV.
  2. Compute the difference: φ_B – φ_A = 5.2 eV – 4.5 eV = 0.7 eV.
  3. Convert the difference to joules: 0.7 eV × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 1.1214 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. Use the elementary charge e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C to find V: $$V = \frac{1.1214 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} = 0.7\,\text{V}.$$
  5. Thus, the contact potential difference is 0.7 V.

Contact between acids and bases

When an acid and a base come into direct contact, they undergo a chemical reaction commonly known as neutralisation. The fundamental definition of neutralisation is the reaction of an acid, which donates protons ($\mathrm{H^+}$), with a base, which accepts protons, to form water and a salt. The general equation can be written as $$\mathrm{Acid + Base \rightarrow Salt + Water}.$$ In aqueous solution, the process is accompanied by the evolution of heat, making it an exothermic reaction. The magnitude of heat released per mole of water formed is termed the heat of neutralisation, which for strong acids and strong bases is approximately $-57.1\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$ at $25^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The law of conservation of mass governs this contact: the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. Moreover, the law of equivalence, derived from the concept of normality, states that one equivalent of an acid reacts with one equivalent of a base. An equivalent is defined as the amount of substance that furnishes or consumes one mole of $\mathrm{H^+}$ or $\mathrm{OH^-}$ ions. Consequently, the stoichiometry of neutralisation can be predicted using the equivalence principle, irrespective of the molecular formulas of the reacting species. Conditions that affect the extent and rate of neutralisation include concentration, temperature, and the nature of the acid–base pair. Strong acids (e.g., $\mathrm{HCl}$, $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$) and strong bases (e.g., $\mathrm{NaOH}$, $\mathrm{KOH}$) dissociate completely in water, leading to rapid and complete neutralisation. In contrast, weak acids (e.g., $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$) and weak bases (e.g., $\mathrm{NH_3}$) only partially dissociate, resulting in an equilibrium mixture of reactants and products. The equilibrium constant $K_\mathrm{eq}$ for the neutralisation of a weak acid with a strong base is given by $K_\mathrm{eq}=K_\mathrm{a}\times K_\mathrm{w}^{-1}$, where $K_\mathrm{a}$ is the acid dissociation constant and $K_\mathrm{w}$ is the ion‑product of water. Exceptions to the ideal behaviour arise under several circumstances. First, when the acid or base is polyprotic (e.g., $\mathrm{H_2SO_4}$), successive neutralisation steps may have different enthalpy changes. Second, in highly concentrated solutions, activity coefficients deviate significantly from unity, and the simple $-57.1\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$ value no longer holds. Third, certain acid–base pairs undergo side reactions, such as oxidation–reduction, which consume part of the heat evolved. Finally, temperature influences the equilibrium position; at elevated temperatures, the endothermic dissociation of water becomes more pronounced, slightly reducing the net heat released. The pH of the resulting solution after neutralisation is a crucial parameter in many industrial and laboratory processes. For a perfect neutralisation of a strong acid with a strong base, the pH is $7$ at $25^\circ\mathrm{C}$. However, when weak acids or bases are involved, the final pH can be calculated using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: $$\mathrm{pH}= \mathrm{p}K_\mathrm{a} + \log\frac{[\text{Base}]}{[\text{Acid}]}.$$ This relationship underscores the importance of buffer systems, where a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base resists drastic pH changes upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or base.

Derivation

The enthalpy change for neutralisation, $\Delta H_\mathrm{n}$, can be derived using Hess’s law. Consider the neutralisation of a strong acid $\mathrm{HA}$ with a strong base $\mathrm{BOH}$: $$\mathrm{HA_{(aq)} + BOH_{(aq)} \rightarrow BA_{(aq)} + H_2O_{(l)}}.$$ Step 1: Write the formation reactions for each species from their elements in their standard states. Step 2: Sum the enthalpies of formation ($\Delta H_f^\circ$) for reactants and subtract the sum for products: $$\Delta H_\mathrm{n}= \big[\Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{BA}) + \Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{H_2O})\big] - \big[\Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{HA}) + \Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{BOH})\big].$$ Since $\Delta H_f^\circ$ of strong acids and bases in aqueous solution is essentially zero (they are formed from ions in solution), the expression simplifies to: $$\Delta H_\mathrm{n}\approx \Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{H_2O_{(l)}}).$$ The standard enthalpy of formation of liquid water is $-285.8\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$. However, only the formation of one mole of water per mole of $\mathrm{H^+}$ and $\mathrm{OH^-}$ is considered, giving the experimentally observed value of $-57.1\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$ after accounting for the ionisation of water ($\Delta H_\mathrm{ion}= -228.7\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$). Thus, $$\Delta H_\mathrm{n}= \Delta H_f^\circ(\mathrm{H_2O_{(l)}}) - \Delta H_\mathrm{ion} = -285.8\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}} + 228.7\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}} = -57.1\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}.$$ The derivation confirms that the heat of neutralisation is essentially the enthalpy change associated with the formation of water from its constituent ions.

Contact between acids and oxides

In the realm of inorganic chemistry, the interaction between acids and oxides is a fundamental process that exemplifies the principles of acid–base chemistry, coordination chemistry, and thermodynamics. An acid, by definition, is a substance that donates protons (H⁺) or, more generally, accepts electron pairs, whereas an oxide is a compound containing oxygen bonded to another element. When an acid comes into contact with an oxide, the reaction can proceed via several mechanistic pathways depending on the nature of the oxide (metallic, non‑metallic, amphoteric) and the strength of the acid. The general stoichiometry of a neutralization reaction between a monoprotic acid (HA) and a binary oxide (MO) is expressed as:

$$\ce{HA + MO -> M^+ + A^- + H2O}$$

Here, the metal cation (M⁺) and the anion (A⁻) form a salt, while water is produced as a by‑product. This simple equation masks a wealth of subtleties. For instance, if the oxide is amphoteric, it can react with both acids and bases, leading to the formation of complex salts such as aluminate or silicate species. Moreover, the reaction may be reversible, especially when the oxide is a weak base or the acid is weak, and the equilibrium position is governed by the relative strengths of the acid and base, as quantified by their dissociation constants (Ka and Kb). The equilibrium constant for the neutralization reaction can be written as:

$$K_{\text{eq}}=\frac{[M^+][A^-][\ce{H2O}]}{[HA][MO]}$$

Since water is a pure liquid, its activity is taken as unity, simplifying the expression to:

$$K_{\text{eq}}=\frac{[M^+][A^-]}{[HA][MO]}$$

When the acid is strong (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄) and the oxide is a weak base (e.g., ZnO, Al₂O₃), the reaction proceeds almost to completion, yielding a high K_eq. Conversely, for weak acids (e.g., acetic acid) reacting with strong oxides (e.g., CaO), the equilibrium lies far to the left, and the reaction may not be practically observable. The thermodynamic driving force is often expressed in terms of the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°), which is related to the equilibrium constant by:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K_{\text{eq}}$$

Thus, a negative ΔG° indicates a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions. The enthalpy change (ΔH°) can be estimated from the standard enthalpies of formation (ΔH_f°) of the reactants and products:

$$\Delta H^\circ = \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})$$

In many acid–oxide reactions, the process is exothermic, releasing heat. However, exceptions exist: the reaction of a weak acid with a very strong base oxide can be endothermic, as seen in the neutralization of acetic acid with calcium oxide, where the lattice energy of the resulting salt competes with the hydration energy of the ions.

Conditions affecting the reaction include temperature, pressure, and the presence of catalysts or solvents. Elevated temperatures generally shift the equilibrium toward the endothermic direction, potentially favoring the reverse reaction. In aqueous media, the solubility of the oxide plays a crucial role; insoluble oxides (e.g., Al₂O₃) may require mechanical activation or the presence of a solvent that can dissolve the oxide to proceed. Pressure has a negligible effect on solid–solid or solid–liquid reactions but can influence gas‑phase reactions involving gaseous oxides such as CO₂ or SO₂.

Exceptions to the straightforward acid–oxide neutralization include the formation of mixed oxides or oxyanions. For example, the reaction of H₂SO₄ with Al₂O₃ yields not only Al₂(SO₄)₃ and water but also can produce intermediate species such as Al₂O₃·SO₃ under certain conditions. Similarly, the reaction of HCl with ZnO can produce zinc chloride and water, but if the system is heated, ZnCl₂ can decompose to ZnCl₂·H₂O or even ZnCl₂·2H₂O, depending on the hydration state.

In summary, the contact between acids and oxides is governed by the interplay of acid strength, oxide basicity, thermodynamic parameters, and reaction conditions. Understanding these factors allows chemists to predict reaction outcomes, design synthesis routes, and control product purity in industrial processes such as the production of metal salts, ceramics, and catalysts.

$$\ce{HA + MO -> M^+ + A^- + H2O}$$

Contact between acids and water is a fundamental concept in acid–base chemistry that explains how acids behave when they are dissolved in aqueous medium. An acid is a substance that donates protons (H⁺) or, more generally, accepts electron pairs in the Brønsted–Lowry sense. Water (H₂O) is a polar solvent capable of forming hydrogen bonds and undergoing autoprotolysis: 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. When an acid is introduced into water, the acid molecules interact with the solvent molecules, leading to proton transfer and the establishment of an equilibrium between the acid, its conjugate base, and the hydronium ion. This process is governed by the acid dissociation constant (K_a) of the acid and the ion product of water (K_w). The extent of dissociation depends on the strength of the acid (K_a value), the concentration of the acid, temperature, and the presence of other ionic species that may influence the activity coefficients. Strong acids (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄) dissociate almost completely in water, whereas weak acids (e.g., acetic acid, H₂CO₃) only partially dissociate, establishing an equilibrium that can be described by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. The pH of the solution, defined as –log[H⁺], is a direct measure of the proton activity and is influenced by both the acid strength and the concentration. In aqueous solutions, the concept of contact is crucial because it determines the effective concentration of H⁺ ions that participate in subsequent chemical reactions, such as neutralization, complexation, or redox processes. The contact between acids and water also introduces the idea of ionic strength and activity coefficients, which become significant in concentrated solutions or in the presence of electrolytes. Exceptions to the simple dissociation model arise when the acid is a polyprotic species, when the solution is highly concentrated leading to non‑ideal behavior, or when the acid is a weak base in reverse (e.g., ammonia in water). In such cases, the equilibrium constants must be corrected for activity, and the simple Ka expression may not accurately predict the proton concentration. The laws of thermodynamics, particularly Le Chatelier’s principle, dictate that the equilibrium will shift to counteract changes in concentration or temperature, thereby altering the extent of proton transfer. Understanding these nuances is essential for accurately predicting reaction outcomes in both laboratory and industrial settings.

Derivation

Consider a weak monoprotic acid HA dissolved in water at concentration C₀. The acid dissociation equilibrium is:

$$\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}$$

Let the degree of dissociation be α. Then:

$$[\ce{H3O+}] = \alpha C_0,\quad [\ce{A-}] = \alpha C_0,\quad [\ce{HA}] = C_0(1-\alpha)$$

The acid dissociation constant is:

$$K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} = \frac{(\alpha C_0)^2}{C_0(1-\alpha)} = \frac{\alpha^2 C_0}{1-\alpha}$$

For weak acids, α ≪ 1, so 1–α ≈ 1. Thus:

$$K_a \approx \alpha^2 C_0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \alpha \approx \sqrt{\frac{K_a}{C_0}}$$

Since [H⁺] = αC₀, we get:

$$[\ce{H+}] \approx \sqrt{K_a C_0}$$

Taking negative logarithm gives the pH:

$$\mathrm{pH} = -\log[\ce{H+}] \approx -\frac{1}{2}\log K_a - \frac{1}{2}\log C_0 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{p}K_a - \log C_0\right)$$

This derivation assumes ideal behavior and negligible contribution of water autoprotolysis. For more accurate calculations, the full quadratic equation must be solved:

$$[\ce{H+}] = \frac{-K_a + \sqrt{K_a^2 + 4K_a C_0}}{2}$$

which reduces to the approximate expression above when K_a ≪ C₀.

A base is a substance that accepts protons or donates hydroxide ions when it comes into contact with water. The fundamental reaction that occurs when a base B dissolves in water is the hydrolysis reaction:

$$\ce{B + H2O <=> BH+ + OH-}$$

In this process, the base B either reacts with water to form its conjugate acid BH+ and hydroxide ions, or it may simply dissociate into its constituent ions if it is a salt of a strong base. The extent of this reaction is governed by the base dissociation constant, Kb, which is defined as the equilibrium constant for the hydrolysis reaction:

$$K_b = \frac{[\ce{BH+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{B}]}$$

For strong bases such as NaOH, Kb is effectively infinite, leading to complete dissociation and a hydroxide ion concentration equal to the initial concentration of the base. Weak bases, like NH3, have finite Kb values and only a fraction of the base molecules react with water to produce OH−.

The equilibrium constant for the hydrolysis of a base is related to the acid dissociation constant (Ka) of its conjugate acid by the ion product of water, Kw:

$$K_a \times K_b = K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{at }25^\circ\text{C}$$

Thus, knowing Ka of BH+ allows one to calculate Kb of B, and vice versa. This relationship is a direct consequence of the fact that the hydrolysis of B and the dissociation of BH+ are reverse processes of each other.

Le Chatelier’s principle dictates that the position of the hydrolysis equilibrium shifts in response to changes in concentration, temperature, or ionic strength. Increasing the concentration of B drives the equilibrium to the right, producing more OH−, while adding a strong acid shifts the equilibrium leftwards, reducing the hydroxide concentration. Temperature changes affect Kb in a manner analogous to acid dissociation constants: for many weak bases, Kb increases with temperature, leading to a higher OH− concentration.

In practice, the activity of ions rather than their concentration is the true thermodynamic quantity. For dilute solutions, activities can be approximated by concentrations, but as ionic strength rises, activity coefficients deviate from unity, requiring corrections. This is particularly important for solutions containing high concentrations of salts or for ionic species with high charges.

There are notable exceptions to the simple hydrolysis picture. Amphoteric bases such as Al(OH)3 and Zn(OH)2 can react with both acids and bases, forming complex ions or precipitates. For example, Al(OH)3 dissolves in excess acid to form Al3+ and water, but in excess base it forms aluminate ions, \(\ce{[Al(OH)4]-}\). Insoluble hydroxides, like Ca(OH)2, exhibit limited hydrolysis because the solid phase restricts the availability of B in solution. In such cases, the solubility product, Ksp, must be considered alongside Kb to determine the hydroxide concentration.

The resulting expression for the hydroxide concentration is:

$$[\ce{OH-}] \approx \sqrt{K_b C}$$

Consequently, the pOH is:

$$\text{pOH} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\text{p}K_b - \log C\right)$$

Strong bases like NaOH release significant heat upon dissolution (exothermic), whereas weak bases may absorb heat (endothermic) due to the energy required for hydrolysis. Contact between bases and oxides

Contact between bases and oxides is a fundamental concept in inorganic chemistry that explains the formation of basic oxides and the nature of their reactions with water and acids. A basic oxide, also known as a metal oxide, is formed when a metal reacts with oxygen under suitable conditions, typically at high temperature. These oxides exhibit basic character because they readily react with acids to form salts and water, a process that is essentially the reverse of the neutralisation reaction. The general reaction can be represented as: $M + O_2 \rightarrow MO$, where $M$ denotes a metal. The basicity of an oxide is directly related to the electropositivity of the metal; highly electropositive metals such as alkali and alkaline earth metals form strongly basic oxides, whereas less electropositive metals may form amphoteric or acidic oxides. The law of conservation of mass governs these reactions, ensuring that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. Temperature and pressure are crucial conditions: most metal oxides are formed at elevated temperatures, often above 500 °C, to overcome the activation energy barrier for the oxidation process. Exceptions to the general trend arise with transition metals and post‑transition metals. Moreover, some heavy metals such as lead form oxides (PbO₂) that are distinctly acidic. The presence of lattice defects, hydration, and polymorphic forms can also influence the basic character of an oxide. In aqueous media, basic oxides undergo hydrolysis: $MO + H_2O \rightarrow M(OH)_2$, producing hydroxide ions ($OH^-$) that raise the pH of the solution. This hydrolysis is a key indicator of the basic nature of the oxide. When a basic oxide reacts with an acid, the reaction follows the neutralisation pattern: $MO + 2HCl \rightarrow MCl_2 + H_2O$, where the metal chloride formed is a salt. The strength of the base formed (the hydroxide) correlates with the solubility of the oxide in water; highly soluble oxides such as Na₂O produce strong bases (NaOH), whereas poorly soluble oxides like CaO produce moderately strong bases (Ca(OH)₂). The concept of basic oxides also extends to the formation of basic salts, where an oxide ion ($O^{2-}$) can replace a halide ion in a salt lattice, leading to compounds such as basic carbonates (e.g., NaHCO₃). These basic salts retain the characteristic $OH^-$ producing ability of the parent oxide. In industrial processes, the contact between bases and oxides is exploited in the production of cement, glass, and ceramics, where controlled oxidation of metal oxides yields materials with desired basicity and structural properties. Understanding the thermodynamic parameters—Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G$), enthalpy ($\Delta H$), and entropy ($\Delta S$)—helps predict the feasibility and direction of oxide formation under given conditions. Overall, the study of contact between bases and oxides integrates concepts of redox chemistry, acid–base theory, and solid‑state chemistry, providing a comprehensive framework for predicting reactivity, designing synthesis routes, and interpreting the behavior of metal oxides in both laboratory and industrial settings.

$$\frac{1}{2}O_2 + 2e^- \rightarrow O^{2-}$$

$$\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}O_2 + 2e^- \rightarrow O^{2-}\right) \; \Rightarrow \; \frac{n}{4}O_2 + ne^- \rightarrow nO^{2-}$$

$$\text{M (s)} + \frac{n}{4}O_2 \rightarrow \text{M}^{n+} + nO^{2-}$$

$$\text{M}^{n+} + nO^{2-} \rightarrow \text{MO}_\frac{n}{2}$$

$$\text{M (s)} + \frac{n}{4}O_2 \rightarrow \text{MO}_\frac{n}{2}$$

$$\text{M (s)} + \frac{1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow \text{MO}$$

$$\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$$
A negative $\Delta G^\circ$ indicates spontaneous formation of the basic oxide under the specified temperature $T$.

Solution steps: 1. Convert $\Delta H^\circ$ to joules: $-601.6\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1} = -601600\ \text{J mol}^{-1}$.
2. Use the Gibbs free energy equation: $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$.
3. Substitute values: $\Delta G^\circ = -601600\ \text{J mol}^{-1} - (298\ \text{K})(-70.0\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1})$.
4. Calculate the entropy term: $(298)(-70.0) = -20860\ \text{J mol}^{-1}$.
5. Subtract: $\Delta G^\circ = -601600\ \text{J mol}^{-1} - (-20860\ \text{J mol}^{-1}) = -601600\ \text{J mol}^{-1} + 20860\ \text{J mol}^{-1} = -580740\ \text{J mol}^{-1}$.

Contact between acids and metals

When an acid comes into contact with a metal, a redox reaction takes place in which the metal is oxidised and the hydrogen ion of the acid is reduced to hydrogen gas. The general reaction can be written as M + 2H⁺ → M²⁺ + H₂. The metal ion produced is soluble in the acid solution, giving rise to a salt of the acid. The reaction is governed by the relative reducing power of the metal and the oxidising strength of the acid. Metals such as zinc, magnesium, iron, aluminium and lead react readily with dilute acids like hydrochloric, sulphuric and nitric acids, whereas noble metals (gold, platinum, silver in dilute HCl) are inert. The rate of the reaction depends on several factors: concentration of the acid, temperature, surface area of the metal, presence of a catalyst or a passivating layer, and the nature of the acid (whether it is a strong or weak acid, or whether it contains oxidising species). The reaction is exothermic in most cases, releasing heat and producing hydrogen gas. The heat released can be significant enough to ignite the hydrogen if the reaction is vigorous. In industrial practice, acid–metal reactions are exploited for hydrogen generation, for cleaning metal surfaces, and for the preparation of metal salts. Exceptions to the simple acid–metal reaction include: (i) passivation of aluminium by a thin oxide film that blocks further reaction; (ii) nitric acid acting as an oxidiser rather than a proton donor, leading to the formation of nitrogen oxides instead of H₂; (iii) the reaction of iron with dilute nitric acid producing iron(III) nitrate and NO₂ gas; (iv) the reaction of copper with nitric acid yielding copper(II) nitrate and NO₂; (v) the reaction of silver with nitric acid giving silver nitrate and NO. These exceptions arise because the acid contains oxidising species that can accept electrons directly from the metal, bypassing the proton reduction step. The spontaneity of the reaction can be predicted using standard electrode potentials. For a given metal M, the oxidation half‑reaction is M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻ E°ox = –E°red(M²⁺/M). The reduction half‑reaction for the acid is 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ E°red = 0 V (by definition). The cell potential is E°cell = E°red(cathode) – E°red(anode) = 0 – (–E°red(M²⁺/M)) = E°red(M²⁺/M). If E°cell > 0, the reaction is spontaneous. Thus, metals with a positive standard reduction potential for their ion form will react with acids. The law of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions are also obeyed, ensuring that the mass of products equals the mass of reactants and that the composition of the metal salt is fixed. The reaction is a classic example of a redox process, where the metal is oxidised and the acid is reduced. The rate of hydrogen evolution can be expressed as a function of acid concentration and temperature, often following Arrhenius behaviour.

Derivation

Step 1: Write the oxidation half‑reaction for the metal M.

$$\text{M (s)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{M}^{2+}\;(\text{aq}) \;+\; 2e^-$$

Step 2: Write the reduction half‑reaction for the acid (proton).

$$2\text{H}^+\;(\text{aq}) \;+\; 2e^- \;\longrightarrow\; \text{H}_2\;(\text{g})$$

Step 3: Add the two half‑reactions, canceling the electrons.

$$\text{M (s)} \;+\; 2\text{H}^+\;(\text{aq}) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{M}^{2+}\;(\text{aq}) \;+\; \text{H}_2\;(\text{g})$$

Step 4: Express the reaction in terms of the acid formula.

$$\text{M (s)} \;+\; 2\text{HA (aq)} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{M(HA)}_2\;(\text{aq}) \;+\; \text{H}_2\;(\text{g})$$

Step 5: Relate the cell potential to the standard electrode potentials.

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = 0 - (-E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}) = E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$$

Step 6: Use the Arrhenius equation to describe the temperature dependence of the rate constant.

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/(RT)}$$

Step 7: Relate the rate of hydrogen evolution to the concentration of acid and metal.

$$r = k\,[\text{HA}]^m\,[\text{M}]^n$$

Step 8: Apply the ideal gas law to calculate the volume of hydrogen produced.

$$P\,V = n\,R\,T \;\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\; V = \frac{n\,R\,T}{P}$$

These steps provide a comprehensive derivation of the acid–metal reaction, linking the stoichiometry, thermodynamics, kinetics, and gas evolution.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • General reaction: M + 2H⁺ → M²⁺ + H₂ (no units)
  • Cell potential: E°cell (V) – Volts
  • Rate law: r = k[HA]^m[Metal]^n – mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
  • Arrhenius equation: k = A exp(–E_a/(RT)) – s⁻¹
  • Ideal gas law: P V = n R T – P (Pa), V (m³), n (mol), R (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), T (K)
  • Volume of H₂: V = n R T / P – m³
  • Standard electrode potential: E° (V) – Volts
  • Reaction enthalpy: ΔH = ΣΔH_f(products) – ΣΔH_f(reactants) – J mol⁻¹
  • Gibbs free energy: ΔG = ΔH – TΔS – J mol⁻¹
  • Equilibrium constant: K = [products]/[reactants] – dimensionless

Contact between bases and metals

Contact between a base and a metal is a classical redox process in which the metal is oxidised and water is reduced to hydrogen gas. The general reaction can be written as $$\text{M} + 2\,\text{OH}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{M(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$$ where M denotes a metal atom or ion. The metal is oxidised to its divalent ion (or a soluble complex in the case of amphoteric metals such as Zn, Al, and Sn) and the hydroxide ion is produced as a spectator. The reaction is spontaneous only if the metal lies above hydrogen in the electrochemical series and the hydroxide concentration is sufficiently high to shift the equilibrium towards product formation. In aqueous solution the reduction potential of the hydrogen electrode in a basic medium is $$\text{E}^\circ_{\text{H}_2/\text{OH}^-} = -0.83\ \text{V}\ \text{vs. SHE}$$ Thus, for a metal to react with a base its standard reduction potential must be more positive than –0.83 V. For example, Zn (E° = –0.76 V) reacts readily with NaOH, whereas Cu (E° = +0.34 V) does not, because the oxidation of Cu⁰ to Cu²⁺ is not favourable in the presence of the basic medium. The reaction is also governed by the solubility of the metal hydroxide; insoluble hydroxides (e.g., Fe(OH)₂) may precipitate and passivate the surface, thereby slowing or stopping the reaction. Temperature, concentration of the base, and the presence of catalysts or impurities can significantly influence the rate. High temperatures increase the kinetic energy of the ions, while high base concentration increases the driving force for the reaction by Le Chatelier’s principle. In some cases, such as with iron or nickel, a thin oxide film forms that protects the metal from further attack; this phenomenon is known as passivation. The reaction can be suppressed by adding small amounts of oxidising agents that maintain the metal in its oxidised state, or by using a base that is weak enough not to provide sufficient hydroxide ions for the reaction to proceed.

There are notable exceptions to the general trend. Amphoteric metals such as Zn, Al, and Sn form soluble hydroxide complexes (e.g., $\text{[Zn(OH)}_4]^{2-}$) and therefore react vigorously with bases. In contrast, noble metals like Ag, Au, and Pt do not react with bases because their standard potentials are far more positive than that of the hydrogen electrode in basic solution. Moreover, some metals that would normally react (e.g., Fe) may be protected by a passive oxide layer that prevents contact between the metal and the hydroxide ions. The presence of such a layer can be overcome by mechanical abrasion or by adding a small amount of acid to dissolve the oxide, after which the metal will react with the base.

In industrial practice, the reaction of metals with bases is exploited for hydrogen generation, for the synthesis of metal hydroxides and complexes, and for the removal of metal ions from waste streams. The reaction is also used in laboratory demonstrations of redox chemistry and in the preparation of metal salts via the reaction of the metal hydroxide with an acid. Understanding the thermodynamics and kinetics of this process is essential for controlling the rate of hydrogen evolution and for predicting the behaviour of different metals in alkaline environments.

Derivation

1. Write the oxidation half‑reaction for the metal: $$\text{M} \longrightarrow \text{M}^{2+} + 2e^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}})$$ 2. Write the reduction half‑reaction for water in a basic medium: $$2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^- \quad (E^\circ_{\text{H}_2/\text{OH}^-} = -0.83\ \text{V})$$ 3. Add the two half‑reactions and cancel the electrons: $$\text{M} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{M}^{2+} + \text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^-$$ 4. Rearrange to obtain the conventional form: $$\text{M} + 2\text{OH}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{M(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$$ 5. Determine spontaneity by calculating the cell potential: $$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = (-0.83\ \text{V}) - E^\circ_{\text{M}^{2+}/\text{M}}$$ If $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$, the reaction proceeds spontaneously. 6. For amphoteric metals, the product is a soluble complex: $$\text{M} + 2\text{OH}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow [\text{M(OH)}_4]^{2-} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$$ The formation of the complex shifts the equilibrium further to the right, enhancing the reaction rate.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Overall reaction: $\text{M} + 2\,\text{OH}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{M(OH)}_2 + \text{H}_2$    Variables: M (solid), OH⁻ (mol L⁻¹), H₂O (L), M(OH)₂ (mol), H₂ (mol)    SI units: mol L⁻¹ for concentrations, L for volume, mol for amounts, V for potential
  • Oxidation half‑reaction: $\text{M} \rightarrow \text{M}^{2+} + 2e^-$    Variables: M (solid), M²⁺ (mol L⁻¹), e⁻ (electron)    SI units: mol L⁻¹ for concentrations, V for potential
  • Reduction half‑reaction: $2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^-$    Variables: H₂O (L), e⁻ (electron), H₂ (mol), OH⁻ (mol L⁻¹)    SI units: L, mol, V
  • Standard cell potential: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$    Variables: $E^\circ_{\text{cathode}}$, $E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$ (V)    SI units: V
  • Gibbs free energy change: $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$    Variables: $n$ (mol e⁻), $F$ (C mol⁻¹), $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ (V)    SI units: J (J mol⁻¹)
  • Solubility product for metal hydroxide: $K_{sp} = [\text{M}^{2+}][\text{OH}^-]^2$    Variables: $[\text{M}^{2+}]$, $[\text{OH}^-]$

Contact between acids and bases

In the realm of chemical reactions, the contact between acids and bases is a fundamental process that exemplifies the principles of neutralization, ion exchange, and the conservation of mass. An acid is defined as a substance that donates protons (H⁺) or, more generally, accepts electrons, whereas a base is a substance that accepts protons or donates hydroxide ions (OH⁻). When an acid and a base are brought into contact, the protons from the acid are transferred to the base, resulting in the formation of water and a salt. This process is governed by the law of conservation of mass, which dictates that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products, and by the law of definite proportions, ensuring that the elements combine in fixed ratios.

Contact reactions are typically classified as double displacement or metathesis reactions, where the cation of the acid pairs with the anion of the base to form a salt, while the hydroxide ion from the base combines with the proton from the acid to produce water. The general form of a contact reaction can be written as:

Acid (HA) + Base (BOH) → Salt (AB) + Water (H₂O)

However, not all acid–base interactions result in a simple neutralization. The reaction may be influenced by factors such as concentration, temperature, and the presence of complex ions. For instance, a strong acid reacting with a weak base may not fully neutralize, leaving excess acid in solution. Similarly, a weak acid reacting with a strong base may leave excess base. The extent of neutralization is often quantified by the equivalence point in titration, where the moles of H⁺ equal the moles of OH⁻.

There are notable exceptions to the straightforward neutralization pathway. When a strong acid reacts with a metal, the acid may reduce the metal ion to its elemental form, producing hydrogen gas instead of water. Conversely, a strong base can react with a metal oxide or hydroxide, leading to the formation of a metal hydroxide and water. Additionally, some acids and bases are amphoteric, meaning they can act as both acids and bases depending on the reaction partner. For example, aluminum hydroxide reacts with both acids and bases, forming aluminum salts or soluble hydroxides respectively.

In aqueous solutions, the contact between acids and bases is mediated by the dissociation of the acid and base into ions. The proton transfer is facilitated by the solvent, which stabilizes the ions through solvation. The pH of the resulting solution is a measure of the hydrogen ion concentration, while the pOH measures the hydroxide ion concentration. The relationship between pH and pOH is governed by the ion product of water, Kw = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C, leading to the equation pH + pOH = 14. This relationship is pivotal in determining the acidity or basicity of the solution after contact.

When dealing with polyprotic acids or bases, multiple proton transfer steps may occur, each characterized by its own acid dissociation constant (Ka) or base dissociation constant (Kb). The equilibrium constants for these steps dictate the degree of ionization and, consequently, the extent of neutralization. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), is often employed to calculate the pH of buffer solutions formed by the contact of weak acids and their conjugate bases.

Derivation

Consider a generic acid HA and a generic base BOH in aqueous solution. The acid dissociates as:

$$\ce{HA <=> H+ + A-}$$

The base dissociates as:

$$\ce{BOH <=> B+ + OH-}$$

When these species are mixed, the proton from the acid reacts with the hydroxide ion from the base to form water:

$$\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$$

Simultaneously, the cation B⁺ from the base associates with the anion A⁻ from the acid to form the salt AB:

$$\ce{B+ + A- -> AB}$$

Combining the above steps, the overall contact reaction is:

$$\ce{HA + BOH -> AB + H2O}$$

In terms of stoichiometry, the reaction is balanced when the number of protons transferred equals the number of hydroxide ions present. For polyprotic acids or bases, the reaction may proceed through multiple stages, each represented by a similar equation with appropriate stoichiometric coefficients.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Neutralization reaction: $\ce{HA + BOH -> AB + H2O}$ – no SI unit (chemical equation)
  • pH: $-\log_{10}[H^+]$ – dimensionless
  • pOH: $-\log_{10}[OH^-]$ – dimensionless
  • Ion product of water: $K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{(mol}^2\text{L}^{-2})$ – SI units: mol² L⁻²
  • Acid dissociation constant: $K_a = \frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$ – dimensionless
  • Base dissociation constant: $K_b = \frac{[OH^-][B^+]}{[BOH]}$ – dimensionless
  • Henderson–Hasselbalch equation: $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$ – dimensionless
Practice Problem 1

Calculate the pH of a solution prepared by mixing 25 mL of 0.10 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) with 25 mL of 0.10 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Assume complete neutralization and that the solution volume is 50 mL.

Solution:

  1. Determine moles of HCl: $n_{\ce{HCl}} = 0.025\,\text{L} \times 0.10\,\text{mol L}^{-1} = 0.0025\,\text{mol}$.
  2. Determine moles of NaOH: $n_{\ce{NaOH}} = 0.025\,\text{L} \times 0.10\,\text{mol L}^{-1} = 0.0025\,\text{mol}$.
  3. Since $n_{\ce{HCl}} = n_{\ce{NaOH}}$, the reaction goes to completion with no excess acid or base.
  4. Products: 0.0025 mol of NaCl and 0.0025 mol of water. The final volume is 0.050 L.
  5. Because the solution is neutral, $[H^+] = [OH^-] = 10^{-7}\,\text{mol L}^{-1}$ at 25 °C.
  6. Thus, $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-7}) = 7.00$.
Practice Problem 2

A 0.050 M solution of acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is titrated with 0.050 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Determine the pH of the solution when 20 mL of NaOH has been added to 20 mL of acetic acid. Use $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ for acetic acid.

Solution:

  1. Initial moles of CH₃COOH: $n_{\ce{CH3COOH}} = 0.020\,\text{L} \times 0.050\,\text{mol L}^{-1} = 0.0010\,\text{mol}$.
  2. Added moles of NaOH: $n_{\ce{NaOH}} = 0.020\,\text{L} \times 0.050\,\text{mol L}^{-1} = 0.0010\,\text{mol}$.
  3. Stoichiometry: $\ce{CH3COOH + OH- -> CH3COO- + H2O}$. The moles of CH₃COOH and OH⁻ are equal, so they react completely, leaving no excess acid or base.
  4. After reaction, moles of CH₃COO⁻ formed = 0.0010 mol. Total volume = 0.040 L.
  5. Concentration of CH₃COO⁻: $[A^-] = \frac{0.0010\,\text{mol}}{0.040\,\text{L}} = 0.025\,\text{mol L}^{-1}$.
  6. Since no CH₃COOH remains, the solution is a buffer of CH₃COO⁻ only. The pH is determined by the hydrolysis of the acetate ion: $\ce{CH3COO- + H2O <=> CH3COOH + OH-}$ with $K_b = \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.56 \times 10^{-10}$.
  7. Let $x$ be the concentration of OH⁻ produced: $K_b = \frac{x^2}{[A^-] - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{0.025}$ (since $x \ll 0.025$).
  8. Solving: $x^2 = K_b \times 0.025 = 5.56 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.025 = 1.39 \times 10^{-11}$, so $x = \sqrt{1.39 \times 10^{-11}} = 3.73 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{mol L}^{-1}$.
  9. Thus, $[OH^-] = 3.73 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{mol L}^{-1}$, so $pOH = -\log_{10}(3.73 \times 10^{-6}) = 5.43$.
  10. Finally, $pH = 14 - pOH = 14 - 5.43 = 8.57$.
