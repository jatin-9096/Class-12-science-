Memories of Childhood

High-scoring CBSE Board Short Answer (40–50 words) and Long Value-Based / Extrapolation Questions (120–150 words).

Section A: Short Answer Questions (40–50 Words)

2 Marks each · Focus on crisp points, textual keywords, and direct answers

Q1 · Part I CBSE Board PYQ
Why did Zitkala-Sa feel deeply uncomfortable and alienated on her first morning at the Carlisle Indian School?

On her first morning, Zitkala-Sa was jarred by the loud metallic breakfast bell, the annoying clatter of stiff leather shoes on bare wooden floors, and the murmur of an unfamiliar foreign language. Stripped of her traditional blanket and forced into tight, clinging Western clothes, she felt immodest, homesick, and completely alienated from her Sioux way of life.

Q2 · Part I Frequently Asked
What does Zitkala-Sa mean by "eating by formula"? How did it humiliate her?

"Eating by formula" refers to the rigid, bell-controlled dining routine at the boarding school—pulling out chairs on the first bell, sitting on the second, and picking up cutlery on the third. Unaware of these rules, Zitkala-Sa sat down too early and rose at the wrong moment under the intimidating stare of a stern woman, leaving her humiliated and in tears.

Q3 · Part I CBSE Board PYQ
Why was Zitkala-Sa terrified of having her long hair cut, and how did her reaction differ from Judewin's?

In Sioux culture, long hair symbolized pride and dignity, while short or shingled hair was worn only by mourners, cowards, or captured warriors. While Judewin felt powerless and advised quiet submission, Zitkala-Sa refused to surrender without fighting—hiding under a bed and resisting fiercely until she was physically overpowered.

Q4 · Part I
Explain the significance of Zitkala-Sa comparing herself to "one of many little animals driven by a herder."

After being dragged out, tied to a chair, and forcibly shorn of her braids, Zitkala-Sa felt completely stripped of her individuality and human dignity. The metaphor captures how the colonial boarding school treated indigenous children not as human beings with heritage, but as voiceless creatures to be tamed and controlled.

Q5 · Part II CBSE Board PYQ
Why did Bama take thirty minutes to an hour to cover a ten-minute walk from school to home?

Young Bama was fascinated by the lively sights and novelties along the village bazaar street. She stopped to watch the performing monkey, the snake charmer, the cyclist riding for days, temple Pongal offerings, frying snacks, street plays, puppet shows, and fiery political speeches, making her short walk endlessly entertaining.

Q6 · Part II CBSE Board PYQ
What initially made Bama laugh at the village elder, and why did her laughter vanish immediately?

Bama burst out laughing seeing a respected elder from her street crouch awkwardly and carry a small vadai packet strictly by its string for the landlord. However, her laughter vanished into shock and fury when her brother Annan explained that upper-caste landlords considered a Dalit's direct touch "polluting."

Q7 · Part II
Why did someone ask Annan which street he lived on, and what does this reveal about society?

In rural villages where communities are strictly segregated by caste, each caste occupies a specific street. Asking Annan his street name was a subtle, indirect way of determining his caste without asking openly—exposing how deeply caste profiling permeates everyday social interactions.

Q8 · Part II Frequently Asked
How did Annan's advice reshape Bama's outlook and future?

Annan advised Bama that the only way to shed caste indignities was to study diligently, learn everything possible, and stay ahead of others. Deeply moved, Bama channeled her anger into intense study, stood first in her class, and earned lasting respect and friendships through academic excellence.

Section B: Long & Value-Based Questions (120–150 Words)

5 Marks each · Includes Comparative, Thematic, and CBSE Competency Extrapolation formats

Q9 · Comparative Most Important 5-Mark PYQ
Both Zitkala-Sa and Bama experienced bitter social discrimination in childhood, yet their responses to oppression differed. Compare and contrast their experiences and modes of resistance.

Though separated by continents and cultures, both Zitkala-Sa and Bama endured the painful loss of childhood innocence through systemic prejudice. Zitkala-Sa, a Native American girl at the Carlisle Indian School, faced racial discrimination and forced Western assimilation designed to erase her Sioux heritage. Bama, a Tamil Dalit girl, confronted deep-rooted caste untouchability when she saw a respected village elder forced to carry a snack packet by its string so he would not "pollute" it for an upper-caste landlord.

Their modes of resistance, however, reflected their circumstances. Zitkala-Sa responded with immediate physical defiance—refusing to submit quietly, hiding under a bed, and kicking and scratching until overpowered. Bama, guided by her elder brother Annan, chose long-term intellectual empowerment. She channeled her righteous anger into relentless study, topped her class, and forced society to respect her merit. Ultimately, both women refused to internalize inferiority and later used literature as a powerful weapon to challenge injustice worldwide.

Q10 · Value-Based CBSE Board PYQ
"Education is the most powerful weapon to dismantle social hierarchies." Discuss this statement with reference to Annan's advice and Bama's journey in "We Too Are Human Beings."

In "We Too Are Human Beings," Bama exposes how caste-based untouchability strips marginalized communities of basic dignity, reducing hardworking elders to crouching figures afraid of "polluting" a food parcel. When young Bama learns this bitter truth, her natural reaction is fury and disgust—she wants to march back and touch the vadai herself.

At this crucial moment, her elder brother Annan provides transformative wisdom. Having personally experienced subtle caste profiling at university, he understands that blind rage alone cannot break centuries-old prejudice. He tells Bama that because society denies them honor by birth, they must earn undeniable respect through knowledge and excellence. Bama takes his words to heart, studies with an intense frenzy, and stands first in her class. Her academic brilliance compels peers and elders alike to approach her with respect, proving that education equips the oppressed with both self-worth and the power to dismantle discriminatory barriers.

Q11 · Thematic
How does "The Cutting of My Long Hair" illustrate that forced assimilation is a form of violence against human identity?

Zitkala-Sa's account at the Carlisle Indian School demonstrates that oppression does not require weapons alone; systematically erasing a child's culture is a profound form of psychological violence. From the moment she arrives, every aspect of her Sioux identity is targeted. Her soft moccasins are replaced by noisy, stiff shoes; her traditional shawl is removed; her native language is silenced; and even eating is reduced to a cold, bell-driven formula.

The climax of this cultural erasure is the forced cutting of her long hair. In Sioux tradition, shingled hair was strictly a mark of mourners, cowards, or defeated captives. By dragging Zitkala-Sa from beneath the bed, tying her to a chair, and shearing her braids against her will, the authorities deliberately broke her spirit to enforce Western conformity. Her haunting description of feeling like a "wooden puppet" and a herded animal underscores how forced assimilation dehumanizes individuals by severing them from their roots.

Q12 · Competency / Speech CBSE New Pattern
Imagine you have been invited to deliver a morning assembly speech on "Standing Up Against Prejudice: Lessons from Zitkala-Sa and Bama." Draft the speech in 120–150 words.

Good morning respected Principal, teachers, and dear friends.

Today, I want to share a powerful lesson from two remarkable women—Zitkala-Sa and Bama—whose childhood memories remind us that silence only strengthens injustice. Whether it is racial prejudice stripping a Native American girl of her cultural pride, or caste untouchability humiliating a respected village elder, discrimination always tries to convince the marginalized that they are less than human.

Yet both Zitkala-Sa and Bama refused to surrender their dignity. Zitkala-Sa taught us the courage to say, "I will struggle first," proving that our heritage deserves to be defended. Bama showed us how to turn righteous anger into academic excellence, using education to command respect and equality. As students and future citizens, let us pledge never to judge anyone by race, caste, or background—and always stand up for human dignity. Thank you.

Q13 · Diary Entry CBSE New Pattern
Imagine you are Bama on the evening Annan explained the truth about the village elder and the vadai packet. Write a diary entry expressing your shock, anger, and new resolve.

Tuesday, 14 October
9:30 PM

Dear Diary,
I cannot stop thinking about what happened today. When I saw the elder from our street crouching and holding that vadai packet by its string for the landlord, I laughed so hard I nearly doubled over. How foolish and innocent I was! Annan's words shattered my laughter in a single moment. To think that a respected older man from our community is treated as if his mere touch could "pollute" food wrapped in paper and banana leaf fills me with disgust and fury.

Our people toil in their fields all day, yet they treat us as lesser beings simply because of our birth. I felt like running back and touching those snacks myself. But Annan is right—anger alone will not change this cruel system. From tonight, I will pour every ounce of my energy into my books. I will study harder than anyone else, stand first in my class, and prove that we too are human beings worthy of equal respect.

— Bama
