Part I: The Cutting of My Long Hair

Characters from Zitkala-Sa's narrative at Carlisle Indian School

Zitkala-Sa (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin)

Protagonist · Part I
Culturally Proud Fiercely Brave Sensitive & Observant Rebellious Spirit

Zitkala-Sa is a young Native American (Sioux) girl uprooted from her mother and placed in a strict missionary boarding school. She represents unyielding cultural pride and raw courage against forced assimilation.

  • Deeply Rooted in Tradition: She cherishes her Sioux heritage. In her community, short or shingled hair is worn only by mourners, cowards, or unskilled warriors captured by the enemy—making the forced haircut an attack on her very identity.
  • Sensitive to Humiliation: She feels immodest in tight Western dresses without her shawl and weeps during the mechanical "eating by formula" breakfast routine.
  • Active Defiance: Rejecting Judewin's advice to submit, she declares, "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!" She hides under a bed in a dark room and kicks and scratches wildly when caught.
  • Enduring Voice: Although physically tied to a chair and shingled, her trauma later fuels her lifelong activism and writing for Native American dignity.
CBSE Board Tip: Always quote "I will struggle first!" and contrast her active physical resistance with Judewin's passive resignation.

Judewin

Friend & Foil · Part I
Alert & Caring Pragmatic Submissive to Authority

Judewin is a fellow Native American girl at the school who knows a few words of English. She serves as a character foil to highlight Zitkala-Sa's extraordinary bravery.

  • Helpful Friend: She overhears the paleface woman's plan to cut their long hair and immediately warns Zitkala-Sa.
  • Resigned Mindset: Believing resistance is pointless against powerful authorities, she says, "We have to submit, because they are strong."

The Paleface Woman (School Authorities)

Antagonist · Part I
Authoritarian Culturally Insensitive Dehumanizing

Representing the colonial boarding-school system, the paleface woman is cold, strict, and indifferent to the emotional trauma of indigenous children. She intimidates Zitkala-Sa with sharp stares during breakfast and enforces cultural erasure by forcibly shingling the girls, treating them like "little animals driven by a herder."

Part II: We Too Are Human Beings

Characters from Bama's narrative in rural Tamil Nadu

Bama (Faustina Mary Fatima Rani)

Protagonist · Part II
Keenly Observant Spirited & Innocent Self-Respecting Determined Achiever

Bama is a lively third-grade Tamil Dalit girl whose carefree childhood is suddenly interrupted by her first conscious encounter with caste-based untouchability.

  • Vibrant Observer: A 10-minute walk home from school takes her 30 minutes to an hour because she absorbs every lively sight in the bazaar—street performers, temple bells, snack stalls, and political speeches.
  • Sharp Moral Awakening: Initially laughing at the village elder holding a vadai packet by its string, she immediately shifts to sadness, disgust, and righteous fury once Annan explains untouchability.
  • Refuses Inferiority: She questions why upper-caste landlords treat Dalits as "polluted" while relying on their hard labor, feeling an urge to touch the vadai herself.
  • Channels Anger into Excellence: Following Annan's advice, she studies with a "frenzy," stands first in her class, and wins respect through education.
CBSE Board Tip: Highlight Bama's shift from innocent laughter → righteous anger → academic triumph, using education as her weapon against casteism.

Annan (Bama's Elder Brother)

Mentor & Guide · Part II
Wise & Mature Socially Aware Progressive Mentor

A university student who has personally experienced subtle caste profiling (being asked which street he lived on), Annan acts as Bama's guiding light.

  • Honest Realist: Instead of shielding Bama with lies, he calmly explains the harsh reality of untouchability behind the elder's behavior.
  • Constructive Visionary: He channels Bama's anger into a practical lifelong strategy—telling her that education and academic excellence are the surest ways to throw away indignities and earn honor.

The Village Elder & The Landlord

Social Contrasts · Part II
Generational Humiliation Feudal Arrogance
  • The Village Elder: A respected older man from Bama's street forced by entrenched caste rules to crouch, bow low, and hold a vadai packet by its string so he won't "pollute" it. He symbolizes the silent, generational indignity endured by Dalits.
  • The Upper-Caste Landlord: Sitting comfortably on a stone ledge watching Dalit workers drive muzzled cattle, he casually eats the vadai without empathy—symbolizing feudal privilege and economic exploitation.
