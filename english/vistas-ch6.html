Memories of Childhood

Introduction & Author Background

Memories of Childhood (NCERT Class 12 Vistas, Chapter 6) presents two autobiographical episodes written by two women from marginalized communities across different continents—united by their childhood encounters with social prejudice, humiliation, and dehumanization.

Both narrators recall the defining moment when their childhood innocence was shattered: racial and cultural oppression for Zitkala-Sa, and caste-based untouchability for Bama. Neither accepts injustice passively; both plant the seeds of dignity and rebellion early in life.

Part I: Zitkala-Sa (1876–1938)

Pen name of Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, a Yankton Dakota Sioux (Native American) writer, violinist, and rights activist. Taken from her reservation to the missionary Carlisle Indian School, her account comes from American Indian Stories and exposes forced cultural assimilation.

Part II: Bama (Born 1958)

Pen name of Faustina Mary Fatima Rani, a renowned Tamil Dalit feminist writer and educator. This excerpt is from her landmark 1992 autobiography Karukku (translated by Lakshmi Holmström), capturing caste oppression in rural Tamil Nadu.

Detailed Scene-by-Scene Summary

Part I: The Cutting of My Long Hair

By Zitkala-Sa · Racial Prejudice & Forced Cultural Assimilation
  • First Day in the "Land of Apples": Zitkala-Sa's first day at the Carlisle Indian School is bitterly cold, with snow blanketing the ground and bare trees against the sky. A loud metallic breakfast bell jars her sensitive ears. The constant clatter of stiff shoes on bare wooden floors and the murmur of an unfamiliar tongue overwhelm her spirit.
  • Unfamiliar Attire & Loss of Modesty: Native American girls and boys march into the dining room from opposite doors. Stripped of her traditional blanket/shawl, Zitkala-Sa feels deeply uncomfortable and immodest in her tight, clinging school dress and stiff shoes. She notices other Indian girls already forced into shingled (short) hair.
  • "Eating by Formula" (The Three-Bell Humiliation): Unfamiliar with the rigid boarding-school rules, she gets humiliated at breakfast:
    • First Bell: Everyone pulls out their chair; Zitkala-Sa sits down immediately while everyone else stays standing.
    • Second Bell: Everyone sits down; Zitkala-Sa starts rising just as they sit, feeling a "paleface woman" staring sharply at her.
    • Third Bell: Everyone picks up their knife and fork to eat, while Zitkala-Sa bursts into tears—describing the soulless routine as "eating by formula."
  • Judewin's Warning & Cultural Significance of Hair: Her friend Judewin, who knows a few words of English, warns her that the paleface woman plans to cut their long, heavy hair. In Sioux culture, short or shingled hair is worn only by mourners, cowards, or unskilled warriors captured by the enemy.
  • Defiance Over Submission: When Judewin says, "We have to submit, because they are strong," Zitkala-Sa fiercely replies: "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!"
  • Hiding, Capture & Forced Haircut: Slipping away unnoticed, Zitkala-Sa creeps upstairs into a dimly lit room with three white beds and dark green curtains, crawling under the farthest bed. Soon, searchers enter, lift the sheet, and drag her out despite her kicking and scratching wildly. Carried downstairs and tied fast to a chair, she feels the cold metal blades of scissors gnaw off her thick braids.
  • Loss of Spirit: The moment her braids fall, she loses her spirit. Tossed about like a "wooden puppet" and stripped of her identity, she moans for her mother, feeling reduced to "one of many little animals driven by a herder."
Key Exam Quote: "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!" — Highlights Zitkala-Sa's refusal to surrender her cultural identity without resistance.

Part II: We Too Are Human Beings

By Bama · Caste Discrimination, Untouchability & Empowerment Through Education
  • Innocent Childhood & The Long Walk Home: In the third class, young Bama has not yet heard people speak openly about untouchability, though she has already felt its sting. A walk home from school that takes only 10 minutes stretches into 30 minutes to an hour because she loves watching every lively sight along the bazaar street.
  • Vibrant Village Street Sights: She watches the performing monkey, the snake charmer, the cyclist who hasn't got off his bike for three days with rupee notes pinned to his shirt, spinning wheels, the Maariyaata temple bell, Pongal offerings cooking outside, dried fish and sweet stalls, street plays, puppet shows, "no magic, no miracle" stunts, political harangues, coffee clubs cooling coffee from a height, and seasonal fruits.
  • The Threshing Floor Scene: Turning into her street one day, Bama sees a threshing floor set up near the opposite corner. An upper-caste landlord sits comfortably on sacking spread over a stone ledge, watching workers from Bama's community drive muzzled cattle round and round to tread out grain.
  • The Elder & The String-Held Packet: Suddenly, Bama spots a respected elder from her street approaching the landlord from the bazaar. He carries a small packet of vadai (or green banana bhajji) by its string, carefully avoiding touching the parcel itself. Crouching awkwardly, he bows low and extends the packet by the string while cupping that hand with the other. The landlord opens it and begins eating.
  • From Laughter to Shock — Annan's Truth: Thinking the elder's posture is hilarious, Bama bursts out laughing and runs home to enact it for her elder brother, Annan. Annan does not laugh. He explains the bitter reality: upper-caste landlords believe that if a Dalit touches the food packet, it becomes "polluted"—which is why the elder had to carry it by the string.
  • Bama's Awakening & Fury: Bama's laughter instantly vanishes, replaced by disgust, sadness, and anger. She wonders how a parcel already wrapped in a banana leaf and paper could be "polluted" by their touch, and feels a fierce urge to go touch the vadai herself. She realizes her people do all the hard labor yet are denied basic humanity simply because landlords hold wealth and caste power.
  • Annan's Advice — Education as a Weapon: Annan (a university student who was himself once asked which street he lived on to figure out his caste) tells Bama the only path to dignity: study with care, learn all you can, and stay ahead in your lessons—once you excel, people will come to you on their own accord.
  • Triumph Through Determination: Taking Annan's words to heart, Bama studies with an intense "frenzy," stands first in her class, and wins lasting respect and many friends.
Key Exam Quote: "If we study and make progress, we can throw away these indignities." — Annan's practical blueprint for overcoming caste oppression.

Comparative Analysis: Zitkala-Sa vs. Bama (Board Favorite)

CBSE frequently asks a 5-mark comparative question linking both stories. Use these exact parallels and contrasts:

Aspect Zitkala-Sa (Part I) Bama (Part II)
Setting & Identity Native American (Sioux) girl at Carlisle Indian School, USA. Tamil Dalit girl in a rural South Indian village.
Type of Oppression Racial & cultural discrimination — forced Western assimilation. Caste-based untouchability — social segregation & feudal exploitation.
Turning Point Dragged from under a bed, tied to a chair, and forcibly shingled. Seeing a village elder carry a vadai packet by its string for a landlord.
Form of Resistance Immediate physical defiance: Hides, kicks, scratches, refuses silent surrender. Long-term intellectual empowerment: Channels anger into studies and tops her class.
Shared Legacy Both refuse to internalize inferiority and later use literature & autobiography to expose social injustice worldwide.

Central Theme & Message

  • Universal Face of Discrimination: Whether racism in America or casteism in India, systemic prejudice operates the same way—stripping marginalized communities of identity, dignity, and equality.
  • Premature Loss of Childhood Innocence: Both accounts capture the painful moment when social cruelty invades a child's carefree world.
  • Courage & Self-Respect: Zitkala-Sa proves one must never surrender cultural dignity without a fight; Bama proves anger can be transformed into constructive self-advancement.
  • Education as the Ultimate Equalizer: Annan's guidance and Bama's triumph establish education as the strongest weapon against entrenched social hierarchies.

Literary Devices & Narrative Style

  • First-Person Autobiographical Voice: Creates raw emotional authenticity and direct reader empathy.
  • Symbolism: Long hair = Native American pride & freedom; String-held packet = invisible barrier of untouchability; Eating by formula = soulless institutional control.
  • Simile & Metaphor: Zitkala-Sa likens herself to "a wooden puppet" and "one of many little animals driven by a herder" to convey total dehumanization.
  • Sensory Imagery: Harsh auditory/tactile imagery in Part I ("metallic clash of bell", "cold blades of the scissors") contrasts with colorful, vibrant bazaar imagery in Part II.
  • Situational Irony & Tone Shift: Bama's playful laughter abruptly shifts to bitter irony and righteous fury once Annan reveals the truth behind the elder's bow.
