On the Face of It

A poignant three-scene play by Susan Hill exploring physical impairment, social alienation, and the transformative power of empathy.

Introduction, Author & Title Significance

On the Face of It (NCERT Class 12 Vistas, Chapter 5) is a deeply moving one-act play divided into three scenes, written by acclaimed British author Susan Hill (born 1942). The play centers on an unexpected friendship between two physically impaired individuals: Derry (Derek), a bitter, withdrawn fourteen-year-old boy with one side of his face scarred by acid, and Mr. Lamb, a warm, philosophical old man with a tin leg.

Title Significance: The idiom "on the face of it" means judging something or someone purely by superficial appearances. The title operates on two levels: literally, it refers to Derry's acid-burned face; metaphorically, it critiques a shallow society that judges people by their outward scars rather than their inner worth—and also reflects how Derry initially misjudges Mr. Lamb's lonely life.

Detailed Scene-by-Scene Summary

Scene 1: Mr. Lamb's Garden

The Encounter, Clash of Perspectives & Derry's Emotional Awakening
  • Derry's Trespass: Fourteen-year-old Derry climbs over the garden wall instead of using the open gate because he believes the garden is empty and wants a quiet place away from people. He is startled when Mr. Lamb calmly calls out, warning him to mind the fallen crab apples in the long grass.
  • Defensive Hostility: Embarrassed and suspicious, Derry prepares to leave, insisting he didn't come to steal apples. Mr. Lamb reassures him warmly: his gate is always open, everyone is welcome, and Derry has no reason to be afraid.
  • The Acid-Burned Face: Derry bluntly assumes Mr. Lamb is afraid of his face—one side of which was eaten away by acid. To Derry's surprise, Mr. Lamb shows neither shock nor pity. He treats Derry like any ordinary boy, noting that he doesn't mind appearances.
  • Weeds vs. Flowers (Perspective): When Derry notices overgrown weeds in the garden, Mr. Lamb explains his philosophy of life: weeds are simply green growing plants just like flowers, fruit, and herbs—the difference is only in how people label them. He asks Derry, "Why is one green, growing plant called a weed and another 'flower'?"
  • Mr. Lamb's Tin Leg & Resilience: Mr. Lamb reveals that his real leg was blown off in the war and replaced with a tin leg. Kids call him "Lamey-Lamb," yet it does not trouble him because he refuses to let cruel labels define his happiness.
  • Derry's Deep Bitterness: Derry opens up about the constant pain of people staring, whispering, or offering fake sympathy. He recalls overhearing a woman at a bus stop call his face "a terrible thing" that only a mother could love—and bitterly notes that even his mother only kisses the unburned side of his face.
  • The Fable of "Beauty and the Beast" & The Locked-Up Man: Derry dismisses fairy tales like Beauty and the Beast, knowing his face will never magically change. Mr. Lamb counters with a humorous yet sharp story about a man who feared everything—getting run over, catching germs, lightning striking him—so he locked himself in a room, only for a picture on the wall to fall on his head and kill him. The lesson: isolating oneself out of fear destroys life itself.
  • Bees Buzzing vs. Singing: Mr. Lamb points out that while some people hear bees "buzzing," he hears them "singing" and humming. Similarly, he keeps no curtains on his windows because he hates shutting things out—he loves light, darkness, wind, books, and listening to the world.
  • A Spark of Hope: Slowly, Mr. Lamb's warmth melts Derry's cynicism. Mr. Lamb reminds him that he has two arms, two legs, eyes, ears, a tongue, and a brain—he can do better than anyone else if he chooses to. Derry promises to go home (three miles away), tell his mother where he is, and come back to help Mr. Lamb pick crab apples.
  • Mr. Lamb's Quiet Loneliness: As Derry runs off, Mr. Lamb speaks softly to himself and his bees, admitting that everyone says they will come back, "but they never do." This brief soliloquy reveals that beneath his cheerful exterior, Mr. Lamb too suffers from deep loneliness.
Key Exam Quote: "Wait, watch, listen. That's all." & "So you're not lost, are you? Not altogether?" — Mr. Lamb's gentle guidance pulls Derry out of his shell.

Scene 2: Derry's House

Clash with His Overprotective Mother & Assertion of Independence
  • Mother's Fear & Prejudice: When Derry tells his mother about Mr. Lamb, she immediately forbids him from going back. Having lived there only three months, she relies on neighborhood gossip, calling Mr. Lamb a crazy old man living alone in a huge house.
  • Derry's Transformation: For the first time in his life, Derry stands up for himself. He argues that Mr. Lamb talks about things that truly matter—things nobody else has ever discussed with him.
  • Choosing Life Over Safety: Derry tells his mother that his looks no longer matter; what matters is what he thinks, feels, sees, and hears. He declares that if he does not go back now, he will never go anywhere in the world again. Defying her orders, he runs out of the house toward Mr. Lamb's garden.

Scene 3: Mr. Lamb's Garden

The Tragic Fall & Emotional Climax
  • Mr. Lamb's Fall: Back in his garden, Mr. Lamb steadies a ladder against a crab apple tree to pull down the ripe apples himself, talking quietly to himself. Suddenly, a branch creaks, the ladder slips, and Mr. Lamb crashes heavily to the ground, lying completely still.
  • Derry's Return & Grief: Panting and excited, Derry bursts through the garden gate shouting, "You see! I came back!"—proving Mr. Lamb's quiet fear wrong. He stops dead in his tracks when he sees Mr. Lamb lying lifeless in the grass.
  • Bittersweet Ending: Kneeling beside him, Derry pleads, "I came back. Lamey-Lamb. I did come back," and begins to weep. Though Mr. Lamb is gone, his philosophy has already succeeded: Derry has conquered his fear of the world and reclaimed his humanity.

Central Themes & Message

  • Pain of Alienation & Loneliness: Physical impairment itself is often less painful than the emotional isolation caused by society's stares, whispers, and misplaced pity. Both Derry and Mr. Lamb are lonely, though they handle it differently.
  • Empathy vs. Sympathy: People with disabilities do not want hollow sympathy or overprotection (like Derry's mother's fear); they want genuine acceptance, respect, and normal human companionship (like Mr. Lamb's openness).
  • Appearance vs. Reality: True worth lies inside a person, not on their skin. Judging others "on the face of it" blinds society to human beauty and potential.
  • Power of Positive Perspective: Mr. Lamb shows that attitude shapes reality—whether you see weeds or growing life, buzzing pests or singing bees, determines whether you live in bitterness or joy.

Symbolism & Literary Devices

The Open Gate & No Curtains

Symbolize Mr. Lamb's open heart, willingness to welcome everyone without judgment, and refusal to shut out the world despite his disability.

Weeds vs. Flowers

Symbolize society's arbitrary labels. Just as weeds and flowers are both living plants, scarred and unscarred people share equal human worth.

Derry Climbing the Wall

Symbolizes Derry's psychological barrier—he avoids the open gate because he expects rejection and prefers hiding from society.

Dramatic Irony of the Ending

Mr. Lamb dies believing Derry will never return, at the exact moment Derry proves his loyalty by running back to him.

Most Important Points for CBSE Board Exams

  • Why Derry climbed the wall: Not to steal apples, but because he thought the garden was empty and wanted to escape people's stares.
  • Why Mr. Lamb's actual loneliness matters: Even though he acts cheerful, his quiet remark ("they never do come back") and his climbing the ladder alone prove he deeply craved companionship.
  • Contrast between Mother and Mr. Lamb: Derry's mother's overprotective love traps him in fear and self-pity, whereas Mr. Lamb's tough, honest acceptance sets him free.
  • Derry's turning point: Realizing that hatred burns worse than acid—"Acid only burned my face... [hatred] can burn you away inside."
  • Why the tragic ending is still hopeful: Mr. Lamb's death is heartbreaking, but Derry's return marks the permanent death of his old, bitter, fearful self.
