Formal Letter

A formal letter is a written communication that follows a strict set of conventions and is used for official or business purposes. It is distinguished from informal letters by its objective tone, precise structure, and adherence to etiquette. The primary purpose of a formal letter is to convey information, request action, or express a formal opinion in a clear, respectful, and professional manner. The audience is usually a person or organization with whom the writer has a formal relationship, such as a government office, a corporate entity, or an academic institution. The content must be concise, factual, and devoid of colloquial language or emotive expressions that could undermine the seriousness of the message.

Key elements of a formal letter include the sender’s address, the date, the recipient’s address, a salutation, the body, a complimentary close, and the sender’s signature. Each element serves a specific function: the sender’s address establishes the origin, the date provides a temporal reference, the recipient’s address ensures correct delivery, the salutation sets the tone, the body delivers the message, the complimentary close signals the end, and the signature authenticates the document. The letter must be typed or printed on plain, white paper, with a clear, legible font such as Times New Roman or Arial in 12-point size. Margins are typically set to 2.5 cm on all sides, and the text is left-aligned. Paragraphs are separated by a single line space, and the first line of each paragraph is not indented.

Formal letters must employ a polite, respectful tone. The writer should use the second person “you” sparingly, preferring the third person or passive constructions to maintain objectivity. The language should be precise, avoiding idioms, contractions, or slang. Punctuation is critical: commas separate clauses, semicolons link closely related independent clauses, and periods conclude statements. The salutation usually begins with “Dear Mr./Ms./Dr.” followed by the last name, and the complimentary close often reads “Yours faithfully” (if the recipient’s name is unknown) or “Yours sincerely” (if the name is known). The signature line should be left-justified, followed by the writer’s printed name and designation.

Exceptions to these conventions arise in specific contexts. For instance, when addressing a large organization or a public office, the salutation may use a generic title such as “To Whom It May Concern.” In cases where the writer is a senior official, the letter may begin with a formal heading indicating the office or department. Additionally, certain cultures or industries may permit the use of a more formal closing such as “Respectfully” or “With best regards.” However, the core structure—sender’s address, date, recipient’s address, salutation, body, complimentary close, signature—remains constant across all formal letters.

Derivation

Let us derive a simple quantitative model for the length of a formal letter, expressed in the number of lines (ℓ). A formal letter can be decomposed into five main components: header (H), salutation (S), body (B), closing (C), and signature (G). Each component contributes a certain number of lines:

$$\ell = H + S + B + C + G$$

Where:

  • H = number of lines in the header (sender’s address and date).
  • S = number of lines in the salutation.
  • B = number of lines in the body.
  • C = number of lines in the complimentary close.
  • G = number of lines in the signature block.

For a typical formal letter, the header occupies 2 lines, the salutation 1 line, the body 5–10 lines depending on content, the closing 1 line, and the signature 3 lines. Thus, a standard letter might have:

$$\ell = 2 + 1 + 7 + 1 + 3 = 14 \text{ lines}$$

In practice, the body (B) is the most variable component. If the writer needs to include multiple points or attachments, B may increase proportionally. The model allows writers to estimate the overall length before drafting, ensuring compliance with institutional guidelines that may limit letter length.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • ℓ – Total number of lines in the letter (unit: lines).
  • H – Header lines (unit: lines).
  • S – Salutation lines (unit: lines).
  • B – Body lines (unit: lines).
  • C – Closing lines (unit: lines).
  • G – Signature lines (unit: lines).
  • Equation: ℓ = H + S + B + C + G

  8. Revised body: 4 lines. Total now 12 lines.
  9. Compose the final letter ensuring each line adheres to the formal tone and structure.

Informal Letter

Informal letters are personal communications written to friends, relatives, or acquaintances with whom the writer shares a close relationship. Unlike formal letters, the tone is conversational, the language is relaxed, and the structure is flexible, allowing the writer to express emotions, share experiences, and maintain personal bonds. The primary purpose of an informal letter is to convey personal feelings, narrate events, seek or give advice, or simply stay connected. The essential elements include a heading (optional), a greeting, the body, a closing, and a signature. The heading may contain the writer’s address and the date, but it is not mandatory; many writers omit it altogether for brevity. The greeting, also called the salutation, reflects intimacy and can range from “Dear Riya,” to “Hey bro!” depending on the closeness of the relationship. The body of the letter is the heart of the communication, where the writer narrates events in a chronological or thematic order, uses colloquial expressions, and may intersperse humor or rhetorical questions. Paragraphs are usually short, and the writer often employs contractions such as “can’t,” “won’t,” and “I’m” to maintain a natural flow. The closing, known as the valediction, mirrors the informality of the greeting; common closings include “Love,” “Take care,” “Yours truly,” or even “Cheers.” Finally, the signature is simply the writer’s first name or a nickname, reinforcing the personal touch.

Several conditions influence the style and content of an informal letter. The age of the writer and the recipient, cultural background, and the medium (handwritten or electronic) shape the choice of language and format. For instance, teenagers often use slang and emojis when writing to peers, whereas older adults may prefer a more restrained yet still informal tone when writing to family members. Exceptions to the general guidelines arise when the writer wishes to convey a serious or sensitive message; in such cases, the tone may shift towards a semi‑formal style, employing polite expressions while retaining personal warmth. Moreover, while the body is typically narrative, it can also include lists, bullet points, or even short poems if the writer feels it enhances the emotional impact. The flexibility of informal letters allows for creative expression, but the writer must still ensure clarity, coherence, and relevance to the recipient’s interests.

Legal considerations are minimal for informal letters, yet certain ethical norms apply. The writer should avoid defamation, invasion of privacy, or any content that could be construed as harassment. In digital communications, the writer must be aware of data protection norms, especially when sharing personal photographs or sensitive information. Exceptions to these norms are rare and usually pertain to consensual sharing among close friends, but the writer should always respect the recipient’s comfort level. In summary, an informal letter is a versatile medium that balances personal expression with basic structural conventions, adapting to the writer’s intent, the recipient’s expectations, and the cultural context.

Derivation

To quantify the length of an informal letter, we can define a simple formula that relates the total word count ($W_{total}$) to the constituent parts of the letter:

$$W_{total}=W_{greeting}+W_{body}+W_{closing}+W_{signature}$$

Here, $W_{greeting}$ represents the number of words in the salutation, $W_{body}$ the sum of words across all paragraphs, $W_{closing}$ the words in the valediction, and $W_{signature}$ the words in the signature. If the writer uses $n$ paragraphs in the body, each with an average word count $ \overline{W}_{p}$, then:

$$W_{body}=n\cdot\overline{W}_{p}$$

Substituting this into the first equation gives:

$$W_{total}=W_{greeting}+n\cdot\overline{W}_{p}+W_{closing}+W_{signature}$$

This relationship helps writers estimate the length of their letter, ensuring it is neither too brief nor overly verbose for the intended purpose.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • $W_{total}$ – Total word count of the letter (unit: words)
  • $W_{greeting}$ – Word count of the greeting (unit: words)
  • $W_{body}$ – Word count of the body (unit: words)
  • $W_{closing}$ – Word count of the closing (unit: words)
  • $W_{signature}$ – Word count of the signature (unit: words)
  • $n$ – Number of paragraphs in the body (unit: count)
  • $\overline{W}_{p}$ – Average words per paragraph (unit: words)
Practice Question 1

Write an informal letter to your best friend describing a recent trip to the mountains. Include a greeting, at least three paragraphs in the body, a closing, and your signature. Ensure the total word count does not exceed 250 words.

Solution Steps:

  1. Start with a friendly greeting such as “Hey Amit,”.
  2. Paragraph 1: Briefly mention the purpose of the trip and the date of departure.
  3. Paragraph 2: Describe the journey, scenery, and any memorable incidents.
  4. Paragraph 3: Share personal reflections, feelings, and an invitation to visit.
  5. Conclude with an informal closing like “Catch you soon,” followed by your name.
  6. Count the words in each section to ensure the total is ≤ 250 words. Adjust by removing redundant adjectives or merging sentences if necessary.
Practice Question 2

Given a letter with the following word distribution: greeting – 3 words, body – 4 paragraphs with an average of 45 words each, closing – 2 words, signature – 1 word. Calculate the total word count using the derived formula.

Solution Steps:

  1. Identify the values: $W_{greeting}=3$, $n=4$, $\overline{W}_{p}=45$, $W_{closing}=2$, $W_{signature}=1$.
  2. Compute $W_{body}=n\cdot\overline{W}_{p}=4\times45=180$ words.
  3. Apply the total word formula: $W_{total}=3+180+2+1=186$ words.
  4. Thus, the letter contains 186 words in total, which falls within the typical range for a concise informal letter.

Formal Application

A formal application is a written request addressed to a person or an institution that seeks a specific action, permission, or information. It is a key communication tool in academic, professional, and administrative contexts. The primary objective of a formal application is to convey the request clearly, respectfully, and concisely while adhering to established conventions of tone, structure, and etiquette. The following sections detail the exhaustive theory, definitions, laws, conditions, and exceptions that govern the composition of a formal application.

Definition and Purpose
A formal application is a document that follows a prescribed format and uses a polite, impersonal tone to request an action or information. It differs from informal requests in that it must demonstrate respect for hierarchy, maintain a professional appearance, and provide all necessary details to facilitate a prompt response. The purpose can range from applying for admission, requesting a fee waiver, seeking a deadline extension, to applying for a job or a loan.

Structural Laws
1. Heading – The title of the application (e.g., “Application for Extension of Deadline”) must be centered and in uppercase or title case. 2. Date – Placed below the heading, aligned to the left. 3. Salutation – Uses a formal greeting such as “Dear Sir/Madam” or “To Whom It May Concern.” 4. Body – Contains a concise introduction, a clear statement of the request, supporting facts, and a polite closing. 5. Closing – Phrases like “Yours faithfully” or “Yours sincerely” followed by a comma. 6. Signature – The applicant’s name, designation (if applicable), and contact details. These elements must appear in the specified order; deviation can undermine the formality and may lead to rejection or delay.

Conditions and Exceptions
- Audience – The application must be tailored to the recipient’s position and the institution’s culture. - Length – Ideally one page; however, complex requests may extend to two pages but should remain concise. - Language – Use formal register, avoid contractions, slang, or emotive language. - Attachments – When required, attach supporting documents (e.g., fee receipts, medical certificates) and reference them in the body. - Exceptions – In emergency situations, a brief informal note may precede the formal application, but the main body must still adhere to formal conventions. - Legal Compliance – Certain applications (e.g., loan applications) must comply with statutory disclosure requirements and may need notarization.

Common Mistakes
- Using informal greetings or salutations. - Omitting the date or misplacing it. - Failing to state the request explicitly. - Overloading the body with irrelevant information. - Neglecting to sign the application. - Using incorrect punctuation or capitalization. Avoiding these pitfalls ensures the application is taken seriously and processed efficiently.

Derivation

The construction of a formal application can be represented as a linear combination of its constituent parts. Each part contributes a specific value to the overall effectiveness of the document.

$$ Application = Heading + Date + Salutation + Body + Closing + Signature $$

Where each symbol denotes a distinct component:

$$ Heading \rightarrow \text{Title of the application} $$ $$ Date \rightarrow \text{Date of writing} $$ $$ Salutation \rightarrow \text{Formal greeting} $$ $$ Body \rightarrow \text{Introduction} + \text{Request} + \text{Justification} + \text{Conclusion} $$ $$ Closing \rightarrow \text{Polite closing phrase} $$ $$ Signature \rightarrow \text{Applicant’s name and contact} $$

By ensuring that each component is present and correctly formatted, the application achieves the desired formal tone and clarity.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Application = Heading + Date + Salutation + Body + Closing + Signature (Unit: Document)
  • Heading (H) – 1 sentence (Unit: Sentence)
  • Date (D) – 1 line (Unit: Line)
  • Salutation (S) – 1 line (Unit: Line)
  • Body (B) – 3–5 sentences (Unit: Sentences)
  • Closing (C) – 1 line (Unit: Line)
  • Signature (Sig) – 1 line (Unit: Line)

Informal Application

Informal application is a type of written communication used to request something, express a desire, or convey a personal sentiment in a relaxed, conversational tone. Unlike formal applications, which adhere strictly to institutional protocols and a rigid structure, informal applications allow the writer to use colloquial language, personal anecdotes, and a friendly voice. The primary purpose is to establish a rapport with the reader while still achieving the objective of the request. The key elements of an informal application are the salutation, the body, the closing, and the signature, each of which can be adapted to suit the context and the relationship between the writer and the recipient.

Definitions: An informal application is a written request that is addressed to a person or a small group, often a friend, a colleague, or a supervisor in a non‑strict environment. It is characterized by a conversational tone, the use of first‑person pronouns, and a flexible structure. The language is usually less formal, with contractions, idioms, and sometimes humor. The writer may also include personal details that help the reader understand the context of the request.

Laws and Conditions: The effectiveness of an informal application depends on several conditions. First, the writer must maintain clarity; the request should be stated explicitly. Second, politeness is essential; even in a relaxed tone, the writer should show respect for the reader’s time and position. Third, the application should be concise; excessive verbosity can dilute the message. Fourth, the writer should tailor the tone to the recipient’s familiarity; a very casual tone may be inappropriate for a senior manager, whereas a slightly formal tone may be unnecessary for a close friend.

Exceptions: In certain contexts, informal applications may be inappropriate. For instance, when dealing with legal matters, medical requests, or situations that require official documentation, a formal application is mandatory. Additionally, if the recipient is a complete stranger or a high‑ranking official, an informal application may be perceived as disrespectful. In such cases, a hybrid approach—using a semi‑formal tone with some conversational elements—can be employed.

In practice, informal applications are commonly used for requests such as: asking a friend to borrow a book, requesting a colleague to cover a shift, or appealing to a supervisor for a short leave. The writer’s voice should reflect the nature of the relationship, but the core structure remains consistent: a friendly greeting, a clear statement of purpose, a brief explanation, a courteous closing, and a signature.

Derivation

Let us derive a simple structural formula for an informal application. We denote:

$$S = \text{Salutation (e.g., Dear John, Hi Sarah)}$$ $$B = \text{Body (request + explanation)}$$ $$C = \text{Closing (e.g., Thanks, Regards)}$$ $$Sig = \text{Signature (Name)}$$

Thus, the overall application (App) can be expressed as:

$$App = S + B + C + Sig$$

Each component can be further broken down:

$$B = R + E$$

where R is the request statement and E is the explanatory context.

In terms of measurable units, we can count words (w) and sentences (s) to gauge length:

$$\text{Length} = w_{\text{total}} = w_S + w_B + w_C + w_{Sig}$$

For a balanced informal application, a typical guideline is:

$$w_{\text{total}} \approx 100 \text{–} 150 \text{ words}$$

and

$$s_{\text{total}} \approx 4 \text{–} 6 \text{ sentences}.$$

These equations help writers maintain brevity while ensuring all essential elements are present.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • App – Informal Application; unit: document
  • S – Salutation; unit: phrase
  • B – Body; unit: paragraph
  • C – Closing; unit: phrase
  • Sig – Signature; unit: name
  • R – Request statement; unit: sentence
  • E – Explanation; unit: sentence
  • w – Words; unit: words (SI unit: word)
  • s – Sentences; unit: sentence (SI unit: sentence)

Report

A report is a formal document that presents information in an organized, objective, and systematic manner for a specific audience. It differs from essays and letters in its purpose, structure, and tone. The primary aim of a report is to convey facts, analysis, and recommendations based on evidence, thereby facilitating decision‑making. In the CBSE Class 12 English curriculum, the “Report” falls under the Advanced Writing Skills unit, where students learn to adopt a neutral voice, use headings and sub‑headings, and present data in tables, charts, or diagrams when necessary.

Key definitions:
• Report – A written account that records, analyses, and interprets data or events, often concluding with actionable suggestions.
• Heading – A short title that indicates the content of a section; it helps the reader navigate the document.
• Sub‑heading – A secondary title that breaks down a heading into finer topics, ensuring logical flow.

Fundamental laws governing report writing include the Law of Objectivity, which mandates that personal bias be eliminated; the Law of Clarity, which requires concise language and unambiguous terminology; and the Law of Relevance, which insists that every piece of information directly support the report’s purpose. Violating any of these laws compromises the credibility of the document.

Conditions for a successful report:
1. Purpose clarity – The writer must identify whether the report is informative, analytical, or persuasive.
2. Audience awareness – Language, depth of detail, and technical jargon should match the reader’s knowledge level.
3. Structure adherence – A typical report follows the sequence: Title, Abstract/Executive Summary, Introduction, Methodology, Findings, Discussion, Conclusion, and Recommendations.
4. Evidence support – All statements must be backed by data, citations, or observations.

Exceptions and special cases:
• Progress reports may omit a formal conclusion if the project is ongoing.
• Incident reports often use a chronological narrative rather than a thematic structure.
• Technical reports sometimes replace an executive summary with a “Key Findings” box for quick reference.
Understanding these nuances enables the writer to adapt the generic template to specific contexts without sacrificing coherence.

Derivation

The logical flow of a report can be expressed as a sequence of functions that transform raw data into a final document. Let D represent the set of raw data, A the analysis function, S the synthesis function, and R the final report. The transformation can be written as: $$R = S\big(A(D)\big)$$

Step 1: Apply the analysis function A to the data set D to obtain interpreted results I:

$$I = A(D)$$

Step 2: Synthesize the interpreted results I with structural elements (headings, tables, charts) using the synthesis function S:

$$R = S(I)$$

Step 3: Incorporate recommendations C based on the conclusions drawn from I. The final report becomes:

$$R_{\text{final}} = S(I) + C$$

Thus, the derivation shows that a report is essentially a two‑stage transformation: data → analysis → synthesis, followed by the addition of actionable recommendations.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Word‑count density: $W_d = \frac{W}{P}$, where $W$ = total words (unit: words), $P$ = number of pages (unit: pages).
  • Reading time estimate: $T_r = \frac{W}{R_s}$, where $R_s$ = average reading speed (unit: words per minute, wpm).
  • Data‑to‑text ratio: $DTR = \frac{N_d}{N_t}$, where $N_d$ = number of data points (unit: count), $N_t$ = number of textual statements (unit: count).
  • Recommendation impact factor: $I_f = \frac{E_{post}}{E_{pre}}$, where $E_{post}$ = expected outcome after implementation, $E_{pre}$ = baseline outcome (unitless).
Practice Problem 1 Practice Question: A student is required to write a 1500‑word report on “The Impact of Renewable Energy on Rural Development.” The report must contain an executive summary of 150 words, three main sections each with equal word allocation, and a conclusion of 100 words. Calculate the word count that should be allocated to each of the three main sections. Solution Steps: 1. Subtract the word count of the executive summary and conclusion from the total word limit.
$$W_{\text{remaining}} = 1500 - (150 + 100) = 1250\ \text{words}$$ 2. Since there are three main sections with equal allocation, divide the remaining words by three.
$$W_{\text{section}} = \frac{1250}{3} \approx 416.67\ \text{words}$$ 3. Round to the nearest whole number for practical writing.
$$W_{\text{section}} \approx 417\ \text{words per section}$$ Thus, each of the three main sections should contain roughly 417 words.
Practice Problem 2 Practice Question: In a technical report, a table presents the monthly electricity consumption (in kilowatt‑hours, kWh) for six months: 120, 135, 150, 165, 180, and 195. The report requires a statement of the average monthly consumption and the percentage increase from the first month to the last month. Compute the average consumption and the percentage increase. Solution Steps: 1. Compute the sum of the six consumption values.
$$\Sigma C = 120 + 135 + 150 + 165 + 180 + 195 = 945\ \text{kWh}$$ 2. Find the average monthly consumption.
$$\bar{C} = \frac{\Sigma C}{6} = \frac{945}{6} = 157.5\ \text{kWh}$$ 3. Determine the increase from the first month ($C_1 = 120$ kWh) to the last month ($C_6 = 195$ kWh).
$$\Delta C = C_6 - C_1 = 195 - 120 = 75\ \text{kWh}$$ 4. Calculate the percentage increase relative to the first month.
$$\%\,\text{Increase} = \left(\frac{\Delta C}{C_1}\right) \times 100 = \left(\frac{75}{120}\right) \times 100 = 62.5\%$$ Result: The average monthly consumption is 157.5 kWh, and the consumption increased by 62.5 % from the first to the sixth month.

Article

An article is a written composition that presents information, analysis, or opinion on a particular subject. Unlike a narrative essay, which primarily tells a story, an article is primarily informative or persuasive, aiming to convey facts, viewpoints, or arguments to a specific audience. The core purpose of an article is to inform, educate, or influence the reader, often within a constrained length and a clear structure. Articles are commonly found in newspapers, magazines, journals, and online platforms, and they adhere to a set of conventions that distinguish them from other forms of writing.

There are several distinct types of articles, each governed by its own conventions and objectives. News articles focus on recent events and are written in a factual, objective tone, often following the inverted pyramid structure where the most critical information appears first. Feature articles delve deeper into a topic, using narrative techniques, anecdotes, and descriptive language to engage the reader emotionally. Editorials express the opinion of the publication or a specific writer, employing a persuasive voice and often addressing current issues. Review articles evaluate products, books, or services, combining objective assessment with subjective judgment. How‑to or instructional articles provide step‑by‑step guidance, emphasizing clarity and practicality.

The structure of an article is a blueprint that guides the reader through the content. The headline captures attention and summarizes the main point. The lede (or lead paragraph) offers the most essential information—who, what, when, where, why, and how—setting the stage for the rest of the piece. The body is divided into logical sections, often marked by subheadings, each containing supporting facts, quotes, statistics, and examples. The conclusion ties the narrative together, reiterating key points, offering a call to action, or leaving the reader with a thought‑provoking insight. In feature and editorial pieces, additional elements such as anecdotes, rhetorical questions, and vivid imagery are woven throughout to maintain engagement.

Writing style in an article is governed by several “laws” that ensure clarity, credibility, and reader interest. First, the article must be accurate; facts should be verified and sources cited. Second, it should maintain objectivity unless it is an opinion piece, in which case the bias must be acknowledged. Third, the tone should be appropriate to the audience—formal for academic journals, conversational for lifestyle magazines. Fourth, the voice should be active and direct, avoiding excessive jargon unless the target readership is specialized. Finally, the article should be concise, eliminating redundancy and ensuring each sentence contributes to the overall purpose.

There are conditions and exceptions to these conventions. The inverted pyramid is standard for news articles but is often abandoned in feature stories where a narrative arc is preferred. Opinion pieces may deliberately use a first‑person perspective and rhetorical devices that would be inappropriate in a strictly factual report. In academic articles, the structure may include abstract, methodology, results, and discussion sections, diverging from the typical journalistic format. Additionally, some online platforms allow for multimedia integration—images, videos, infographics—within the article body, which can alter the pacing and emphasis of the written content.

Derivation

To quantify certain aspects of an article, we can derive simple formulas that relate measurable variables. These formulas help writers plan length, estimate reading time, and assess readability.

1. Reading Time (RT) is calculated by dividing the total word count (W) by the average reading speed (R) of the target audience. The derivation is straightforward:

$$RT = \frac{W}{R}$$

where $W$ is measured in words and $R$ in words per minute (wpm). The result $RT$ is expressed in minutes.

2. Average Sentence Length (ASL) measures the average number of words per sentence, indicating sentence complexity:

$$ASL = \frac{W}{S}$$

where $S$ is the total number of sentences. $ASL$ is expressed in words per sentence.

3. Article Length (AL) can be broken down into headline words ($H$), subheading words ($SH$), and body words ($B$). The total word count is the sum of these components:

$$AL = H + SH + B$$

Each variable is measured in words. This formula assists editors in ensuring that the article meets publication constraints.

Formula and SI‑unit summary
  • Reading Time (RT): $$RT = \frac{W}{R}$$
    • $W$ – Total words (unit: words)
    • $R$ – Reading speed (unit: words per minute)
    • $RT$ – Reading time (unit: minutes)
  • Average Sentence Length (ASL): $$ASL = \frac{W}{S}$$
    • $W$ – Total words (unit: words)
    • $S$ – Total sentences (unit: sentences)
    • $ASL$ – Average words per sentence (unit: words per sentence)
  • Article Length (AL): $$AL = H + SH + B$$
    • $H$ – Headline words (unit: words)
    • $SH$ – Subheading words (unit: words)
    • $B$ – Body words (unit: words)
    • $AL$ – Total article words (unit: words)
Practice Question 1 A news article contains 1,200 words. The average reading speed of the target audience is 200 words per minute. Calculate the estimated reading time for the article.

Solution:

  1. Identify variables: $W = 1,200$ words, $R = 200$ wpm.
  2. Apply the reading time formula: $$RT = \frac{W}{R} = \frac{1,200}{200} = 6$$
  3. Interpretation: The article will take approximately 6 minutes to read.
Practice Question 2 A feature article has 1,200 words and contains 40 sentences. Determine the average sentence length.

Solution:

  1. Identify variables: $W = 1,200$ words, $S = 40$ sentences.
  2. Apply the average sentence length formula: $$ASL = \frac{W}{S} = \frac{1,200}{40} = 30$$
  3. Interpretation: Each sentence averages 30 words, indicating a moderate level of complexity suitable for a general audience.

Speech

Speech is a structured form of verbal communication that employs language to convey ideas, emotions, and information to an audience. It can be classified into several distinct categories: formal speeches, informal speeches, persuasive speeches, informative speeches, and ceremonial speeches. Each category follows its own set of conventions, purposes, and audience expectations.

In formal speeches, the speaker adheres to a predetermined structure, often including an introduction, body, and conclusion, and employs rhetorical devices such as ethos, pathos, and logos to establish credibility, appeal to emotions, and present logical arguments. Informal speeches are more spontaneous and conversational, allowing the speaker to adapt to the audience’s reactions in real time. Persuasive speeches aim to influence the audience’s beliefs or actions, relying heavily on emotional appeals and logical reasoning. Informative speeches focus on delivering knowledge, facts, and explanations, prioritizing clarity and organization over emotional impact. Ceremonial speeches, such as eulogies or toasts, are tailored to specific occasions and often incorporate cultural or symbolic elements.

Several laws govern effective speech delivery. The Law of Clarity states that the speaker must use language that is easily understood by the target audience; ambiguity or excessive jargon can dilute the message. The Law of Engagement requires the speaker to maintain eye contact, modulate tone, and use gestures to sustain audience interest. The Law of Timing dictates that speeches should be paced appropriately; speaking too quickly can overwhelm listeners, while speaking too slowly may cause disengagement. The Law of Adaptation emphasizes the need for the speaker to adjust content and style based on audience demographics, cultural context, and situational factors.

Conditions for a successful speech include a clear purpose, a well-defined audience, a structured outline, and rehearsal. The speaker must also consider the venue’s acoustics, available technology, and time constraints. Exceptions arise in impromptu or extemporaneous speeches, where the speaker may rely on a loose outline or mental notes rather than a fully scripted text. In such cases, the speaker’s spontaneity and ability to think on their feet become critical. Additionally, informal or conversational speeches may deliberately break conventional rules to foster a relaxed atmosphere and encourage audience participation.

Speech also involves non-verbal elements such as posture, facial expressions, and vocal variety. These non-verbal cues can reinforce or contradict the spoken words, influencing the audience’s perception of the speaker’s authenticity and confidence. Mastery of both verbal and non-verbal communication is essential for delivering a compelling and persuasive speech.

Derivation

1. Words per minute (WPM) is calculated by dividing the total number of words by the duration in minutes:
$$\text{WPM} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{Duration (min)}}$$
2. Average speaking rate in words per second (WPS) is derived by converting minutes to seconds:
$$\text{WPS} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{Duration (min)} \times 60}$$
3. Speech length in minutes can be found by dividing total words by WPM:
$$\text{Length (min)} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{WPM}}$$
4. Sound level in decibels (dB) for a given sound power \(P\) relative to a reference power \(P_{\text{ref}}\) is:
$$L = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}}\right)$$
5. Clarity Index (CI) as a percentage of clear words to total words:
$$\text{CI} = \left(\frac{\text{Clear Words}}{\text{Total Words}}\right) \times 100\%$$
These equations provide quantitative measures that help speakers assess and refine their delivery.

Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Words per minute: $$\text{WPM} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{Duration (min)}}$$
    • Variables: Total Words (dimensionless), Duration (min)
  • Words per second: $$\text{WPS} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{Duration (min)} \times 60}$$
    • Variables: Total Words (dimensionless), Duration (min)
  • Speech length: $$\text{Length (min)} = \frac{\text{Total Words}}{\text{WPM}}$$
    • Variables: Total Words (dimensionless), WPM (words/min)
  • Sound level: $$L = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}}\right)$$
    • Variables: P (W), P_ref (W)
    • SI unit: decibel (dB)
  • Clarity Index: $$\text{CI} = \left(\frac{\text{Clear Words}}{\text{Total Words}}\right) \times 100\%$$
    • Variables: Clear Words (dimensionless), Total Words (dimensionless)
Practice Problem 1 A speaker delivered a 1200‑word speech in 15 minutes. Calculate the words per minute and the average speaking rate in words per second.

Solution:
1. Words per minute:
$$\text{WPM} = \frac{1200}{15} = 80 \text{ words/min}$$
2. Words per second:
$$\text{WPS} = \frac{1200}{15 \times 60} = \frac{1200}{900} \approx 1.33 \text{ words/s}$$
Practice Problem 2 The sound power of a speaker is \(1.0 \times 10^{-3}\) W. The reference power is \(1.0 \times 10^{-12}\) W. Calculate the sound level in decibels.

Solution:
1. Compute the ratio:
$$\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-3}}{1.0 \times 10^{-12}} = 1.0 \times 10^{9}$$
2. Apply the decibel formula:
$$L = 10 \log_{10}(1.0 \times 10^{9}) = 10 \times 9 = 90 \text{ dB}$$

Review

A review is a critical evaluation of a literary work, a film, a play, a piece of music, a product, or any other form of creative or commercial output. It goes beyond mere summary; it involves analysis, interpretation, and judgment. The primary purpose of a review is to inform the potential audience about the merits and demerits of the subject, helping them decide whether to engage with it. A well‑crafted review balances objectivity with personal response, providing evidence‑based arguments while reflecting the reviewer’s voice.

In the context of English language and literature, a review typically follows a structured format: an introduction that identifies the work and its creator, a brief synopsis that avoids spoilers, a body that examines key elements such as theme, style, characterisation, and technical aspects, and a conclusion that offers a clear verdict. The language used should be precise, vivid, and persuasive, employing rhetorical devices like metaphors, similes, and parallelism to enhance readability.

Several conditions must be met for a review to be effective. First, the reviewer must have a thorough understanding of the genre and the conventions that govern it. Second, the evaluation should be supported by concrete examples from the work—quotations, scenes, or specific features. Third, the tone should remain respectful; even harsh criticism must be constructive, pointing out how the work could be improved rather than merely dismissing it.

There are notable exceptions to the conventional format. For instance, a micro‑review on social media may omit a detailed synopsis due to character limits, focusing instead on a rapid impression and a rating. Similarly, a peer‑review in an academic journal follows a different set of criteria, emphasizing methodology, originality, and contribution to the field rather than entertainment value. In such cases, the reviewer adapts the language and structure to suit the target audience and platform.

Legal and ethical considerations also influence review writing. Plagiarism is strictly prohibited; the reviewer must credit any direct quotations or paraphrased ideas. Defamation laws require that any negative statements be factual and verifiable, avoiding unfounded personal attacks. Moreover, conflicts of interest—such as receiving compensation from the creator—must be disclosed to maintain credibility.

Derivation

To quantify the overall assessment of a work, reviewers often use a weighted rating system. Suppose a review evaluates three criteria: Content (C), Presentation (P), and Impact (I). Each criterion is assigned a weight reflecting its relative importance: $w_C$, $w_P$, and $w_I$, where $w_C + w_P + w_I = 1$.

$$\text{Overall Rating (R)} = w_C \times C + w_P \times P + w_I \times I$$

Here, $C$, $P$, and $I$ are scores on a scale of 0 to 10. The weights ensure that the sum of contributions equals the final rating.

Step‑by‑step calculation:

  1. Determine the individual scores: $C = 8$, $P = 7$, $I = 9$.
  2. Assign weights based on the review’s focus: $w_C = 0.4$, $w_P = 0.3$, $w_I = 0.3$.
  3. Multiply each score by its weight:
    • $0.4 \times 8 = 3.2$
    • $0.3 \times 7 = 2.1$
    • $0.3 \times 9 = 2.7$
  4. Add the weighted contributions: $$R = 3.2 + 2.1 + 2.7 = 8.0$$
  5. The final overall rating is $8.0$ out of $10$.
Formula and SI-unit summary
  • Overall Rating (R): $R = w_C \times C + w_P \times P + w_I \times I$ — dimensionless (scale 0–10)
  • Weight ($w$): $w_C, w_P, w_I$ — dimensionless fractions, $\sum w = 1$
  • Score (C, P, I): numeric value on a predefined scale (commonly 0–10) — dimensionless
Practice Problem 1

Write a review of the novel “The Alchemist” by Paulo Coelho. Your review should include an introduction, a brief synopsis (max 50 words), analysis of theme and character development, and a concluding verdict with a rating out of 5.

Solution Steps:

  1. Identify the work and author: “The Alchemist” by Paulo Coelho.
  2. Craft an engaging introduction that mentions the genre (philosophical fiction) and the novel’s global popularity.
  3. Provide a concise synopsis: “A shepherd boy named Santiago embarks on a journey across the desert in search of a hidden treasure, discovering the true meaning of destiny.”
  4. Analyze the central theme of personal legend and the interplay between fate and free will, citing specific passages.
  5. Discuss character development, focusing on Santiago’s transformation from a naive dreamer to a self‑realised seeker.
  6. Conclude with a clear verdict, e.g., “A timeless tale that inspires introspection, deserving a solid 4.5 out of 5.”
  7. Optionally, apply the weighted rating formula: assign scores (Content=9, Presentation=8, Impact=9) with weights (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) to compute an overall rating of 8.8/10, which translates to 4.4/5.
Practice Problem 2

Using the weighted rating formula, evaluate a film based on the following scores: Story = 7.5, Cinematography = 8.2, Music = 6.8. Assign weights of 0.5 for Story, 0.3 for Cinematography, and 0.2 for Music. Calculate the overall rating and express it on a 5‑star scale.

Solution Steps:

  1. List the given scores: $S = 7.5$, $C = 8.2$, $M = 6.8$.
  2. Write the weights: $w_S = 0.5$, $w_C = 0.3$, $w_M = 0.2$.
  3. Apply the formula: $$R = 0.5 \times 7.5 + 0.3 \times 8.2 + 0.2 \times 6.8$$
  4. Calculate each term:
    • $0.5 \times 7.5 = 3.75$
    • $0.3 \times 8.2 = 2.46$
    • $0.2 \times 6.8 = 1.36$
  5. Sum the contributions: $$R = 3.75 + 2.46 + 1.36 = 7.57$$
  6. Convert the 10‑point rating to a 5‑star scale by dividing by 2: $$\text{Stars} = \frac{7.57}{2} \approx 3.79$$
  7. Round to the nearest half‑star: 3.5 stars out of 5.
  8. Thus, the film receives an overall rating of 3.5/5 stars.
