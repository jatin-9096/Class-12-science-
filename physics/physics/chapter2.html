1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Electrostatic Potential: Definition and Physical Meaning

The electrostatic potential $V$ at a point in space is defined as the work done per unit positive test charge in bringing the charge from infinity to that point, without any acceleration. Mathematically, $$V = \frac{W}{q}\,,$$ where $W$ is the work done against the electric field and $q$ is the magnitude of the test charge. Since the work is path‑independent in an electrostatic field, $V$ is a scalar quantity and can be assigned a unique value at every point in the field. The unit of potential is the volt (V), where $1\ \text{V}=1\ \text{J C}^{-1}$. The concept of potential simplifies many electrostatic problems because the superposition principle applies directly to potentials: the total potential at a point due to several charges is the algebraic sum of the individual potentials. For a point charge $Q$ situated at a distance $r$, the potential is $$V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}\,,$$ where $\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space. This expression highlights that potential varies inversely with distance, unlike the electric field which varies as $1/r^2$. The sign of $V$ follows the sign of $Q$: a positive charge creates a positive potential, while a negative charge creates a negative potential. Potential is a convenient way to discuss energy considerations because the potential energy $U$ of a charge $q$ placed in a potential $V$ is simply $U = qV$.

Physics · Core Law
Electric Potential Difference and Equipotential Surfaces

The potential difference (or voltage) between two points $A$ and $B$ is the work done per unit charge in moving a test charge from $A$ to $B$ against the electric field. It is expressed as $$\Delta V_{AB}=V_B-V_A = -\int_{A}^{B}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}\,,$$ where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field vector and $d\mathbf{l}$ is an infinitesimal displacement along the path. The negative sign indicates that the potential decreases in the direction of the field. Because $\mathbf{E}$ is the gradient of the potential, we have $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$. Equipotential surfaces are loci of points that share the same potential value. By definition, no work is required to move a charge along an equipotential surface because $\Delta V=0$; consequently, the electric field is always perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. For a point charge, the equipotentials are concentric spheres centered on the charge. In the case of a uniform electric field, equipotentials are parallel planes spaced uniformly. Understanding equipotentials is essential for visualizing field configurations and for solving problems involving conductors, where the entire surface of a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential.

Physics · Core Law
Capacitance: Definition, Formulae, and Types of Capacitors

Capacitance $C$ quantifies a system’s ability to store electric charge per unit potential difference. It is defined as $$C=\frac{Q}{\Delta V}\,,$$ where $Q$ is the magnitude of charge on either plate of a capacitor and $\Delta V$ is the potential difference between the plates. The SI unit of capacitance is the farad (F), where $1\ \text{F}=1\ \text{C V}^{-1}$. In practice, most capacitors have values in the microfarad ($\mu$F), nanofarad (nF), or picofarad (pF) range. For a parallel‑plate capacitor with plate area $A$ and separation $d$, filled with a dielectric of permittivity $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$, the capacitance is $$C=\frac{\varepsilon A}{d}\,.$$ This expression shows that capacitance increases with larger plate area, higher dielectric constant, and smaller separation. Other common geometries include cylindrical and spherical capacitors, each having distinct formulae derived from the geometry of the conductors and the dielectric medium. Dielectrics enhance capacitance by reducing the effective electric field within the capacitor. The dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$ is a dimensionless factor that measures how much a material can be polarized by an external field. When a dielectric is inserted, the capacitance becomes $C'=\varepsilon_r C$, and the stored energy for a given charge also changes, as discussed later.

Physics · Core Law
Energy Stored in Capacitors and the Role of Dielectrics

The energy $U$ stored in a capacitor is the work required to charge it from zero to a charge $Q$ (or equivalently to a voltage $V$). By integrating the incremental work $dW = V\,dQ$, we obtain three equivalent forms: $$U = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{1}{2}CV^{2} = \frac{Q^{2}}{2C}\,.$$ These expressions are useful in different contexts: the first emphasizes charge, the second voltage, and the third capacitance. When a dielectric is introduced after the capacitor is charged and isolated, the voltage across the plates decreases because the dielectric reduces the electric field. The stored energy also decreases, and the difference appears as mechanical work done in inserting the dielectric. Conversely, if the capacitor remains connected to a constant voltage source while the dielectric is inserted, the charge on the plates increases, and the energy supplied by the source is partially stored in the electric field and partially transferred to the dielectric as polarization energy. The energy density $u$ (energy per unit volume) in the electric field of a capacitor is given by $$u = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^{2}\,,$$ where $E$ is the magnitude of the electric field. This relation underscores the importance of the dielectric constant: a higher $\varepsilon$ allows more energy to be stored for the same field strength, which is why high‑$\varepsilon_r$ materials are sought for compact energy‑storage devices.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this section we present the standard CBSE‑level derivations that form the backbone of Chapter 2 – Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance. Each derivation follows a logical sequence, beginning from fundamental definitions, invoking Gauss’s law or the principle of superposition where required, and arriving at the compact formulae that appear in the textbook. The derivations are written in a step‑by‑step manner so that a Class‑12 student can follow the algebraic manipulations without any hidden jumps.

1. Relation between Electric Field and Potential

We start from the definition of electric potential difference between two points A and B as the work done per unit charge by the electric field when a test charge moves from A to B:

$$V_B - V_A = -\int_{A}^{B}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

Taking the limit where B approaches A, the differential form is obtained:

$$dV = -\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$

Choosing the displacement $d\mathbf{l}$ along the direction of the field, we have $d\mathbf{l}=dl\,\hat{e}$ and $\mathbf{E}=E\hat{e}$. Hence

$$\frac{dV}{dl} = -E$$

In vector notation this becomes the fundamental relation

$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$$

Thus the electric field is the negative gradient of the scalar potential. This result is used repeatedly in the subsequent derivations.

2. Potential due to a Point Charge

Consider a point charge $q$ placed at the origin. The electric field at a distance $r$ from the charge is given by Coulomb’s law:

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^{2}}\hat{r}$$

Using the relation $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$, we write the radial component:

$$E_r = -\frac{dV}{dr}$$

Substituting the expression for $E_r$:

$$-\frac{dV}{dr} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^{2}}$$

Integrating both sides from a reference point at infinity (where $V=0$) to a point at distance $r$:

$$\int_{0}^{V(r)} dV = -\int_{\infty}^{r}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^{2}}dr$$

Carrying out the integration gives

$$V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}$$

This is the well‑known expression for the electrostatic potential of a point charge.

3. Potential of a System of Point Charges

For a collection of $n$ point charges $q_1, q_2, \dots , q_n$ located at positions $\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \dots , \mathbf{r}_n$, the principle of superposition allows us to write the total potential at a field point $\mathbf{r}$ as the algebraic sum of the individual potentials:

$$V(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_i}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|}$$

Each term is obtained exactly as in the single‑charge case, and the linearity of the governing equations guarantees that the sum is valid.

4. Potential Energy of a System of Point Charges

The electrostatic potential energy $U$ of a system of point charges is defined as the work required to assemble the charges from infinity to their final positions, keeping each charge stationary while the others are already in place. For two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r_{12}$, the work done in bringing $q_2$ into the field of $q_1$ is

$$U_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$

Extending this to $n$ charges, we must avoid double counting. The total potential energy is therefore

$$U = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{q_i q_j}{r_{ij}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i V_i$$

where $V_i$ is the potential at the location of $q_i$ due to all other charges. The factor $\frac12$ corrects for the fact that each pair $(i,j)$ appears twice in the double sum.

5. Capacitance of a Parallel‑Plate Capacitor (Vacuum)

A parallel‑plate capacitor consists of two large conducting plates of area $A$, separated by a uniform distance $d$, with vacuum (or air) in between. The electric field between the plates, assuming edge effects are negligible, is uniform and given by

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

where $\sigma = \frac{Q}{A}$ is the surface charge density on the positively charged plate. The potential difference $V$ between the plates follows from $V = Ed$:

$$V = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\,d$$

Capacitance $C$ is defined as $C = \frac{Q}{V}$. Substituting the expression for $V$ yields

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

This is the standard formula for the capacitance of a parallel‑plate capacitor in vacuum.

6. Capacitance with a Dielectric Medium

If the space between the plates is completely filled with a dielectric material of relative permittivity (dielectric constant) $\kappa$, the electric field is reduced by a factor $\kappa$ because the free‑charge field is partially cancelled by bound charges. The modified field becomes $E = \frac{\sigma}{\kappa\varepsilon_0}$, and the potential difference is

$$V = \frac{Q d}{\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}$$

Consequently, the capacitance increases by the factor $\kappa$:

$$C = \kappa\,\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d}$$

where $\varepsilon = \kappa\varepsilon_0$ is the absolute permittivity of the dielectric.

7. Energy Stored in a Capacitor

The work done in charging a capacitor from zero to a final charge $Q$ is the area under the $V$–$Q$ curve. Since $V = Q/C$, the incremental work $dW$ required to add a small charge $dQ$ is

$$dW = V\,dQ = \frac{Q}{C}\,dQ$$

Integrating from $0$ to $Q$ gives the total electrostatic energy $U$ stored in the capacitor:

$$U = \int_{0}^{Q}\frac{Q'}{C}\,dQ' = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^{2}}{C}$$

Using $V = Q/C$, the energy can also be expressed in alternative forms:

$$U = \frac{1}{2}CV^{2} = \frac{1}{2}QV$$

All three expressions are equivalent and are frequently used depending on which quantities are known.

8. Energy Density in an Electric Field

Consider a volume element $d\tau$ within a uniform electric field $E$. The energy stored in this element is $dU = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^{2} d\tau$. Dividing by the volume gives the energy density $u$:

$$u = \frac{dU}{d\tau} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^{2}$$

This result is universal; it holds for any configuration of static electric fields, not just parallel plates.

9. Potential Due to an Infinite Charged Plane

An infinite plane with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$ creates a constant electric field on either side, given by $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ (pointing away from the plane if $\sigma>0$). Choosing the plane as the reference $V=0$, the potential at a distance $x$ from the plane is obtained by integrating the field:

$$V(x) = -\int_{0}^{x}E\,dx' = -\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}x$$

Thus the potential varies linearly with distance from the plane, a useful result for problems involving charged sheets.

10. Potential of a Uniformly Charged Sphere (Outside)

For a sphere of radius $R$ carrying total charge $Q$ uniformly distributed over its surface, the external field is identical to that of a point charge at the centre. Therefore the potential at a point outside the sphere ($r\ge R$) is

$$V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}$$

At the surface ($r=R$) this reduces to $V_R = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R}$.

Inside the sphere ($r $$V(r) = V_R - \int_{R}^{r}E(r')\,dr' = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R} - \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 R^{3}}(r^{2}-R^{2})$$

which simplifies to

$$V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{2R}\left(3-\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}}\right)$$

This expression is often required when dealing with spherical conductors or charged insulating spheres.
