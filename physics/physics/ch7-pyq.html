Practice Questions – Detailed Theory (Class 12 Physics)

Practice Question 1 (5 marks)

Derive the expression for the magnetic field at a point P on the axis of a circular loop of radius $R$ carrying a current $I$. Show that for $P$ at a distance $x$ from the centre (along the axis) the field is given by

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$$

Marking Scheme:

  • Statement of Biot–Savart law – 1 mark
  • Setting up the geometry and element $d\ell$ – 1 mark
  • Evaluation of the integral (showing limits and symmetry) – 2 marks
  • Final simplified result – 1 mark

Practice Question 2 (5 marks)

Explain the working principle of a Hall probe and derive the formula for Hall voltage $V_H$ in terms of magnetic field $B$, current $I$, carrier density $n$, charge $e$, and thickness $t$ of the semiconductor.

Marking Scheme:

  • Diagram and description of Hall probe – 1 mark
  • Force balance on charge carriers (Lorentz force = electric force) – 1 mark
  • Derivation of $V_H = \dfrac{IB}{net}$ – 2 marks
  • Explanation of each term and practical considerations – 1 mark

Practice Question 3 (3 marks)

State and explain Lenz’s law with the help of a simple example involving a moving magnet and a conducting loop.

Marking Scheme:

  • Statement of Lenz’s law – 1 mark
  • Illustrative example (magnet entering loop) – 1 mark
  • Explanation of induced emf direction opposing the change – 1 mark

Practice Question 4 (3 marks)

Marking Scheme:

  • Formula $U = \dfrac{1}{2}LI^2$ – 1 mark
  • Derivation sketch (integrating $V = L \frac{dI}{dt}$) – 1 mark
  • Application (e.g., smoothing current in power supplies) – 1 mark

Practice Question 5 (3 marks)

Define magnetic dipole moment $\vec{\mu}$ for a current loop and state its direction using the right‑hand rule.

Marking Scheme:

  • Definition $\vec{\mu}= I\vec{A}$ (where $\vec{A}$ is area vector) – 1 mark
  • Magnitude $ \mu = IA$ – 1 mark
  • Direction given by right‑hand rule (thumb along $\vec{\mu}$) – 1 mark

State the unit of magnetic flux density $B$ in the SI system.

  • Answer: tesla (T) – 2 marks

Case Study 1 – Magnetic Braking in High‑Speed Trains

Passage: Modern high‑speed trains such as the Japanese Shinkansen and the French TGV use magnetic braking to decelerate safely without mechanical wear. When the train needs to slow down, a set of copper or aluminium plates is mounted on the bogie, while powerful electromagnets are placed on the carriage body. On energising the electromagnets, a magnetic field $B$ penetrates the conducting plates. According to Lenz’s law, an eddy current $I_{\text{eddy}}$ is induced in the plates, producing a magnetic field opposite to the applied field. The interaction between the applied field and the induced field creates a retarding force $F_{\text{brake}}$ that is proportional to the train’s speed $v$:

$$F_{\text{brake}} = -k\,v,$$ where $k$ depends on the geometry of the plates, the conductivity $\sigma$, and the magnetic field strength. The braking torque is therefore non‑contact, friction‑less, and highly reliable. The system is also regenerative: the induced emf can be fed back to the power supply, improving overall energy efficiency.

Practice Questions

  1. Practice Question 1: Derive the expression for the induced emf $\mathcal{E}$ in a rectangular conducting plate of length $l$ moving with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $B$.
  2. Practice Question 2: Show that the retarding force $F_{\text{brake}}$ is directly proportional to the speed $v$ and obtain the proportionality constant $k$ in terms of $B$, $\sigma$, $l$, and the thickness $t$ of the plate.
  3. Practice Question 3: If the train of mass $m=2.5\times10^{5}\,\text{kg}$ is travelling at $v=80\,\text{m s}^{-1}$ and the magnetic brake provides $k=1.2\times10^{4}\,\text{N s m}^{-1}$, calculate the stopping distance assuming the brake is applied continuously until the train stops.
  4. Practice Question 4: Discuss two advantages of magnetic braking over conventional friction brakes in the context of high‑speed railways.

Full Solutions

Solution to Practice Question 1: Consider a rectangular plate of length $l$ moving with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a magnetic field $B$ (Fig. 1). The magnetic flux $\Phi$ through the plate is $\Phi = B\,A$, where $A = l\,x$ is the instantaneous area swept out as the plate moves a distance $x$. The rate of change of flux is

$$\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=B\,\frac{dA}{dt}=B\,l\,\frac{dx}{dt}=B\,l\,v.$$ By Faraday’s law, the magnitude of the induced emf is $$\mathcal{E}= \left|\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\right| = B\,l\,v.$$ The direction of the induced emf follows Lenz’s law, opposing the motion.

Solution to Practice Question 2: The induced emf drives an eddy current $I_{\text{eddy}}$ in the plate. For a plate of thickness $t$ and conductivity $\sigma$, the resistance $R$ of the current path of length $l$ and cross‑sectional area $l\,t$ is

$$R = \frac{\rho\,l}{l\,t}= \frac{1}{\sigma t},$$ where $\rho=1/\sigma$ is the resistivity. Hence $$I_{\text{eddy}} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}= B\,l\,v \,\sigma t.$$ The magnetic force on a current‑carrying conductor of length $l$ in a field $B$ is $F = I_{\text{eddy}}\,l\,B$ (using the Lorentz force $d\mathbf{F}=I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$). Substituting $I_{\text{eddy}}$ gives $$F_{\text{brake}} = (B\,l\,v\,\sigma t)\,l\,B = \sigma t\,B^{2}\,l^{2}\,v.$$ Identifying $k = \sigma t\,B^{2}\,l^{2}$, we obtain the required linear relationship $F_{\text{brake}} = -k\,v$.

Solution to Practice Question 3: The deceleration $a$ produced by the magnetic brake follows Newton’s second law:

$$m\,a = -k\,v \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; a = -\frac{k}{m}\,v.$$ This is a first‑order differential equation: $\displaystyle \frac{dv}{dt}= -\frac{k}{m}v$. Integrating, $$v(t)=v_{0}\,e^{-\frac{k}{m}t},$$ where $v_{0}=80\,\text{m s}^{-1}$. The distance travelled until the train stops is $$s = \int_{0}^{\infty} v(t)\,dt = v_{0}\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{k}{m}t}\,dt = v_{0}\,\frac{m}{k}.$$ Substituting the numbers, $$s = 80\,\frac{2.5\times10^{5}}{1.2\times10^{4}} \approx 1.67\times10^{3}\,\text{m}.$$ Hence the train requires roughly $1.7\,$km to come to rest under continuous magnetic braking.

Solution to Practice Question 4: Advantage 1 – No Mechanical Wear: Magnetic brakes exert force without physical contact, eliminating wear of brake pads and discs. This reduces maintenance intervals and downtime, which is crucial for high‑frequency rail services. Advantage 2 – Regenerative Capability: The induced emf can be routed back to the train’s power system or the grid, converting kinetic energy into electrical energy. This improves overall energy efficiency, especially in routes with frequent deceleration and acceleration.

Case Study 2 – Photovoltaic Cells in Rural Electrification

Passage: In many remote villages of India, the government has promoted solar photovoltaic (PV) systems as a sustainable source of electricity. A typical rooftop PV installation consists of several silicon p‑n junction solar cells connected in series to achieve a required voltage. When sunlight of intensity $I_{\text{sun}}$ (in $\text{W m}^{-2}$) falls on a cell of area $A$, photons with energy $h\nu$ greater than the band gap $E_{g}$ generate electron‑hole pairs. The generated current density $J_{\text{ph}}$ is given by

$$J_{\text{ph}} = q\,\eta_{\text{abs}}\,\frac{I_{\text{sun}}}{h\nu},$$ where $q$ is the elementary charge and $\eta_{\text{abs}}$ is the absorption efficiency. The cell behaves like a current source in parallel with a diode. The I‑V characteristic of a solar cell under illumination is $$I = I_{\text{ph}} - I_{0}\!\left(e^{\frac{qV}{nkT}}-1\right),$$ where $I_{\text{ph}}$ is the photo‑generated current, $I_{0}$ the reverse saturation current, $n$ the ideality factor, $k$ Boltzmann’s constant and $T$ the absolute temperature. The maximum power point (MPP) occurs at voltage $V_{\text{m}}$ and current $I_{\text{m}}$, giving efficiency $\eta = \dfrac{V_{\text{m}}I_{\text{m}}}{I_{\text{sun}}A}$. In practice, a maximum‑power‑point‑tracking (MPPT) controller is used to keep the load at the MPP despite variations in sunlight and temperature.

  1. Practice Question 1: Derive the expression for the open‑circuit voltage $V_{\text{oc}}$ of a solar cell in terms of $I_{\text{ph}}$, $I_{0}$, $n$, $k$, $T$, and $q$.
  2. Practice Question 2: For a silicon cell with $I_{\text{ph}}=5\,\text{A}$, $I_{0}=1\times10^{-10}\,\text{A}$, $n=1$, $T=300\,\text{K}$, calculate $V_{\text{oc}}$.
  3. Practice Question 3: Explain how temperature affects both $I_{0}$ and $V_{\text{oc}}$, and consequently the overall efficiency of the PV module.
  4. Practice Question 4: A village installation consists of 20 cells in series, each of area $A=0.15\,\text{m}^{2}$, receiving $I_{\text{sun}}=800\,\text{W m}^{-2}$. If the measured MPP is $V_{\text{m}}=30\,\text{V}$ and $I_{\text{m}}=4.5\,\text{A}$, compute the overall efficiency $\eta$ of the system.
