Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Definition of Electromagnetic Induction

Electromagnetic induction is the process by which a changing magnetic flux through a closed conducting loop induces an electromotive force (emf) in the loop. The phenomenon was first discovered experimentally by Michael Faraday in 1831 and later mathematically formulated by James Clerk Maxwell. The induced emf, $ \mathcal{E} $, is given by Faraday’s law: $$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$ where $ \Phi_B = \int \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A} $ is the magnetic flux through the surface bounded by the circuit. The negative sign embodies Lenz’s law, indicating that the induced emf always acts to oppose the change in flux that produced it. This fundamental principle underlies the operation of generators, transformers, inductors, and many modern electronic devices. The direction of the induced current can be determined using the right‑hand rule for the magnetic field and the orientation of the loop, ensuring consistency with the conservation of energy.

Physics · Fundamental Law
Faraday’s Law and Its General Form

Faraday’s law can be expressed in two equivalent forms: the integral form and the differential form. The integral form, $$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A},$$ relates the line integral of the electric field $ \mathbf{E} $ around a closed path $ \partial S $ to the time rate of change of magnetic flux through the surface $ S $ bounded by that path. In the differential form, using Stokes’ theorem, we obtain $$\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}.$$ This equation shows that a time‑varying magnetic field produces a non‑conservative electric field, often called an induced electric field. Unlike electrostatic fields, the induced field cannot be expressed as the gradient of a scalar potential; instead, it forms closed loops that drive currents in conductors. The law is universal: it applies to any closed conducting circuit, irrespective of its shape, material, or motion, provided the magnetic flux through the circuit changes with time.

Physics · Lenz’s Law
Direction of Induced Current – Lenz’s Principle

Lenz’s law provides the qualitative direction of the induced emf. It states that the induced current will flow in such a direction that its own magnetic field opposes the original change in magnetic flux. Mathematically, this is captured by the negative sign in Faraday’s law. For a rectangular loop moving into a region of stronger magnetic field, the induced current will generate a magnetic field opposite to the external field, creating a repulsive force that resists the motion. Conversely, if the magnetic field through a stationary loop is decreasing, the induced current will produce a magnetic field in the same direction as the original field, attempting to maintain the flux. This opposition to change is a manifestation of the conservation of energy; any attempt to extract energy from the system without an external work input would violate the first law of thermodynamics.

Physics · Motion‑Induced emf
Motional emf in a Conducting Rod

When a straight conductor of length $ l $ moves with velocity $ \mathbf{v} $ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $ \mathbf{B} $, an emf is induced across its ends. The magnitude of this motional emf is given by $$\mathcal{E} = B l v \sin\theta,$$ where $ \theta $ is the angle between $ \mathbf{v} $ and $ \mathbf{B} $. This result can be derived from the Lorentz force on charge carriers: $ \mathbf{F}=q(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}) $. The charges experience a force that separates them, establishing a potential difference. In a closed circuit, such as a rod sliding on rails connected to a resistor, the induced current $ I $ follows Ohm’s law, $$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R} = \frac{B l v}{R},$$ where $ R $ is the total resistance of the circuit. The mechanical power supplied to keep the rod moving at constant speed, $$P_{\text{mech}} = \mathcal{E} I = \frac{(B l v)^2}{R},$$ is exactly equal to the electrical power dissipated in the resistor, confirming energy conservation. This principle is the basis of simple electric generators and rail‑gun concepts.

Physics · Self‑Induction & Mutual Induction
Inductance, Self‑Induced emf and Mutual Induction

A coil of $ N $ turns carrying a current $ I $ creates a magnetic flux $ \Phi = L I $ linking each turn, where $ L $ is the self‑inductance of the coil. The self‑induced emf opposes any change in current: $$\mathcal{E}_{\text{self}} = -L\frac{dI}{dt}.$$ The inductance depends on geometry, core material, and the number of turns; for a solenoid of length $ \ell $ and cross‑sectional area $ A $, $$L = \mu_0 \mu_r \frac{N^2 A}{\ell}.$$ Mutual induction occurs when a time‑varying current in a primary coil produces a changing flux in a secondary coil. The mutual inductance $ M $ relates the flux in the secondary to the current in the primary: $$\Phi_{21} = M I_1,\qquad \mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{dI_1}{dt}.$$ Reciprocity ensures $ M_{12}=M_{21}=M $. Transformers exploit this principle: an alternating voltage applied to the primary creates a varying flux that induces a voltage in the secondary. The voltage ratio follows the turn ratio, $$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{N_2}{N_1},$$ assuming ideal (lossless) conditions. Real transformers introduce leakage inductance, winding resistance, and core losses, which are accounted for in practical design. Understanding self‑ and mutual inductance is essential for analyzing circuits containing inductors, for designing filters, and for predicting transient behavior in RL and RLC networks.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

Electromagnetic induction forms the cornerstone of modern electrical technology. The CBSE Class 12 curriculum expects a clear, step‑by‑step derivation of each master equation, together with a concise formula table that lists symbols, SI units and the specific conditions under which each relation holds. The following exposition follows the textbook methodology, emphasizing physical insight, vector calculus where required, and the logical flow from Maxwell’s equations to the practical formulas used in laboratory and examination problems.

1. Faraday’s Law of Electromagnetic Induction

Consider a closed conducting loop C enclosing a surface S. The magnetic flux through the loop is defined as

$$\Phi = \int_{S} \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

where $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field (in tesla, T) and $d\mathbf{A}$ is an infinitesimal area vector pointing normal to the surface. According to Maxwell–Faraday equation, a time‑varying magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (emf) $\mathcal{E}$ around the loop:

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

The negative sign embodies Lenz’s law, indicating that the induced emf always opposes the change in flux. To derive this result from first principles, start with the integral form of Faraday’s law:

$$\oint_{C} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S} \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

Here $\mathbf{E}$ is the induced electric field, and $d\mathbf{l}$ is an element of the loop. Applying Stokes’ theorem to the left‑hand side gives

$$\int_{S} (\nabla\times\mathbf{E})\cdot d\mathbf{A} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S} \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$$

Since the surface S is arbitrary, the integrands must be equal, leading to the differential form

$$\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$$

Integrating the induced electric field around the loop yields the emf, which is precisely the line integral $\oint_{C}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$. Hence the macroscopic expression $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi/dt$ follows directly.

2. Lenz’s Law – Direction of Induced Current

Lenz’s law is not a separate equation but a qualitative rule that determines the sign of the induced emf. If the magnetic flux through the loop is increasing in a particular direction, the induced current will generate a magnetic field opposing that increase. Mathematically, the direction is encoded in the negative sign of Faraday’s law. In practice, one uses the right‑hand rule: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of the induced current; the thumb points along the induced magnetic field, which must oppose the change in the original flux.

3. Induced emf in a Moving Conductor (Motional emf)

When a straight conductor of length $l$ moves with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, free charges inside the conductor experience a magnetic Lorentz force $\mathbf{F}=q(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$. This force separates charges, establishing an electric field $\mathbf{E}$ inside the conductor until equilibrium is reached. The resulting potential difference (emf) between the ends of the conductor is

$$\mathcal{E}=Blv\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. Derivation: The magnetic force per unit charge is $|\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}| = vB\sin\theta$. Over a distance $l$, the work done per unit charge equals the emf, giving the expression above.

4. Self‑Induction and Self‑Inductance

When a time‑varying current $i(t)$ flows through a coil of $N$ turns, it creates a magnetic flux $\Phi$ linking each turn. The total flux linkage is $N\Phi$, and by definition of self‑inductance $L$:

$$N\Phi = Li$$

Thus the flux through a single turn is $\Phi = (L/N)i$. Differentiating with respect to time gives the induced emf (self‑induced emf):

$$\mathcal{E}_{\text{self}} = -\frac{d}{dt}(N\Phi) = -L\frac{di}{dt}$$

Derivation of $L$ for a solenoid: Consider a long solenoid of $n$ turns per unit length, cross‑sectional area $A$, and length $\ell$. The magnetic field inside is $B = \mu_0 n i$. The flux through one turn is $\Phi = BA = \mu_0 n i A$. With $N = n\ell$, the total flux linkage becomes $N\Phi = (\mu_0 n\ell)(\mu_0 n i A) = \mu_0 n^2 A \ell\, i$. Hence

$$L = \mu_0 n^2 A \ell = \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{\ell}$$

This expression is valid for an ideal solenoid (length $\gg$ diameter) and neglects edge effects.

5. Mutual Induction and Mutual Inductance

When two circuits, say coil 1 and coil 2, are placed such that the magnetic field produced by coil 1 links coil 2, a change in current $i_1$ in coil 1 induces an emf in coil 2. The mutual inductance $M$ is defined by

$$\mathcal{E}_2 = -M\frac{di_1}{dt}$$

Similarly, a change in $i_2$ induces $\mathcal{E}_1 = -M\,di_2/dt$. To derive $M$, consider coil 1 carrying current $i_1$ and producing magnetic field $\mathbf{B}_1$. The flux through each turn of coil 2 is $\Phi_{21} = \int_{S_2}\mathbf{B}_1\cdot d\mathbf{A}$. The total flux linkage is $N_2\Phi_{21}$, and by definition $N_2\Phi_{21}=Mi_1$. For coaxial solenoids of radii $a$ and $b$ ($a $$M = \mu_0 \frac{N_1 N_2 \pi a^2}{\ell}$$

where $\ell$ is the common length, assuming the magnetic field of the inner solenoid is uniform across the cross‑section of the outer solenoid.

6. Energy Stored in an Inductor

The work done in establishing a current $i$ in an inductor of inductance $L$ is stored as magnetic energy $U$. Starting from the power supplied $P = \mathcal{E}i = L i \, di/dt$, integrate from $0$ to $i$:

$$U = \int_{0}^{i} L i'\, di' = \frac{1}{2} L i^{2}$$

Alternatively, using flux linkage $\lambda = Li$, the energy can be expressed as $U = \frac{1}{2}\lambda i$. For a solenoid, substituting $L = \mu_0 N^{2}A/\ell$ yields

$$U = \frac{1}{2}\mu_0 \frac{N^{2}A}{\ell} i^{2} = \frac{B^{2}}{2\mu_0} \, (\text{volume})$$

which shows that the magnetic energy density is $u = B^{2}/(2\mu_0)$, a result that is consistent with the general electromagnetic energy density formula.

7. Induced emf in a Rotating Loop (Alternating Current Generator)

Consider a rectangular loop of area $A$ rotating with angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, the normal to the loop making an angle $\theta = \omega t$ with $\mathbf{B}$. The instantaneous flux is

$$\Phi(t) = BA\cos(\omega t)$$

Applying Faraday’s law:

$$\mathcal{E}(t) = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = BA\omega\sin(\omega t)$$

Thus the induced emf is sinusoidal, with peak value $\mathcal{E}_{\max}=BA\omega$. This is the fundamental principle behind AC generators.

8. Eddy Currents and Their Damping Effect

When a conducting plate moves through a magnetic field, circulating currents (eddy currents) are induced. The induced emf in a small rectangular element $dx\,dy$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is $d\mathcal{E}=Bv\,dx$. The resulting current density $J = \sigma E$ (where $\sigma$ is conductivity) produces a magnetic force $\mathbf{F} = J \times \mathbf{B}$ opposing the motion. The net retarding force per unit area is $f = \sigma B^{2} v$, leading to a damping term proportional to velocity. This phenomenon is exploited in electromagnetic brakes.
