Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Electric Current and Ohm's Law

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. It is quantified by the relation $$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$ where $I$ is the current in amperes, $Q$ is the charge in coulombs, and $t$ is the time in seconds. In a metallic conductor, electrons drift under the influence of an electric field, and the drift velocity is typically very small compared to the thermal velocity of the electrons. The electric potential difference, or voltage, across a conductor is related to the current by Ohm’s law, which states that for a homogeneous conductor the voltage is directly proportional to the current: $$V = IR$$. Here $V$ is the voltage in volts, $I$ is the current in amperes, and $R$ is the resistance in ohms. The resistance of a uniform conductor is given by $$R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$$ where $\rho$ is the resistivity of the material, $l$ is the length, and $A$ is the cross‑sectional area. Resistivity is an intrinsic property of the material and varies with temperature; for most metals it increases linearly with temperature. The reciprocal of resistivity is conductivity, $\sigma = 1/\rho$, which measures how easily charge carriers can move through the material. In practice, Ohm’s law holds for many conductors over a wide range of voltages, but deviations occur in non‑ohmic devices such as diodes, transistors, and in situations where the temperature changes significantly during operation.

**Worked Example:** Consider a copper wire of length $l = 2.0\,\text{m}$, cross‑sectional area $A = 1.0\times10^{-6}\,\text{m}^2$, and resistivity $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\,\Omega\text{m}$. The resistance is $$R = \rho \frac{l}{A} = 1.68\times10^{-8}\,\frac{2.0}{1.0\times10^{-6}} \approx 0.0336\,\Omega.$$ If a potential difference of $V = 1.0\,\text{V}$ is applied, the current flowing through the wire is $$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{1.0}{0.0336} \approx 29.8\,\text{A}.$$ This example illustrates how the dimensions of the conductor and its material properties determine the current for a given applied voltage. The large current is a consequence of the very low resistance of copper and the small length of the wire.

Physics · Core Law
Kirchhoff’s Laws and Equivalent Resistance

Kirchhoff’s circuit laws are fundamental tools for analyzing complex electrical networks. The first law, known as Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL), states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving it: $$\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$$. This is a direct consequence of charge conservation. The second law, Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL), asserts that the algebraic sum of potential differences around any closed loop in a network is zero: $$\sum V = 0$$. This follows from the conservation of energy for a charge moving in a conservative electric field. These laws allow the systematic construction of simultaneous equations for unknown currents and voltages in any circuit, regardless of its complexity.

When resistors are connected in series, their resistances add: $$R_{\text{eq,series}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_n$$. In a parallel arrangement, the reciprocal of the equivalent resistance is the sum of reciprocals: $$\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq,parallel}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}$$. These formulas are derived directly from KVL and KCL by considering the potential drops and current splits at junctions.

**Worked Example:** Three resistors of $4\,\Omega$, $6\,\Omega$, and $12\,\Omega$ are connected in parallel and a $12\,\text{V}$ battery is applied across the network. The equivalent resistance is $$R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{12}} = \frac{1}{0.25 + 0.1667 + 0.0833} = 2\,\Omega.$$ The total current supplied by the battery is $$I_{\text{total}} = \frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{12}{2} = 6\,\text{A}.$$ Using KCL, the current through each resistor can be found: $$I_4 = \frac{12}{4} = 3\,\text{A},\quad I_6 = \frac{12}{6} = 2\,\text{A},\quad I_{12} = \frac{12}{12} = 1\,\text{A}.$$ The sum of these currents equals the total current, confirming KCL. This example demonstrates how Kirchhoff’s laws provide a systematic approach to solving for currents and voltages in networks with multiple branches.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In the CBSE Class 12 syllabus, the chapter “Current Electricity” is built around a few fundamental concepts that link the microscopic motion of charge carriers to the macroscopic quantities measured in a circuit. The derivations presented below follow the standard CBSE methodology, i.e., they start from basic definitions, invoke appropriate physical assumptions, and finally arrive at the compact formulae that appear in the textbook. Each derivation is broken into clear logical steps, and every symbol introduced is later listed in the formula table with its SI unit and the condition under which the formula holds.

1. Drift Velocity and Current Density

Consider a metallic conductor of cross‑sectional area $A$ containing $n$ free electrons per unit volume. When an electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is applied, each electron acquires a small average velocity in the direction opposite to $\mathbf{E}$, called the drift velocity $\mathbf{v}_d$. The magnitude of the drift velocity is given by

$$\mathbf{v}_d = \mu \mathbf{E}$$

where $\mu$ is the electron mobility (units: $\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$). The current density $\mathbf{J}$, defined as the charge crossing a unit area per unit time, can be expressed as

$$\mathbf{J} = -n e \mathbf{v}_d$$

Substituting the expression for $\mathbf{v}_d$ gives

$$\mathbf{J} = -n e \mu \mathbf{E}$$

Since the direction of conventional current is opposite to the electron flow, we drop the negative sign and write

$$\mathbf{J} = n e \mu \mathbf{E}$$

This relation is the microscopic form of Ohm’s law and introduces the conductivity $\sigma$ as

$$\sigma = n e \mu \qquad\text{(S m$^{-1}$)}$$

Thus, $\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$, which is the first fundamental equation of current electricity.

2. Macroscopic Ohm’s Law and Resistance

For a uniform cylindrical conductor of length $L$ and cross‑section $A$, the electric field inside the conductor is uniform and related to the potential difference $V$ across its ends by

$$E = \frac{V}{L}$$

The total current $I$ flowing through the conductor is obtained by integrating the current density over the cross‑section:

$$I = \int_A \mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = J A = \sigma E A$$

Substituting $E = V/L$ yields

$$I = \sigma \frac{V}{L} A$$

Rearranging, we obtain the familiar linear relation

$$V = I \underbrace{\frac{L}{\sigma A}}_{R}$$

The quantity in the denominator is defined as the resistance $R$ of the conductor:

$$R = \frac{L}{\sigma A}$$

Since conductivity $\sigma$ is the reciprocal of resistivity $\rho$, i.e., $\sigma = 1/\rho$, we can rewrite the resistance as

$$\boxed{R = \rho \frac{L}{A}}$$

This is the standard resistance formula used throughout the CBSE curriculum. It shows that resistance is directly proportional to the length of the conductor, inversely proportional to its cross‑sectional area, and depends on the material through $\rho$.

3. Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

For most metallic conductors, resistivity varies linearly with temperature over a moderate range. The empirical relation is

$$\rho(T) = \rho_0 \bigl[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\bigr]$$

where $\rho_0$ is the resistivity at a reference temperature $T_0$ (usually $20^\circ\text{C}$), and $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistivity (units: $\text{K}^{-1}$). Substituting this into the resistance formula gives

$$R(T) = R_0 \bigl[1 + \alpha (T - T_0)\bigr]$$

with $R_0 = \rho_0 L/A$ being the resistance at $T_0$. This expression is essential for solving problems involving heating or cooling of conductors.

4. Power Dissipation (Joule’s Law)

The electrical power $P$ delivered to a resistor is the rate at which energy is converted into heat. Starting from the definition of power as the product of potential difference and current, we have

$$P = VI$$

Using Ohm’s law $V = IR$, we obtain two alternative forms:

$$P = I^2 R \qquad\text{and}\qquad P = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

These three expressions constitute Joule’s law. They are particularly useful when either current, voltage, or resistance is known, allowing the other quantities to be computed directly.

5. Derivation of Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

KVL states that the algebraic sum of potential differences around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. The derivation rests on the principle of conservation of energy. Consider a closed loop consisting of $n$ elements, each having a potential difference $V_k$ (positive if the element is traversed from higher to lower potential). The work done by the electric field in moving a test charge $q$ around the loop is

$$W = q\sum_{k=1}^{n} V_k$$

Since the charge returns to its starting point, its net change in potential energy must be zero, implying $W = 0$. Dividing by $q$ gives

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} V_k = 0$$

This is the mathematical statement of KVL. In circuit analysis, each $V_k$ may be expressed using Ohm’s law ($V_k = I_k R_k$) or as a source voltage, leading to a set of linear equations that can be solved simultaneously.

6. Derivation of Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)

KCL asserts that the algebraic sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving it. The law follows directly from the continuity equation for electric charge. Let a junction have $m$ incoming currents $I_{\text{in},i}$ and $n$ outgoing currents $I_{\text{out},j}$. The net charge accumulation $\Delta Q$ at the junction over a small time interval $\Delta t$ is

$$\Delta Q = \biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{m} I_{\text{in},i} - \sum_{j=1}^{n} I_{\text{out},j}\biggr)\Delta t$$

For a steady‑state circuit, charge does not build up at any point, so $\Delta Q = 0$. Dividing by $\Delta t$ yields

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m} I_{\text{in},i} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} I_{\text{out},j}$$

This is the formal statement of KCL, which is used to write current equations at each node of a network.

7. Wheatstone Bridge Balance Condition

A Wheatstone bridge consists of four resistors $R_1$, $R_2$, $R_3$, and $R_4$ forming a diamond shape, with a galvanometer connected across the two opposite nodes. When the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the galvanometer. Applying KVL to the two loops that share the galvanometer gives

$$I_1 R_1 = I_2 R_2 \quad\text{and}\quad I_1 R_3 = I_2 R_4$$

Since the same current $I_1$ flows through $R_1$ and $R_3$, and $I_2$ flows through $R_2$ and $R_4$, dividing the two equations eliminates the currents and yields the balance condition

$$\boxed{\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}}$$

This relation is used to determine an unknown resistance by adjusting the known resistances until the galvanometer reads zero.

8. Derivation of the Formula for Equivalent Resistance in Series and Parallel

Series Combination: When $n$ resistors $R_1, R_2, \dots, R_n$ are connected end‑to‑end, the same current $I$ flows through each resistor. The total voltage across the combination is the sum of individual voltage drops:

$$V_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} I R_k = I \sum_{k=1}^{n} R_k$$

Hence the equivalent resistance $R_{\text{eq}}$ satisfies $V_{\text{total}} = I R_{\text{eq}}$, giving

$$\boxed{R_{\text{eq}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} R_k}$$

Parallel Combination: For $n$ resistors connected in parallel, the voltage across each resistor is the same, equal to $V$. The total current supplied by the source is the sum of the branch currents:

$$I_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{V}{R_k} = V \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{R_k}$$

Defining $R_{\text{eq}}$ by $I_{\text{total}} = V / R_{\text{eq}}$, we obtain

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{R_k}}$$

These two formulas are the backbone of any circuit simplification problem in the CBSE exam.
