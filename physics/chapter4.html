1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

The study of moving charges and magnetism in Class 12 Physics is grounded in the interplay between electric and magnetic fields. The foundational concept is that a moving electric charge constitutes a current, and a current generates a magnetic field. This relationship is encapsulated in Ampère’s law and the Biot–Savart law, which provide the quantitative tools to calculate magnetic fields produced by steady currents. The Lorentz force law, $ \mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$, unifies the action of electric and magnetic fields on a moving charge, revealing that magnetic forces are perpendicular to the velocity of the charge and do no work. The magnetic field is a vector field described by the right‑hand rule: if the thumb points in the direction of current, the curled fingers indicate the direction of the magnetic field lines. The magnitude of the magnetic field due to a long straight conductor is given by $ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} $, where $ \mu_0 $ is the permeability of free space, $ I $ is the current, and $ r $ is the perpendicular distance from the wire. The concept of magnetic flux, $ \Phi_B = \int \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A} $, is central to Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction, which states that a changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (emf) in a circuit: $ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} $. The negative sign reflects Lenz’s law, indicating that the induced emf opposes the change in flux. These principles form the backbone of electromagnetism, linking motion, charge, and magnetic phenomena in a coherent framework.

Physics · Core Law
Magnetic Field of a Current Loop and Solenoid

A circular current loop of radius $ a $ carrying current $ I $ produces a magnetic field along its axis given by $ B(z) = \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}} $. At the centre of the loop ($ z = 0 $), this simplifies to $ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a} $. For a solenoid of $ n $ turns per unit length, the magnetic field inside the solenoid is approximately uniform and given by $ B = \mu_0 n I $. The field outside a long solenoid is negligible, illustrating the shielding effect of tightly wound coils. The magnetic field of a solenoid can be derived by superposing the contributions of infinitesimal current elements using the Biot–Savart law or by applying Ampère’s law to a rectangular Amperian loop that encloses the solenoid. The uniformity of the field inside a solenoid is crucial for applications such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and particle accelerators, where precise control of magnetic forces is required. The concept of magnetic field lines, which are continuous and do not begin or end, provides an intuitive visual representation of the field distribution around coils and magnets.

Physics · Core Law
Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday’s Law

Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction is a cornerstone of modern electrical engineering. It states that the induced emf in a closed loop is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop: $ \mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} $, where $ N $ is the number of turns. The negative sign embodies Lenz’s law, which ensures conservation of energy by producing a current that opposes the change in flux. In practical terms, this principle underlies transformers, electric generators, and inductors. A transformer consists of two windings (primary and secondary) wound on a common magnetic core; the changing current in the primary winding produces a time‑varying flux that induces an emf in the secondary winding. The ratio of the induced emf in the secondary to that in the primary equals the ratio of the number of turns: $ \frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} $. In generators, mechanical rotation of a coil in a magnetic field converts mechanical work into electrical energy, with the induced emf given by $ \mathcal{E} = B l v $, where $ l $ is the length of the conductor and $ v $ is its velocity relative to the magnetic field. Inductors store magnetic energy in the field created by a current; the inductance $ L $ of a coil is defined by $ \Phi_B = L I $. The energy stored is $ U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2 $. Understanding the interplay between changing magnetic fields and induced currents is essential for designing efficient power transmission systems and for mitigating electromagnetic interference in electronic circuits.

Physics · Core Law
Magnetic Forces on Moving Charges and Currents

The magnetic force on a moving charge is given by the Lorentz force law: $ \mathbf{F}_m = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} $. This force is always perpendicular to both the velocity of the charge and the magnetic field, implying that magnetic fields do not perform work on moving charges. For a straight conductor of length $ l $ carrying current $ I $ in a uniform magnetic field $ \mathbf{B} $, the magnetic force is $ \mathbf{F} = I\,\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B} $. The magnitude is $ F = I l B \sin\theta $, where $ \theta $ is the angle between the conductor and the magnetic field. This principle is exploited in electric motors, where the interaction between current‑carrying conductors and magnetic fields produces torque. The torque on a current loop of area $ A $ in a magnetic field is $ \tau = I A B \sin\phi $, with $ \phi $ being the angle between the normal to the loop and the field. In magnetic levitation (maglev) trains, magnetic forces are used to counteract gravitational forces, enabling frictionless motion. The concept of magnetic pressure, $ P_m = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} $, describes the force exerted by a magnetic field on a surface, which is relevant in plasma confinement devices such as tokamaks. The magnetic force law also explains the deflection of charged particles in magnetic spectrometers, where the radius of curvature of a particle’s trajectory is $ r = \frac{mv}{qB} $. These applications demonstrate how the magnetic force law translates microscopic charge dynamics into macroscopic mechanical effects.

Physics · Core Law
Magnetic Materials and Magnetization

Magnetic materials are classified into diamagnetic, paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, and antiferromagnetic categories based on their response to external magnetic fields. Diamagnetic materials exhibit a weak, negative susceptibility $ \chi < 0 $ and generate a magnetic field opposing the applied field. Paramagnetic materials have a small positive susceptibility $ \chi > 0 $ and align weakly with the field. Ferromagnetic materials, such as iron, cobalt, and nickel, possess a large positive susceptibility and exhibit spontaneous magnetization due to exchange interactions between neighboring electron spins. The magnetization $ \mathbf{M} $ of a material is defined as the magnetic moment per unit volume, and the magnetic field inside a material is related to the applied field by $ \mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H} + \mathbf{M}) $. In ferromagnets, the relationship between $ \mathbf{B} $ and $ \mathbf{H} $ is nonlinear, leading to hysteresis loops that describe the magnetization history of the material. The coercive field $ H_c $ and remanent magnetization $ M_r $ are key parameters in characterizing magnetic memory. Antiferromagnets, on the other hand, have adjacent spins aligned antiparallel, resulting in zero net magnetization in the absence of an external field. Understanding the magnetic properties of materials is essential for designing permanent magnets, magnetic recording media, and magnetic shielding devices. The study of magnetization also provides insight into fundamental quantum mechanical interactions, such as spin–orbit coupling and exchange energy, which govern the macroscopic magnetic behavior of solids.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this section we present the complete set of derivations that form the backbone of the “Moving Charges & Magnetism’’ chapter of the CBSE Class 12 Physics syllabus. Each derivation follows the standard CBSE methodology: we start from fundamental postulates, invoke symmetry arguments wherever appropriate, and finally arrive at the compact analytical expressions that are used in problem‑solving. The derivations are written in a step‑by‑step manner so that a student can follow the logical flow without any gaps. All mathematical expressions are presented in LaTeX format for clarity.

1. Lorentz Force on a Moving Charge

The most elementary interaction between a moving charge and a magnetic field is encapsulated in the Lorentz force law. Starting from the experimental observation that a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ experiences a force perpendicular both to its velocity and to the magnetic field $\vec{B}$, we write the vector form of the force as

$$\vec{F}=q\left(\vec{E}+\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\right)$$

When only a magnetic field is present ($\vec{E}=0$), the expression reduces to the magnetic part alone:

$$\vec{F}_{\!B}=q\,\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$$

To verify the direction, we apply the right‑hand rule: point the fingers in the direction of $\vec{v}$, curl them towards $\vec{B}$, and the thumb points in the direction of $\vec{F}_{\!B}$. The magnitude follows from the definition of the cross product:

$$F_{B}=qvB\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\vec{B}$. This result is the cornerstone for all subsequent magnetic force calculations.

2. Magnetic Field Due to a Straight Long Conductor (Biot–Savart Law)

The Biot–Savart law provides the magnetic field produced at a point $P$ by an infinitesimal element $d\vec{l}$ of a current‑carrying conductor. The law is derived from the experimental observation that the field is directly proportional to the current $I$, the length element $dl$, and the sine of the angle between $d\vec{l}$ and the line joining $dl$ to $P$, and inversely proportional to the square of the distance $r$ from $dl$ to $P$.

$$d\vec{B}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}}{r^{2}}$$

Integrating this expression over an infinitely long straight wire gives the well‑known result:

$$\vec{B}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi r}\,\hat{\phi}$$

Here $\hat{\phi}$ is the azimuthal unit vector that circles the wire according to the right‑hand grip rule. The derivation proceeds by choosing a coordinate system where the wire lies along the $z$‑axis, expressing $d\vec{l}=dz\,\hat{k}$, and evaluating the integral

$$\vec{B}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz\,\hat{k}\times\vec{r}}{r^{3}}$$

where $\vec{r}=r\hat{r}+z\hat{k}$ and $r$ is the perpendicular distance from the wire to point $P$. Performing the cross‑product and integrating yields the final expression shown above.

3. Magnetic Field at the Centre of a Circular Loop

Consider a circular loop of radius $R$ carrying a steady current $I$. By symmetry, every infinitesimal element $d\vec{l}$ contributes a magnetic field $d\vec{B}$ that points along the axis of the loop. Using the Biot–Savart law for a single element and integrating over the full $2\pi$ radians, we obtain

$$\vec{B}_{\text{centre}}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2R}\,\hat{n}$$

where $\hat{n}$ is the unit vector normal to the plane of the loop, determined by the right‑hand rule. The derivation involves noting that $r=R$ for every element, $\sin\theta=1$ because $d\vec{l}$ is always perpendicular to the radius vector, and the integral simplifies to

$$B_{\text{centre}}=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4\pi R^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}R\,d\phi=\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2R}.$$

4. Magnetic Field on the Axis of a Circular Loop

When the point of observation lies on the axis at a distance $x$ from the centre, the contribution from each element $d\vec{l}$ has a component along the axis and a component perpendicular to it. The perpendicular components cancel by symmetry, leaving only the axial component. Using the Biot–Savart law, the axial field is

$$B_{\text{axis}}=\frac{\mu_{0}I R^{2}}{2\left(R^{2}+x^{2}\right)^{3/2}}.$$

The derivation proceeds by writing the distance from $d\vec{l}$ to the point $P$ as $r=\sqrt{R^{2}+x^{2}}$, and the angle between $d\vec{l}$ and $\vec{r}$ satisfies $\sin\theta=R/r$. Substituting these into the Biot–Savart expression and integrating over $0$ to $2\pi$ yields the above compact formula.

5. Force Between Two Parallel Current‑Carrying Conductors

Let two long, straight, parallel conductors be separated by a distance $d$, carrying currents $I_{1}$ and $I_{2}$ respectively. The magnetic field produced by the first conductor at the location of the second is

$$B_{1}=\frac{\mu_{0}I_{1}}{2\pi d}.$$

The second conductor experiences a magnetic force per unit length given by $F/L=I_{2}B_{1}$. Substituting $B_{1}$, we obtain the celebrated Ampère’s force law:

$$\frac{F}{L}=\frac{\mu_{0}I_{1}I_{2}}{2\pi d}.$$

The direction of the force follows from the right‑hand rule: if the currents flow in the same direction the force is attractive, otherwise it is repulsive. This result is fundamental for the definition of the ampere in the SI system.

6. Torque on a Current Loop in a Uniform Magnetic Field

A rectangular (or any planar) loop of area $A$, carrying current $I$, placed in a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}$ experiences a torque that tends to align the magnetic dipole moment $\vec{\mu}=I\vec{A}$ with the field. The torque magnitude is derived by summing the forces on opposite sides of the loop. The result is

$$\tau = IAB\sin\theta = \mu B\sin\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{\mu}$ and $\vec{B}$. In vector form, the torque is expressed as

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times\vec{B}.$$

The derivation explicitly uses the fact that the forces on the sides parallel to $\vec{B}$ cancel, while the forces on the sides perpendicular to $\vec{B}$ produce a couple of magnitude $I B L$ separated by a distance $w$, giving a torque $I B L w = IAB\sin\theta$.

7. Magnetic Dipole Moment of a Current Loop

The magnetic dipole moment $\vec{\mu}$ of a planar loop is defined as the product of the current and the vector area:

$$\vec{\mu}=I\vec{A}=I A\,\hat{n},$$

where $\hat{n}$ is a unit vector normal to the plane of the loop, chosen according to the right‑hand rule (curl fingers in the direction of current, thumb points along $\hat{n}$). This definition is consistent with the torque expression derived above and provides a bridge to the quantum mechanical notion of magnetic moments.

8. Motion of a Charged Particle in a Uniform Magnetic Field

When a charged particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ enters a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}$ with a velocity $\vec{v}$ perpendicular to $\vec{B}$, it experiences a centripetal magnetic force $F_{B}=qvB$. Equating this to the required centripetal force $m v^{2}/r$, we obtain the radius of the circular trajectory:

$$r=\frac{mv}{|q|B}.$$

The angular frequency (cyclotron frequency) of the motion is

$$\omega=\frac{|q|B}{m},$$

and the period of revolution is $T=2\pi/\omega = 2\pi m/(|q|B)$. If the velocity makes an angle $\alpha$ with $\vec{B}$, the motion becomes helical: the component $v_{\parallel}=v\cos\alpha$ remains unchanged, while the perpendicular component $v_{\perp}=v\sin\alpha$ produces the circular motion described above. The pitch of the helix (distance advanced along $\vec{B}$ in one complete turn) is $p=v_{\parallel}T$.

9. Ampère’s Circuital Law (Integral Form)

Ampère’s law relates the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop to the net current enclosed by the loop. Starting from the experimental observation that the magnetic field lines form closed loops, we write

$$\oint\limits_{\mathcal{C}}\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l}= \mu_{0}I_{\text{enc}}.$$

For highly symmetric situations (infinite straight wire, solenoid, toroid), the law enables direct calculation of $\vec{B}$ without invoking the Biot–Savart integral. The derivation for a long solenoid, for example, assumes a uniform field inside and negligible field outside, leading to

$$B_{\text{sol}}=\mu_{0}nI,$$

where $n$ is the number of turns per unit length.
