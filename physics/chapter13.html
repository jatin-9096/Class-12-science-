Radioactivity and its types

Radioactivity – spontaneous emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation from an unstable nucleus.

Key decay law:

$$N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$$

where $N$ = number of undecayed nuclei at time $t$, $N_0$ = initial number, $\lambda$ = decay constant (s$^{-1}$).

Activity $A = \lambda N$ (unit: becquerel, $1\ \text{Bq}=1\ \text{s}^{-1}$).

Half‑life $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$.

Beta$\beta^-$$-e$$\approx 0$Medium$5\ \text{mm}$ Al
\Gamma$\gamma$Neutral$0$High$5\ \text{cm}$ Pb

Example: A sample contains $N_0 = 2\times10^{20}$ nuclei of a radionuclide with $\lambda = 2\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find the number of nuclei \left after $t = 5\times10^{2}$ s and the activity at that instant.

Solution:

  • Decay law: $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}=2\times10^{20} e^{-(2\times10^{-3})(5\times10^{2})}=2\times10^{20} e^{-1}=2\times10^{20}\times0.368=7.36\times10^{19}$.
  • Activity: $A = \lambda N = (2\times10^{-3})(7.36\times10^{19}) = 1.47\times10^{17}\ \text{s}^{-1}=1.47\times10^{17}\ \text{Bq}$.

Nuclear decay law and decay constant

Definition: Nuclear decay law states that the probability of a nucleus decaying in a small time interval $dt$ is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei $N$ present at that instant.

Mathematically, $$\\frac{dN}{dt} = -\\lambda N$$ where $\\lambda$ is the decay constant (SI unit s$^{-1}$). Integrating gives $$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\\lambda t}$$ and the activity $A = -\\frac{dN}{dt}=\\lambda N$ (unit Bq).

  • Half‑life ($T_{1/2}$): time required for $N$ \to reduce \to $\\frac{N_0}{2}$. From the decay law, $$T_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{\\lambda}$$.
  • Mean life ($\\tau$): average lifetime of a nucleus, $$\\tau = \\frac{1}{\\lambda}$$.
QuantitySymbolExpressionSI Unit
Decay constant$\\lambda$$$\\lambda = \\frac{\\ln 2}{T_{1/2}}$$s$^{-1}$
Half‑life$T_{1/2}$$$T_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{\\lambda}$$s
Activity$A$$$A = \\lambda N$$Bq

Example: A sample contains $5.0\\times10^{20}$ $^{60}$Co nuclei. Its half‑life is $5.27$ years. Find the activity after $3$ years.

Solution:

  1. Convert half‑life \to seconds: $T_{1/2}=5.27\\times365\\times24\\times3600\\approx1.66\\times10^{8}\\,\\text{s}$.
  2. Decay constant $\\lambda = \\frac{\\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \\frac{0.693}{1.66\\times10^{8}} = 4.18\\times10^{-9}\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$.
  3. Number of nuclei after $t=3\\,\\text{yr}=9.46\\times10^{7}\\,\\text{s}$: $N = N_0 e^{-\\lambda t}=5.0\\times10^{20} e^{-4.18\\times10^{-9}\\times9.46\\times10^{7}} \\approx 3.0\\times10^{20}$.
  4. Activity $A = \\lambda N = 4.18\\times10^{-9}\\times3.0\\times10^{20}=1.25\\times10^{12}\\,\\text{Bq}$.

Thus the sample’s activity after 3 years is $1.3\\times10^{12}$ Bq (≈ $1.3$ TBq).

Half‑life, mean life and activity

Half‑life (T_{1/2}) – time required for the activity or number of radioactive nuclei to reduce to half of its initial value.

Mean life (\tau) – average lifetime of a nucleus; \(\tau = \frac{1}{\lambda}\) where \(\lambda\) is the decay constant.

Activity (A) – number of decays per unit time, $A = -\\frac{dN}{dt}=\\lambda N$ (SI unit: becquerel, Bq = s^{-1}).

Decay law – the number of undecayed nuclei at time \(t\) is $N = N_{0}\\,e^{-\\lambda t}$.

QuantityFormulaSI Unit
Decay constant$\\lambda$s^{-1}
Half‑life$T_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{\\lambda}$s
Mean life$\\tau = \\frac{1}{\\lambda}=\\frac{T_{1/2}}{\\ln 2}$s
Activity$A = \\lambda N$Bq

Derivation of half‑life: Set \(N = N_{0}/2\) in the decay law:
$\frac{1}{2}=e^{-\\lambda T_{1/2}} \Rightarrow T_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{\\lambda}$.

Example:

  • Given \(\\lambda = 0.693\times10^{-3}\,s^{-1}\) and initial nuclei \(N_{0}=2.0\times10^{6}\).
  • Half‑life: $T_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{0.693\times10^{-3}}=10^{3}\,s=16.67\,\text{min}$.
  • Initial activity: $A_{0}=\\lambda N_{0}=0.693\times10^{-3}\times2.0\times10^{6}=1.386\times10^{3}\,s^{-1}=1.386\,\text{kBq}$.
  • After \(t=10\,\text{min}=600\,s\):
    $N= N_{0}e^{-\\lambda t}=2.0\times10^{6}e^{-0.693\times10^{-3}\times600}=2.0\times10^{6}e^{-0.4158}\approx1.32\times10^{6}$.
    $A=\\lambda N=0.693\times10^{-3}\times1.32\times10^{6}\approx9.15\times10^{2}\,s^{-1}=0.915\,\text{kBq}$.

Thus the half‑life, mean life and activity are inter‑related through the decay constant, enabling quick calculations in nuclear problems.

Mass defect, binding energy and nuclear stability

Mass Defect (\Delta m): Difference between the sum of individual nucleon masses and the actual mass of the nucleus.

\Delta m = $\\sum m_{p}+\\sum m_{n} - m_{nucleus}$ (kg)

Binding Energy (B): Energy required to separate a nucleus into its constituent nucleons.

$$B = \\Delta m\\,c^{2}$$ where $c = 3\\times10^{8}\\,\text{m s}^{-1}$.

SI unit: joule (J). Often expressed \in MeV: $1\\,\text{u}=931.5\\,\text{MeV}$.

Binding Energy per Nucleon (B/A): $\\frac{B}{A}$, indicates nuclear stability; peaks near $^{56}\text{Fe}$.

QuantityFormulaUnit
Mass defect$\\Delta m = \\sum m_{p}+\\sum m_{n} - m_{nucleus}$kg
Binding energy$B = \\Delta m\\,c^{2}$J (or MeV)
Binding energy per nucleon$\\frac{B}{A}$MeV

Example: Calculate the binding energy of $^{4}\text{He}$.

Given: $m_{p}=1.007276\,\text{u}$, $m_{n}=1.008665\,\text{u}$, $m_{\alpha}=4.002603\,\text{u}$.

\Delta m = $2\\times1.007276 + 2\\times1.008665 - 4.002603 = 0.030379\,\text{u}$.

Energy: $B = 0.030379\\times931.5 = 28.30\,\text{MeV}$.

Thus $B/A = 28.30/4 = 7.07\,\text{MeV}$, showing high stability.

Nuclear fission and its energetics

Definition: Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two (or more) lighter fragments accompanied by the release of neutrons and a large amount of energy.

  • Typical reaction: $\\,^{235}_{92}\\text{U}+n \\rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\\text{Ba}+^{92}_{36}\\text{Kr}+3n+\\text{energy}$
  • Energy originates from conversion of mass defect $\\Delta m$ to energy via Einstein’s relation $E=\\Delta m c^2$.

Key Formulas

QuantityFormulaSI Unit
Mass defect$\\Delta m = \\sum m_{\\text{reactants}}-\\sum m_{\\text{products}}$kg
Energy released$E = \\Delta m\\,c^2$J
Energy per fission$E_f = \\frac{E}{N_A}\\,\\frac{1}{A}$ (or simply $\\approx 200\\,\\text{MeV}$ for $^{235}$U)J or MeV
Power from 1 g U‑235$P = \\frac{E_f \\times N}{t}$W

Derivation of $E_f$: For a single fission event, $\\Delta m$ is obtained from atomic masses. Substituting $c = 3\\times10^8\\,\\text{m s}^{-1}$ gives $E = \\Delta m c^2$. For $^{235}$U, $\\Delta m \\approx 0.200\\,\\text{u}$, thus $E_f \\approx 0.200\\times931.5\\,\\text{MeV} \\approx 200\\,\\text{MeV}$.

Example (Energy from 1 g $^{235}$U):

  1. Number of nuclei in 1 g: $N = \\frac{1\\,\\text{g}}{235\\,\\text{g mol}^{-1}}\\times N_A \\approx 2.56\\times10^{21}$.
  2. Energy per nucleus $= 200\\,\\text{MeV}= 200\\times1.602\\times10^{-13}\\,\\text{J}=3.20\\times10^{-11}\\,\\text{J}$.
  3. Total energy $E_{\\text{tot}} = N\\times 3.20\\times10^{-11}\\,\\text{J} \\approx 8.2\\times10^{10}\\,\\text{J}$ (≈ 22 MWh).

Thus a gram of $^{235}$U can power a typical household for several months, illustrating the enormous energy density of fission.

Nuclear fusion and its conditions

Definition: Nuclear fusion is the process in which two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy because the total binding energy of the products exceeds that of the reactants.

Key conditions to overcome the Coulomb barrier

  • Coulomb barrier energy: $\\frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{4\\pi\\epsilon_0 r}$ (SI unit: joule)
  • Temperature: $T \ge 10^8\,\text{K}$ to give nuclei kinetic energy $\approx \frac{3}{2}kT$ comparable to $E_C$.
  • Pressure / density: $n \ge 10^{20}\,\text{m}^{-3}$ to increase collision probability.
  • Confinement time ($\tau$): must satisfy the Lawson criterion $n\tau \ge 10^{14}\,\text{cm}^{-3}\,\text{s}$ for D‑T fuel.

Lawson criterion (general form)

$$n\tau \ge \frac{12kT}{\langle \sigma v \rangle E_{fusion}}$$ where $\langle \sigma v \rangle$ is the reactivity (m$^3$ s$^{-1}$) and $E_{fusion}$ is the energy released per reaction (J).

Typical fusion parameters (D‑T reaction)

ParameterRequired value
Temperature$10^8\,\text{K}$
Density $n$$10^{20}\,\text{m}^{-3}$
Confinement time $\tau$$1\,\text{s}$ (magnetic) or $10^{-9}\,\text{s}$ (inertial)

Solved example (D + T → $^4\text{He}$ + n + 17.6 MeV)

Energy per reaction: $E = 17.6\,\text{MeV}=17.6\times10^6\times1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}=2.82\times10^{-12}\,\text{J}$.

Mass of one D‑T pair: $m = (2+3)u =5\times1.6605\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg}=8.30\times10^{-27}\,\text{kg}$.

Energy per kilogram of fuel: $\frac{E}{m}=\frac{2.82\times10^{-12}}{8.30\times10^{-27}}\approx3.4\times10^{14}\,\text{J kg}^{-1}$, which is $\sim10^5$ \times the energy from chemical combustion.

This high energy density, together with the stringent $T$, $n$, and $\tau$ requirements, defines the engineering challenge of achieving controlled nuclear fusion.

Applications of nuclear physics (power generation, medical, industrial)

Definition: Applications of nuclear physics exploit the energy released in nuclear reactions or the properties of radioactive nuclei for power generation, medical diagnostics/therapy, and industrial processes.

Key Formulas & SI Units

  • Mass‑energy equivalence: $E=mc^2$ (Joules)
  • Energy released per fission of $^{235}$U: $\approx200\,\text{MeV}=200\times1.602\times10^{-13}\,\text{J}$
  • Power: $P=\frac{Q}{t}$ (W)
  • Radioactive activity: $A=\lambda N$ (Bq)
  • Half‑life: $t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}$ (s)
  • Number of atoms: $N=\frac{m}{M}N_A$ (dimensionless)

Applications

SectorUseTypical Reaction / Isotope
Power GenerationHeat → Steam → Electricity$^{235}$U fission, $^{239}$Pu fission
MedicalRadiotherapy, PET imaging$^{60}$Co gamma rays, $^{18}$F positrons
IndustrialRadiography, tracer studies$^{192}$Ir source, $^{14}$C labeling

Solved Example

Find the average power output if $1\,\text{g}$ of $^{235}$U undergoes complete fission in $1\,\text{h}$.

  1. Number of atoms: $N=\frac{1\times10^{-3}\,\text{kg}}{235\times10^{-3}\,\text{kg\,mol^{-1}}}\times6.022\times10^{23}=2.56\times10^{21}$.
  2. Total energy released: $E=N\times200\,\text{MeV}=2.56\times10^{21}\times200\times1.602\times10^{-13}\,\text{J}=8.20\times10^{10}\,\text{J}$.
  3. Time: $t=1\,\text{h}=3600\,\text{s}$.
  4. Power: $P=\frac{E}{t}=\frac{8.20\times10^{10}}{3600}=2.28\times10^{7}\,\text{W}=22.8\,\text{MW}$.

Thus, $1\,\text{g}$ of $^{235}$U can theoretically produce about $23\,\text{MW}$ of thermal power for one hour.

Nuclear forces and stability of nuclei

Definition: Nuclear force (strong interaction) is the attractive force between nucleons that overcomes electrostatic repulsion, has a range ≈ $1$ fm, is charge‑independent and saturates.

Key properties

  • Short‑range: effective only for $r\lesssim 2$ fm.
  • Charge independence: same strength for $pp$, $nn$, $pn$ pairs.
  • Saturation: each nucleon interacts with only a few nearest neighbours.

Important formulas

QuantityExpressionSI unit
Mass defect$\Delta m = Z\,m_p + N\,m_n - M_{\text{nucl}}$kg
Binding energy$B = \Delta m\,c^{2}$J (or MeV)
Binding energy per nucleon$\dfrac{B}{A}$MeV
Nuclear radius$R = r_{0}A^{1/3}$m ( $r_{0}\approx1.2$ fm )

Stability condition – For light nuclei $Z/A\approx0.5$; for heavy nuclei the curve shifts to $Z/A\approx0.4$ due to increasing Coulomb repulsion. The most stable nuclei lie near the valley of stability given by the semi‑empirical mass formula.

Example: Calculate the binding energy per nucleon of $^{12}$C ( $Z=6$, $N=6$, atomic mass $12.0000\,$u).
Mass of proton $m_p=1.007276\,$u, neutron $m_n=1.008665\,$u, $1\,$u $=931.5\,$MeV/$c^{2}$.
$\Delta m = 6m_p+6m_n-12.0000 = 0.095646\,$u.
$B = 0.095646\times931.5 = 89.1\,$MeV.
$B/A = 89.1/12 = 7.43\,$MeV per nucleon.
