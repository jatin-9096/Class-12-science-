1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Definition of the Nucleus and Fundamental Nuclear Forces

The nucleus is the compact, positively charged core of an atom, composed of protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons). The number of protons, denoted by the atomic number $Z$, uniquely identifies the chemical element, while the total number of nucleons, called the mass number $A$, determines the isotope. The strong nuclear force, a short‑range interaction that acts between all nucleons, overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between protons and binds the nucleus together. This force is mediated by the exchange of mesons (pions) and is characterized by a potential that rises steeply at distances beyond $1.5$ fm, leading to a saturation property: each nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbours. The weak nuclear force, responsible for beta decay, and the electromagnetic force, responsible for the repulsion between protons, play secondary roles in nuclear structure. The interplay of these forces gives rise to the observed binding energies, nuclear shapes, and the existence of magic numbers, which correspond to closed shells of nucleons. The nuclear radius scales with the mass number as $R \approx R_0 A^{1/3}$, where $R_0 \approx 1.2$ fm, reflecting the nearly constant density of nuclear matter. Understanding the nucleus requires a quantum mechanical treatment, as nucleons occupy discrete energy levels within a potential well, leading to shell structure and collective phenomena such as vibrations and rotations. The study of nuclear forces and structure forms the foundation for exploring nuclear reactions, stability, and applications in energy production and medical diagnostics.

Physics · Core Law
Binding Energy, Mass Defect, and the Semi‑Empirical Mass Formula

The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of the masses of its constituent protons and neutrons; this difference is called the mass defect $\Delta m$, which is converted into binding energy $E_b$ via Einstein’s relation $E_b = \Delta m c^2$. The binding energy per nucleon, $E_b/A$, peaks around iron ($A \approx 56$), indicating that nuclei near this mass are most stable. The semi‑empirical mass formula (SEMF), also known as the liquid‑drop model, expresses the binding energy as a sum of volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms: $$ E_b(A,Z) = a_v A - a_s A^{2/3} - a_c \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_a \frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A} + \delta(A,Z), $$ where $a_v$, $a_s$, $a_c$, $a_a$ are empirically determined constants and $\delta$ accounts for pairing effects. The volume term reflects the short‑range nature of the strong force, the surface term corrects for nucleons at the boundary, the Coulomb term accounts for proton–proton repulsion, and the asymmetry term penalizes neutron–proton imbalance. The pairing term favours even–even nuclei. The SEMF reproduces the overall trend of binding energies and explains why heavy nuclei undergo fission and light nuclei undergo fusion. It also provides a framework for understanding nuclear stability, the valley of stability, and the limits of nuclear existence (drip lines). The mass defect and binding energy concepts are central to nuclear astrophysics, where they govern stellar nucleosynthesis and energy generation in stars.

Physics · Core Law
Nuclear Stability, the Valley of Stability, and Magic Numbers

Nuclear stability is governed by the balance between the attractive strong force and the repulsive electromagnetic force. The valley of stability is a curve in the $N$–$Z$ plane where stable nuclei lie; nuclei far from this valley are unstable and undergo radioactive decay. The concept of magic numbers ($2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126$) arises from the shell model, where nucleons occupy discrete energy levels in a mean potential. Closed shells confer extra stability, leading to pronounced peaks in binding energy per nucleon. The shell model explains phenomena such as the sudden drop in binding energy for nuclei with one more neutron than a magic number and the existence of doubly magic nuclei (e.g., $^{16}$O, $^{40}$Ca, $^{208}$Pb). The stability of a nucleus also depends on the neutron‑to‑proton ratio; for light nuclei $N \approx Z$ is optimal, while for heavy nuclei a surplus of neutrons is required to offset Coulomb repulsion. The drip lines mark the limits beyond which adding one more neutron or proton results in an unbound system. The interplay of shell effects, pairing, and deformation leads to a rich landscape of nuclear shapes and collective excitations, which are probed by spectroscopic techniques such as gamma‑ray spectroscopy and electron scattering. Understanding nuclear stability is essential for predicting decay modes, half‑lives, and for designing experiments in nuclear physics and astrophysics.

Physics · Core Law
Radioactive Decay Modes and Decay Laws

Radioactive decay is a stochastic process by which an unstable nucleus transforms into a more stable configuration, emitting particles or radiation. The most common decay modes are alpha ($\alpha$) decay, beta ($\beta^\pm$) decay, gamma ($\gamma$) emission, and electron capture. Alpha decay involves the emission of a $^4$He nucleus, reducing $Z$ by 2 and $A$ by 4; it is prevalent in heavy nuclei with $A > 200$. Beta decay changes a neutron into a proton (beta minus) or a proton into a neutron (beta plus), altering $Z$ by $\pm 1$ while leaving $A$ unchanged; it is mediated by the weak force. Gamma emission is the de‑excitation of a nucleus from an excited state to a lower energy state, emitting a high‑energy photon. Electron capture is the absorption of an orbital electron by a proton, converting it into a neutron and emitting a neutrino. The probability of decay per unit time is characterized by the decay constant $\lambda$, related to the half‑life $T_{1/2}$ by $\lambda = \ln 2 / T_{1/2}$. The number of undecayed nuclei $N(t)$ follows the exponential law $N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$. Decay chains, where daughter nuclei are themselves unstable, lead to complex temporal behaviour and are crucial in radiometric dating techniques (e.g., $^{14}$C dating). The energy released in decay, the Q‑value, is given by the mass difference between parent and daughter nuclei: $Q = [m_{\text{parent}} - m_{\text{daughter}}]c^2$. Understanding decay modes and laws is essential for nuclear safety, waste management, medical imaging (PET, SPECT), and for probing fundamental symmetries in particle physics.

Physics · Core Law
Nuclear Reactions, Cross Sections, and Applications

Nuclear reactions involve the interaction of two nuclei (or a nucleus and a particle) leading to the formation of new nuclei. The reaction cross section $\sigma$ quantifies the probability of a reaction occurring and depends on the incident energy, nuclear structure, and interaction potential. For charged‑particle reactions, the Coulomb barrier must be overcome; quantum tunnelling allows reactions to occur at sub‑barrier energies, a principle exploited in stellar nucleosynthesis. Fusion reactions, such as $p + p \rightarrow d + e^+ + \nu_e$, release energy when light nuclei combine, powering stars. Fission reactions, where a heavy nucleus splits into two fragments, release large amounts of energy and are the basis of nuclear reactors and atomic weapons. The energy released per fission event is about $200$ MeV, corresponding to a mass defect of $\sim 0.1$% of the original mass. Controlled fission in reactors is achieved by moderating neutrons with materials like graphite or heavy water to sustain a chain reaction. Applications of nuclear reactions extend to medical isotope production (e.g., $^{99m}$Tc), cancer therapy (hadron therapy), and industrial imaging. The study of reaction mechanisms, such as direct reactions, compound nucleus formation, and statistical decay, provides insight into nuclear structure and the forces at play. Theoretical models, including the optical model, coupled‑channels approach, and Hauser–Feshbach statistical theory, are employed to predict cross sections and reaction rates, which are essential for designing reactors, assessing radiological hazards, and understanding nucleosynthesis pathways in astrophysical environments.

1. Mass Defect and Binding Energy

The mass of a nucleus is not simply the sum of the masses of its constituent protons and neutrons. The missing mass, called the mass defect, is a direct manifestation of the binding energy that holds the nucleons together. Consider a nucleus with atomic mass number $A$ and atomic number $Z$. The total rest mass of the free nucleons is

$M_{\text{free}} = Z\,m_p + (A-Z)\,m_n$

where $m_p$ and $m_n$ are the rest masses of the proton and neutron, respectively. The actual rest mass of the nucleus, $M$, is measured experimentally and is always smaller than $M_{\text{free}}$. The mass defect is defined as

$\Delta M = M_{\text{free}} - M$

Using Einstein’s mass–energy equivalence, the binding energy $E_B$ of the nucleus is obtained by

$$E_B = \Delta M\,c^2 = \bigl(Z\,m_p + (A-Z)\,m_n - M\bigr)c^2$$

where $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum ($c \approx 3.00\times10^8$ m s$^{-1}$). The binding energy is always positive, indicating that energy must be supplied to disassemble the nucleus into free nucleons. The binding energy per nucleon, $E_B/A$, provides a convenient measure of the stability of a nucleus; it reaches a maximum for iron and nickel isotopes, which explains why these nuclei are the end products of both fusion and fission processes.

2. Semi‑Empirical Mass Formula (Weizsäcker Formula)

The semi‑empirical mass formula (SEMF) offers an approximate expression for the binding energy of a nucleus in terms of its mass number $A$ and atomic number $Z$. It is derived by considering the nucleus as a liquid drop, incorporating volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing effects. The SEMF is written as

$$E_B(A,Z) = a_v A - a_s A^{2/3} - a_c \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}} - a_a \frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A} + \delta(A,Z)$$

where the coefficients $a_v$, $a_s$, $a_c$, $a_a$, and $\delta$ are empirically determined constants. The pairing term $\delta(A,Z)$ is given by

$$\delta(A,Z) = \begin{cases} +a_p A^{-3/4} & \text{for even–even nuclei} \\ 0 & \text{for odd $A$} \\ -a_p A^{-3/4} & \text{for odd–odd nuclei} \end{cases}$$

with $a_p$ another empirical constant. The volume term $a_v A$ reflects the short‑range attractive nuclear force that acts uniformly throughout the nucleus. The surface term $-a_s A^{2/3}$ corrects for the fact that nucleons at the surface have fewer neighbors. The Coulomb term $-a_c Z(Z-1)/A^{1/3}$ accounts for the electrostatic repulsion between protons. The asymmetry term $-a_a (A-2Z)^2/A$ penalizes nuclei that deviate from equal numbers of protons and neutrons. Finally, the pairing term captures the extra stability of nuclei with paired nucleons.
