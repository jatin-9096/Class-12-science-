1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

Magnetism is a fundamental interaction that arises from the motion of electric charges. The magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ at a point in space is defined as the force per unit charge that a small test charge would experience if it were moving with an infinitesimally small velocity $\mathbf{v}$, i.e. $\mathbf{F}=q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$. The direction of $\mathbf{B}$ is given by the right‑hand rule: if the fingers of the right hand point in the direction of $\mathbf{v}$ and the thumb points in the direction of the force, the palm faces the direction of $\mathbf{B}$. The magnetic flux $\Phi$ through a surface $S$ is the surface integral of the normal component of the magnetic field: $$\Phi=\int_S \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}.$$ In SI units, the magnetic field is measured in tesla (T), where $1\,\text{T}=1\,\text{kg}\,\text{s}^{-2}\,\text{A}^{-1}$. The magnetic field produced by a steady current element $I\,d\mathbf{l}$ is given by the Biot–Savart law: $$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2},$$ where $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the unit vector from the current element to the field point and $r$ is the distance between them. The integral over a closed loop yields Ampère’s law in integral form: $$\oint \mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l}= \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}},$$ where $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the net current enclosed by the loop. These laws form the backbone of magnetostatics, the study of magnetic fields in the absence of time‑varying electric fields. The Lorentz force law, which combines electric and magnetic effects, is expressed as $$\mathbf{F}=q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}),$$ and reduces to the magnetic force when $\mathbf{E}=0$. Understanding these basic definitions and laws is essential before exploring the magnetic behaviour of materials and the dynamic phenomena of electromagnetic induction.

Physics · Core Law
Magnetic Force on Moving Charges and Current-Carrying Conductors

The magnetic force on a moving charge is perpendicular to both the velocity of the charge and the magnetic field. This perpendicularity leads to circular or helical trajectories of charged particles in uniform magnetic fields, a principle exploited in devices such as cyclotrons and mass spectrometers. For a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ in a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, the magnitude of the force is $F=qvB\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. When a conductor of length $l$ carrying a current $I$ is placed in a magnetic field, the net magnetic force on the conductor is $$\mathbf{F}=I\,\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}.$$ The direction follows the right‑hand rule applied to the current direction and the magnetic field. This principle underlies the operation of electric motors, where the interaction between current and magnetic field produces torque. The torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ on a current loop of area $A$ with magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\mu}=I\,\mathbf{A}$ in a magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is $$\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B}.$$ The magnetic moment is a vector normal to the plane of the loop, and its magnitude is $I$ times the area. The energy of a magnetic dipole in a field is $U=-\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{B}$, indicating that the dipole tends to align with the field. A worked example: a rectangular loop of side lengths $a$ and $b$ lies in the $xy$‑plane, carrying a current $I$ counter‑clockwise when viewed from the positive $z$‑axis. If a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}=B\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ is applied, the torque on the loop is $$\boldsymbol{\tau}=I\,ab\,B\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}=0,$$ showing that the loop experiences no torque because its magnetic moment is parallel to the field. However, if the field is tilted by an angle $\phi$ from the $z$‑axis, the torque becomes $\tau=I\,ab\,B\sin\phi$, causing the loop to rotate until it aligns with the field. Such examples illustrate the vector nature of magnetic forces and the importance of geometry in magnetic interactions.

Physics · Core Law
Magnetic Properties of Materials

Materials respond to applied magnetic fields in diverse ways, classified mainly into diamagnetic, paramagnetic, ferromagnetic, antiferromagnetic, and ferrimagnetic categories. Diamagnetism arises from induced magnetic moments that oppose the applied field, leading to a weak repulsive effect. The susceptibility $\chi$ for diamagnetic materials is negative and typically of the order $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$. Paramagnetic substances possess unpaired electrons that align partially with the field, resulting in a weak attraction; their susceptibility is positive but small ($10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$). Ferromagnetic materials exhibit spontaneous magnetization even in the absence of an external field, due to exchange interactions that align neighbouring spins. The magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ can be large, and the relationship between $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{H}$, and $\mathbf{M}$ is $$\mathbf{B}=\mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M}).$$ The hysteresis loop of a ferromagnet shows the dependence of $\mathbf{M}$ on $\mathbf{H}$, revealing remanence and coercivity. Antiferromagnets have neighbouring spins aligned antiparallel, canceling each other’s magnetization, while ferrimagnets have unequal antiparallel moments, giving a net magnetization. The Curie temperature $T_C$ marks the transition from ordered to disordered magnetic phases; above $T_C$, thermal agitation overcomes exchange interactions, and the material becomes paramagnetic. A practical example: iron (Fe) has a Curie temperature of $1043\,\text{K}$. Below this temperature, iron exhibits strong ferromagnetism, making it useful for permanent magnets and transformer cores. Above $T_C$, iron behaves as a paramagnet with a negligible magnetic response. Understanding these magnetic behaviours is crucial for designing magnetic devices, selecting core materials, and interpreting magnetic measurements in research and industry.

Physics · Core Law
Electromagnetic Induction

Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction states that a change in magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force (emf) in that circuit: $$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}.$$ The negative sign embodies Lenz’s law, which asserts that the induced emf generates a current whose magnetic field opposes the change in flux. For a coil of $N$ turns, the induced emf becomes $$\mathcal{E}=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}.$$ Self‑inductance $L$ quantifies the emf induced in a circuit due to its own changing current: $$\mathcal{E}=-L\frac{dI}{dt}.$$ Mutual inductance $M$ describes the emf induced in one circuit due to a changing current in another: $$\mathcal{E}_2=-M\frac{dI_1}{dt}.$$ A classic example is a transformer: a primary coil with $N_p$ turns and a secondary coil with $N_s$ turns are magnetically coupled through a core. The voltage ratio is given by $$\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p},$$ assuming ideal coupling. In practice, core losses, leakage inductance, and winding resistance reduce efficiency. Another worked example: a single loop of radius $a$ lies in a uniform magnetic field $B(t)=B_0\sin\omega t$ perpendicular to the loop plane. The flux through the loop is $\Phi(t)=\pi a^2 B_0\sin\omega t$, giving an induced emf $$\mathcal{E}(t)=-\pi a^2 B_0\omega\cos\omega t.$$ The maximum emf is $\mathcal{E}_{\max}=\pi a^2 B_0\omega$, illustrating how the rate of change of the magnetic field dictates the induced voltage. Electromagnetic induction underpins generators, inductors, transformers, and many modern technologies, linking magnetic fields and electric currents in a dynamic, reciprocal relationship.

Physics · Core Law
Applications and Advanced Topics in Magnetism

Magnetic circuits are engineered systems that guide magnetic flux through ferromagnetic cores, minimizing leakage and maximizing efficiency. The magnetic reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ of a path is analogous to electrical resistance and is defined as $$\mathcal{R}=\frac{\ell}{\mu A},$$ where $\ell$ is the path length, $A$ the cross‑sectional area, and $\mu$ the permeability of the material. The total flux in a magnetic circuit is then $\Phi=F/\mathcal{R}$, where $F$ is the magnetomotive force $F=NI$. Magnetic shielding employs high‑permeability materials like mu‑metal to divert stray magnetic fields, protecting sensitive equipment. In magnetic recording, data is stored by magnetizing tiny domains on a medium; the orientation of each domain represents binary information. The phenomenon of magnetic resonance, particularly nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and electron spin resonance (ESR), exploits the precession of nuclear or electron spins in a magnetic field, providing detailed insights into molecular structure and electronic environments. The Larmor frequency of precession is given by $$\omega_L=\gamma B,$$ where $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio. In NMR, this frequency lies in the radio‑frequency range, enabling spectroscopic analysis of chemical compounds. In medical imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) uses strong magnetic fields and radio‑frequency pulses to generate detailed images of soft tissues, revolutionizing diagnostics. These advanced applications demonstrate how the fundamental principles of magnetism translate into technologies that shape modern life, from power generation to data storage and medical imaging.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this section we present the step‑by‑step derivations of the most frequently used equations in the chapter Magnetism & Matter as prescribed by the CBSE Class 12 syllabus. Each derivation follows the logical sequence adopted in the NCERT textbook, beginning with the fundamental laws (Biot–Savart law, Ampère’s circuital law, Lorentz force) and proceeding to the specialised results (magnetic dipole moment, torque on a current loop, magnetic susceptibility, Curie’s law, Hall effect). The aim is to provide a clear, mathematically rigorous pathway that students can follow while solving numerical problems or answering conceptual questions.

1. Magnetic Field of a Long Straight Conductor (Biot–Savart Law)

Consider an infinitely long straight wire carrying a steady current $I$ along the $z$‑axis. Choose a field point $P$ at a perpendicular distance $r$ from the wire. An infinitesimal element $d\vec{l}=dz\,\hat{k}$ of the wire contributes a magnetic field $d\vec{B}$ at $P$ given by the Biot–Savart law:

$$d\vec{B}= \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}}{r^2}$$

Here $\hat{r}$ is the unit vector from the element to $P$, and $r$ is the magnitude of that displacement. Because $d\vec{l}$ is parallel to $\hat{k}$ and $\hat{r}$ lies in the radial direction, $d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}$ points in the azimuthal $\hat{\phi}$ direction. The magnitude of the cross‑product is $|d\vec{l}\times\hat{r}|=dz\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $d\vec{l}$ and $\vec{r}$. Geometry of the right‑angled triangle gives $\sin\theta = \dfrac{r}{\sqrt{r^2+z^2}}$ and $r$ (the distance from the element to $P$) equals $\sqrt{r^2+z^2}$. Substituting these relations:

$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,dz\,r}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$$

The direction is $\hat{\phi}$, so we write $d\vec{B}=dB\,\hat{\phi}$. Integrating $z$ from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$:

$$\vec{B}= \hat{\phi}\,\frac{\mu_0 I r}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$$

Let $z=r\tan\theta$, then $dz=r\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $r^2+z^2=r^2\sec^2\theta$. The integral simplifies to:

$$\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{r\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{\bigl(r^2\sec^2\theta\bigr)^{3/2}} =\frac{1}{r^2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta =\frac{2}{r^2}$$

Hence the magnetic field magnitude is

$$B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$

and the vector form is

$$\boxed{\vec{B}= \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\hat{\phi}}$$

This result is valid for an infinitely long straight conductor carrying a steady current, under the assumption that the surrounding medium is free space (relative permeability $\mu_r=1$).

2. Ampère’s Circuital Law for a Solenoid

Consider a tightly wound solenoid of $n$ turns per unit length, carrying a current $I$. Choose a rectangular Amperian loop that encloses $N=n\ell$ turns over a length $\ell$ of the solenoid. Ampère’s law states

$$\oint\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l}= \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$

Inside the solenoid the field is essentially uniform and parallel to the axis; outside it is negligible. Therefore the line integral reduces to $B\ell$ (the contribution from the side parallel to the field) and the enclosed current is $NI=n\ell I$. Substituting:

$$B\ell = \mu_0 n\ell I\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B = \mu_0 n I$$

When the solenoid is filled with a magnetic material of relative permeability $\mu_r$, the field becomes

$$\boxed{B = \mu_0 \mu_r n I = \mu n I}$$

where $\mu=\mu_0\mu_r$ is the absolute permeability of the core material. This expression holds under the condition that the solenoid is long ($\ell\gg$ radius) so that edge effects are negligible.

3. Torque on a Current Loop (Magnetic Dipole Moment)

Take a planar rectangular loop of area $A$ carrying a current $I$ in a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}$. The forces on opposite sides are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, producing a couple. The torque magnitude is

$$\tau = IAB\sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the normal $\hat{n}$ to the loop and $\vec{B}$. Defining the magnetic dipole moment $\vec{m}=I A \hat{n}$, the torque vector can be written compactly as

$$\boxed{\vec{\tau}= \vec{m}\times\vec{B}}$$

This relation is central to the analysis of magnetic materials, as the net magnetisation $\vec{M}$ of a material is the magnetic moment per unit volume.

4. Force on a Moving Charge (Lorentz Force)

The fundamental expression for the force experienced by a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec{v}$ in a magnetic field $\vec{B}$ is

$$\boxed{\vec{F}= q\,\vec{v}\times\vec{B}}$$

When an electric field $\vec{E}$ is also present, the total electromagnetic force (Lorentz force) becomes

$$\boxed{\vec{F}= q\bigl(\vec{E}+\vec{v}\times\vec{B}\bigr)}$$

These equations are valid for non‑relativistic speeds ($v\ll c$) and assume that the fields are static or vary slowly enough that induced electric fields can be neglected.

5. Hall Effect and Hall Coefficient

Consider a thin rectangular slab of a conductor carrying a current $I$ along the $x$‑direction, placed in a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}=B\hat{z}$. Charge carriers of magnitude $e$ and number density $n$ experience a magnetic force $q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ that pushes them towards the $y$‑faces, creating a transverse electric field $E_y$ (Hall field). At equilibrium the magnetic force balances the electric force:

$$eE_y = ev_d B$$

where $v_d$ is the drift velocity related to current density $J$ by $J = ne v_d$. Since $J = I/(wt)$ for a slab of width $w$ and thickness $t$, we obtain

$$E_y = \frac{IB}{netw}$$

The Hall voltage $V_H$ across the width $w$ is $V_H = E_y w$, giving

$$\boxed{V_H = \frac{IB}{net}}$$

The Hall coefficient $R_H$ is defined as

$$\boxed{R_H = \frac{E_y}{J B}= \frac{1}{ne}}$$

This expression allows experimental determination of the sign and magnitude of charge carriers in a material.

6. Magnetisation, Magnetic Susceptibility and Permeability

Magnetisation $\vec{M}$ is defined as the magnetic moment per unit volume:

$$\boxed{\vec{M}= \frac{\vec{m}_{\text{total}}}{V}}$$

For linear, isotropic, and homogeneous materials, $\vec{M}$ is proportional to the applied magnetic field $\vec{H}$:

$$\boxed{\vec{M}= \chi_m \vec{H}}$$

where $\chi_m$ is the volume magnetic susceptibility (dimensionless). The magnetic flux density $\vec{B}$ relates to $\vec{H}$ and $\vec{M}$ as

$$\boxed{\vec{B}= \mu_0(\vec{H}+\vec{M}) = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)\vec{H}= \mu \vec{H}}$$

Thus the absolute permeability $\mu$ is $\mu = \mu_0\mu_r$, with $\mu_r = 1+\chi_m$ being the relative permeability.

7. Curie’s Law for Paramagnets

In a paramagnetic material, the magnetic susceptibility varies inversely with absolute temperature $T$. Starting from the statistical alignment of atomic magnetic moments in a field, one obtains

$$\chi_m = \frac{C}{T}$$

where $C$ is Curie’s constant, given by

$$C = \frac{\mu_0 N \mu_{\text{eff}}^2}{3k_B}$$

Here $N$ is the number of magnetic ions per unit volume, $\mu_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective magnetic moment, and $k_B$ is Boltzmann’s constant. Curie’s law holds for temperatures well above any ordering temperature (e.g., Néel or Curie temperature) and for weak applied fields.

8. Magnetic Energy in a Field

The potential energy $U$ of a magnetic dipole $\vec{m}$ in a uniform magnetic field $\vec{B}$ is

$$\boxed{U = -\vec{m}\cdot\vec{B}}$$

For a current loop of $N$ turns, $U = -N I A B \cos\theta$. The energy density $u$ stored in a magnetic field in free space is

$$\boxed{u = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}}$$

This expression is derived by equating the work done in establishing the field to the integral of $\vec{B}\cdot d\vec{H}$ over the volume.
