Magnetic field: definition, lines of force and properties

Definition: The magnetic field $ \\vec B$ at a point is defined as the force per unit positive test charge moving with velocity $ \\vec v$ perpendicular to the field, i.e., $\n\\vec B = \frac{\n\\vec F}{q\,v\,\sin\theta}$. Its SI unit is the tesla (T), where $1\,\text{T}=\frac{\text{N}}{\text{A}\,\text{m}}$.

Lines of magnetic force

  • They are continuous loops; they never begin or end.
  • Direction of a line at any point is tangent to the direction of $\n\\vec B$.
  • Density of lines (lines per unit area) is proportional to the magnitude of $\n\\vec B$.
  • Lines never intersect each other.

Key formula for a long straight conductor

From the Biot–Savart law, the magnetic field at a perpendicular distance $r$ from a straight wire carrying current $I$ is

$$\n\\vec B = \frac{\n\\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$

where $\n\\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\text{T·m/A}$.

SI Units

QuantitySymbolUnit
Magnetic field$\n\\vec B$tesla (T)
Current$I$ampere (A)
Permeability of free space$\n\\mu_0$T·m/A

Solved example

Problem: A long straight wire carries $I=10\,\text{A}$. Find the magnetic field $B$ at a point $5\,\text{cm}$ from the wire.

Solution: Convert $r=5\,\text{cm}=0.05\,\text{m}$. Using $B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$,

$$\n\\vec B = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})\times10}{2\pi\times0.05}=\frac{4\pi\times10^{-6}}{0.1\pi}=4\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$$

Thus $B=4\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$ directed azimuthally according to the right‑hand rule.

Motion of charged particles in a magnetic field and cyclotron motion

Magnetic force on a moving charge: When a charge $q$ moves with velocity $\\vec{v}$ in a magnetic field $\\vec{B}$, it experiences a force $\\vec{F}=q\\,\\vec{v}\\times\\vec{B}$ (SI unit N). The magnitude is $F=|q|vB\\sin\\theta$, where $\\theta$ is the angle between $\\vec{v}$ and $\\vec{B}$.

Uniform field, $90^\\circ$ leads to uniform circular motion. Equating magnetic force to centripetal force $\\frac{mv^{2}}{r}$ gives

$r=\\frac{mv}{|q|B}$, $T=\\frac{2\\pi m}{|q|B}$, $f=\\frac{|q|B}{2\\pi m}$.

Key formulas:

QuantityFormulaSI unit
Radius $r$$r=\\frac{mv}{|q|B}$m
Period $T$$T=\\frac{2\\pi m}{|q|B}$s
Frequency $f$$f=\\frac{|q|B}{2\\pi m}$Hz

Example:

A proton ($m=1.67\\times10^{-27}\\,\\text{kg}$, $q=+e=1.60\\times10^{-19}\\,\\text{C}$) enters a uniform magnetic field $B=0.5\\,\\text{T}$ perpendicular to its velocity $v=2\\times10^{6}\\,\\text{m s}^{-1}$. Find the radius of its path and the cyclotron frequency.

  • Radius $r=\\frac{mv}{qB}=\\frac{(1.67\\times10^{-27})(2\\times10^{6})}{(1.60\\times10^{-19})(0.5)}\\approx4.2\\times10^{-2}\\,\\text{m}$.
  • Frequency $f=\\frac{qB}{2\\pi m}=\\frac{(1.60\\times10^{-19})(0.5)}{2\\pi(1.67\\times10^{-27})}\\approx7.6\\times10^{6}\\,\\text{Hz}$.

Thus the proton moves in a circular orbit of radius $\\approx4.2\\,\\text{cm}$ with a cyclotron frequency of $\\approx7.6\\,\\text{MHz}$.

Force on a current‑carrying conductor and Hall effect

Force on a current‑carrying conductor

When a conductor of length $\ell$ carrying current $I$ is placed in a magnetic field $B$, the magnetic force on it is given by

$$F = I\,\ell\,B\,\sin\theta$$

Derivation: The drift velocity of charge carriers is $v_d$. For $n$ carriers per unit volume, charge $q$, the current $I = n q A v_d$. The Lorentz force on each carrier is $q\,v_d\,B\,\sin\theta$. Multiplying by the total number of carriers \in the segment $n A \ell$ gives $F = I \ell B \sin\theta$.

SI unit: newton (N).

Hall effect

When a magnetic field $B$ is applied perpendicular \to the direction of current $I$ \in a thin slab of thickness $t$, charge carriers accumulate on one side, creating a transverse Hall voltage $V_H$:

$$V_H = \frac{I B}{n e t} = R_H \frac{I B}{t}$$

Hall coefficient $R_H = \frac{1}{n e}$ (unit $m^3 C^{-1}$). $V_H$ is measured \in volts (V).

SymbolFormulaUnit
$F$$I\,\ell\,B\,\sin\theta$$N$
$V_H$$\frac{I B}{n e t}$$V$
$R_H$$\frac{1}{n e}$$m^3 C^{-1}$

Example

Find the magnetic force on a 5 m long wire carrying $3\,\text{A}$ placed perpendicular ($\theta=90^\circ$) \to a uniform magnetic field of $0.2\,\text{T}$.

Solution: $F = I\,\ell\,B = 3 \times 5 \times 0.2 = 3\,\text{N}$.

Torque on a current loop and magnetic dipole moment

Torque on a current loop – When a planar loop carrying current $I$ is placed in a uniform magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, each side experiences a magnetic force $d\mathbf{F}=I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$. The resultant couple about the centre is called the torque $\boldsymbol{\tau}$.

Magnetic dipole moment – For any closed loop the vector quantity

SymbolExpressionSI unit
$\boldsymbol{\mu}$$I\mathbf{A}$$\text{A·m}^2$

where $\mathbf{A}=A\hat{n}$ is the area vector (magnitude $A$ and direction $\hat{n}$ normal to the plane).

Key formula

  • $\boldsymbol{\tau}= \boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B}$
  • Magnitude: $\tau = IAB\sin\theta$

SI unit of torque: newton‑metre (N·m). $I$ in ampere (A), $A$ in square metre (m$^2$), $B$ in tesla (T), $\theta$ the angle between $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.

Derivation (brief)

Consider a rectangular loop of sides $l$ and $w$. Forces on opposite sides are equal and opposite, producing a couple $F l = I B w \; l$. Adding the contribution from the other pair gives $\tau = I B (l w)\sin\theta = IAB\sin\theta$, which is independent of the shape, leading to the vector form $\boldsymbol{\tau}= \boldsymbol{\mu}\times\mathbf{B}$.

Example

A rectangular loop $0.10\ \text{m}\times0.20\ \text{m}$ carries $I=5\ \text{A}$. It is placed in a uniform field $B=0.30\ \text{T}$ making $\theta=30^\circ$ with the normal. Area $A=0.10\times0.20=0.020\ \text{m}^2$. Magnetic moment $\mu = I A =5\times0.020=0.10\ \text{A·m}^2$. Torque $\tau = \mu B\sin\theta =0.10\times0.30\times\sin30^\circ =0.015\ \text{N·m}$.

Earth’s magnetic field and magnetic declination

Earth’s Magnetic Field

  • Definition: The magnetic field produced by the Earth behaves like a giant bar‑magnet whose magnetic dipole moment $M$ is aligned approximately along the geographic axis.
  • SI unit: $B$ – tesla (T); $M$ – ampere‑meter$^2$ (A·m$^2$).
  • Dipole formula (at surface, $r=R_E$): $$B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2M}{R_E^3}$$ where $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\text{H·m}^{-1}$.
  • Components: Horizontal $B_H = B\cos I$, vertical $B_V = B\sin I$, where $I$ is magnetic inclination.

Magnetic Declination ($D$)

  • Angle between geographic north (true north) and magnetic north (direction of $B_H$).
  • Positive eastward, negative westward. Measured \in degrees ($^\circ$).
SymbolMeaningUnit
$B$Resultant magnetic fieldT
$B_H$Horizontal componentT
$B_V$Vertical componentT
$I$Magnetic inclination°
$D$Magnetic declination°

Solved Example

Given $B = 5.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$ and $I = 60^\circ$, find $B_H$ and $B_V$.

Solution:

  • $B_H = B\cos I = 5.0\times10^{-5}\cos60^\circ = 5.0\times10^{-5}\times0.5 = 2.5\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$
  • $B_V = B\sin I = 5.0\times10^{-5}\sin60^\circ = 5.0\times10^{-5}\times\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 4.33\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$

Thus $B_H = 2.5\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$ and $B_V = 4.33\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$.

Magnetic materials: diamagnetism, paramagnetism and ferromagnetism

Magnetic Materials

  • Diamagnetism: All atoms develop an induced magnetic moment opposite to the applied field. $\chi < 0$ (dimensionless). $\vec{M}= \chi \vec{H}$ with $\chi$ small ($\sim -10^{-5}$). No permanent magnetic moment.
  • Paramagnetism: Materials possess permanent atomic moments that align partially with $\vec{H}$. $\chi > 0$ and follows Curie’s law $\chi = \dfrac{C}{T}$, where $C$ is Curie constant. Magnetization $M = \chi H = \dfrac{C}{T} H$.
  • Ferromagnetism: Strong exchange interaction aligns moments parallel even without $\vec{H}$. Below Curie temperature $T_C$, spontaneous magnetization $M_s$ appears. Above $T_C$, susceptibility obeys Curie‑Weiss law $\chi = \dfrac{C}{T-\theta}$ with $\theta \approx T_C$. Weiss molecular field $H_{eff}=H+\lambda M$.

Key Relations

QuantitySymbolExpressionSI Unit
Magnetization$M$$M=\chi H$A·m$^{-1}$
Magnetic susceptibility$\chi$dimensionless–
Curie law (paramagnets)$\chi = \dfrac{C}{T}$–
Curie‑Weiss law (ferromagnets)$\chi = \dfrac{C}{T-\theta}$–
Weiss field constant$\lambda$$H_{eff}=H+\lambda M$A·m$^{-1}$

Solved Example

Given $C = 1.5\ \text{K·emu·mol}^{-1}$, $T = 300\ \text{K}$ and $H = 10^{4}\ \text{A·m}^{-1}$, find $M$.

  1. Compute susceptibility: $\chi = \dfrac{C}{T}= \dfrac{1.5}{300}=5.0\times10^{-3}$.
  2. Magnetization: $M = \chi H = 5.0\times10^{-3}\times10^{4}=50\ \text{A·m}^{-1}$.

Thus the paramagnetic sample acquires $M = 50\ \text{A·m}^{-1}$ under the given field.

Magnetic hysteresis, domains and Curie temperature

Magnetic Hysteresis, Domains and Curie Temperature

When a ferromagnetic material is subjected to a cyclic magnetizing field $H$, the magnetic induction $B$ does not retrace the same path; the $B$–$H$ curve forms a hysteresis loop. The loop is characterised by:

  • Retentivity (or remanent induction) $B_{r}$: $B$ remaining when $H$ is reduced to zero (unit: $\text{T}$).
  • Coercivity $H_{c}$: magnitude of reverse field required to bring $B$ to zero (unit: $\text{A/m}$).
  • Loop area: proportional to energy loss per unit volume per cycle, $W = \text{area}$ (unit: $\text{J/m}^3$).

Fundamental relations:

SymbolDefinitionFormulaSI Unit
$B$Magnetic induction$B = \mu_{0}(H+M)$T
$H$Magnetizing field–A/m
$M$Magnetisation$M = \chi H$A/m
$\mu$Permeability$\mu = \frac{B}{H}$–
$\chi$Magnetic susceptibility$\chi = \frac{M}{H}$–

Ferromagnetic materials consist of magnetic domains – regions where atomic moments are aligned. In the unmagnetised state, domains are randomly oriented, giving net $M=0$. An external $H$ grows favourably aligned domains, producing macroscopic magnetisation.

Curie Temperature $T_{C}$ is the temperature at which spontaneous magnetisation vanishes and the material becomes paramagnetic. Above $T_{C}$ the susceptibility follows the Curie‑Weiss law:

$$\chi = \frac{C}{T - T_{C}}$$

where $C$ is the Curie constant (unit: K). At $T=T_{C}$, $\chi$ diverges.

Example: A ferromagnet shows a rectangular hysteresis loop with $B_{r}=0.8\,\text{T}$ and $H_{c}=200\,\text{A/m}$. Approximate the energy loss per unit volume per cycle.

For a rectangular loop, $W \approx 4 B_{r} H_{c}$. Hence

$$W = 4 \times 0.8 \times 200 = 640\,\text{J/m}^3$$

The material loses $640\,\text{J}$ of energy per cubic metre each magnetisation cycle.

Electromagnetic induction: Faraday’s law and Lenz’s law

Electromagnetic Induction – When a magnetic flux through a circuit changes with time, an emf is induced in the circuit.

Faraday’s law (integral form):

$$\mathcal{E}= -\,\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$

For a coil of $N$ turns, $$\mathcal{E}= -N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}$$ where $\Phi = B A\cos\theta$.

  • SI unit of emf: $V$ (volt).
  • SI unit of magnetic flux: $Wb$ (weber) = $T\;m^{2}$.

Lenz’s law – The direction of the induced emf is such that the induced current opposes the change producing it.

SymbolMeaningSI Unit
$\mathcal{E}$Induced emf$V$
$\Phi$Magnetic flux$Wb$
$B$Magnetic flux density$T$
$A$Area of coil$m^{2}$
$N$Number of turns–

Key derivation (emf for a rotating coil)

For a coil rotating with angular speed $\omega$ \in a uniform $B$, $\Phi = B A\cos\omega t$. Hence $$\mathcal{E}= -N\frac{d}{dt}(B A\cos\omega t)= N B A\omega\sin\omega t$$ and the maximum emf $$\mathcal{E}_{\max}= N B A\omega$$.

Example

A rectangular coil of $N=50$ turns, area $A=0.02\;m^{2}$ rotates at $\omega =60\;rad\,s^{-1}$ \in a magnetic field $B=0.5\;T$. Find the peak emf.

Solution:

$$\mathcal{E}_{\max}= N B A\omega = 50\times0.5\times0.02\times60 = 30\;V$$

Thus the coil produces a sinusoidal emf of amplitude $30\;V$.
