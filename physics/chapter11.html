Photoelectric effect and Einstein’s equation

Photoelectric Effect – emission of electrons from a metal surface when it is illuminated by light of frequency $\nu$ greater than a threshold frequency $\nu_0$.

  • Einstein’s equation: $eV_s = h\nu - \phi$, where $V_s$ is the stopping potential (V), $h$ is Planck’s constant ($6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$), $\nu$ is the frequency of incident radiation (Hz), $\phi$ is the work function (J or eV), and $e$ is the elementary charge ($1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$).
  • Maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons: $K_{max}=\frac{1}{2}m_e v_{max}^2 = h\nu - \phi$.
  • Threshold condition: $h\nu_0 = \phi$.

Key observations

  • Existence of a cut‑off frequency $\nu_0$; electrons are emitted only if $\nu>\nu_0$, irrespective of light intensity.
  • Kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends linearly on frequency and is independent of light intensity.
  • Increasing intensity raises the number of emitted electrons but not their kinetic energy.

Sample problem

Monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda = 400\,\text{nm}$ shines on a sodium surface whose work function is $\phi = 2.28\,\text{eV}$. Find the stopping potential $V_s$.

Solution:

  1. Convert wavelength to frequency: $\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda}=\frac{3.00\times10^8}{4.00\times10^{-7}}=7.50\times10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.
  2. Photon energy: $E_{ph}=h\nu = 6.626\times10^{-34}\times7.50\times10^{14}=4.97\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}=3.10\,\text{eV}$.
  3. Apply Einstein’s equation: $eV_s = E_{ph} - \phi = 3.10\,\text{eV} - 2.28\,\text{eV}=0.82\,\text{eV}$.
  4. Since $e=1\,\text{eV/V}$, $V_s = 0.82\,\text{V}$.

Thus the stopping potential required to halt the most energetic electrons is $0.82\,\text{V}$.

Work function, threshold frequency and stopping potential

Work function (φ): Minimum energy required to liberate an electron from the surface of a metal. SI unit: joule (J) or electron‑volt (eV).

Threshold frequency (ν0): The lowest frequency of incident radiation that can cause photo‑emission. It is related to the work function by
$$\phi = h\nu_{0}$$ where $h$ is Planck's constant.

Stopping potential (V_{s}): The retarding potential that reduces the photo‑electric current \to zero. The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons is
$$K_{max}=eV_{s}=h\nu-\phi$$ where $e$ is the elementary charge.

  • Einstein’s photo‑electric equation: $K_{max}=h\nu-\phi$.
  • SI units: $\phi$ – J (or eV), $\nu$ – Hz, $h$ – J·s, $e$ – C, $V_{s}$ – V.

Work function\phi\phi = h\nu_{0}J (eV)
Threshold frequency\nu_{0}\nu_{0}=\phi/hHz
Stopping potentialV_{s}eV_{s}=h\nu-\phiV

Example: Light of wavelength $\lambda=400\,\text{nm}$ falls on a metal whose work function is $\phi=2.0\,\text{eV}$. Find the stopping potential.

Solution:

  1. Frequency $\nu = c/\lambda = (3\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s})/(4\times10^{-7}\,\text{m}) = 7.5\times10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.
  2. Photon energy $E_{ph}=h\nu = 6.626\times10^{-34}\times7.5\times10^{14}=4.97\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}=3.10\,\text{eV}$.
  3. Maximum kinetic energy $K_{max}=E_{ph}-\phi = 3.10\,\text{eV}-2.0\,\text{eV}=1.10\,\text{eV}$.
  4. Stopping potential $V_{s}=K_{max}/e = 1.10\,\text{V}$.

Thus, $V_{s}=1.10\,\text{V}$.

Compton effect and wavelength shift formula

Compton Effect – Elastic scattering of a photon by a loosely bound (practically free) electron, resulting in a measurable increase in the photon’s wavelength.

  • Key formula (wavelength shift): $\\Delta \\lambda = \\lambda' - \\lambda = \\frac{h}{m_e c}(1-\\cos\\theta)$
  • SI units: $\\lambda,\\lambda'$ – metres (m); $\\Delta \\lambda$ – m; $h$ – J·s; $m_e$ – kg; $c$ – m·s$^{-1}$; $\\theta$ – rad (or $^{\circ}$).

Derivation (outline)

  1. Initial photon: energy $E = h\\nu = \\frac{hc}{\\lambda}$, momentum $p = \\frac{h}{\\lambda}$.
  2. After scattering at angle $\\theta$: energy $E' = \\frac{hc}{\\lambda'}$, momentum $p' = \\frac{h}{\\lambda'}$.
  3. Electron initially at rest: $E_e = m_e c^2$, $p_e = 0$.
  4. Apply conservation of energy: $\\frac{hc}{\\lambda}+m_e c^2 = \\frac{hc}{\\lambda'}+\\sqrt{(p_e c)^2 + (m_e c^2)^2}$.
  5. Apply conservation of momentum in x and y directions and eliminate $p_e$ to obtain $\\Delta \\lambda = \\frac{h}{m_e c}(1-\\cos\\theta)$.

Numerical Example

Given $\\lambda = 0.071\\,\\text{nm}$ and $\\theta = 60^{\\circ}$, find $\\lambda'$.

Constants: $\\frac{h}{m_e c}=2.426\\times10^{-12}\\,\\text{m}$ (Compton wavelength of electron).

$$\\Delta \\lambda = 2.426\\times10^{-12}(1-\\cos60^{\\circ}) = 2.426\\times10^{-12}\\times0.5 = 1.213\\times10^{-12}\\,\\text{m}$$

$$\\lambda' = \\lambda + \\Delta \\lambda = 0.071\\times10^{-9} + 1.213\\times10^{-12} = 7.12\\times10^{-11}\\,\\text{m}$$

Thus the scattered photon’s wavelength increases by $1.21\\times10^{-12}\\,$m.

de Broglie hypothesis and matter waves

de Broglie hypothesis – Any particle having momentum $p$ exhibits wave‑like behaviour with a wavelength

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

Here $h$ is Planck’s constant ($6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$), $p$ is linear momentum (kg·m/s) and $\lambda$ is the de Broglie wavelength (m).

Derivation (outline)

  • For a photon, $E = h\nu$ and $E = pc$ → $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$.
  • Assuming the same relation holds for massive particles, replace $p = mv$ (non‑relativistic) \to obtain $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$.
  • Relativistic form uses $p = \gamma mv$ where $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}}}$.

Key formulas & SI units

QuantitySymbolFormulaSI unit
Planck constant$h$$6.626\times10^{-34}$J·s
Momentum$p$$mv$ (non‑relativistic)kg·m/s
de Broglie wavelength$\lambda$$\frac{h}{p}$m

Example

An electron is accelerated through a potential difference $V = 150\,\text{V}$. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

Energy gained $= eV$ where $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. For non‑relativistic electrons, kinetic energy $= \frac{p^{2}}{2m}$.

$$\frac{p^{2}}{2m}=eV \;\Rightarrow\; p=\sqrt{2meV}$$

Hence

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$$

Substituting $m=9.11\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, $e=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, $V=150\,\text{V}$ and $h=6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$,

$$\lambda = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2\times9.11\times10^{-31}\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times150}} \approx 1.0\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}$$

Thus the accelerated electron behaves like a wave of wavelength about $1\,\text{Å}$, comparable to inter‑atomic spacings, which explains electron diffraction experiments.

Davisson‑Germer experiment

Davisson‑Germer Experiment (1927) – First direct proof of the wave nature of electrons. A beam of electrons is accelerated through a potential $V$ and incident on a nickel crystal. The scattered electrons produce intensity maxima at specific angles, obeying Bragg’s law, confirming de Broglie’s hypothesis.

Key Relations

  • De Broglie wavelength: $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$  (SI: $\lambda$ in m, $h$ in J·s, $m$ in kg, $e$ in C, $V$ in V).
  • Bragg’s law for crystal planes spaced $d$: $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$, $n=1,2,\dots$.
  • Momentum of electron: $p = \sqrt{2meV}$.

Derivation of $\lambda$ from accelerating voltage

Electron kinetic energy $K = eV = \frac{p^{2}}{2m}$ \Rightarrow $p = \sqrt{2meV}$, then $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$.

SI Units

QuantitySymbolSI Unit
Planck constant$h$J·s
Electron mass$m$kg
Elementary charge$e$C
Accelerating potential$V$V
Wavelength$\lambda$m

Solved Example

Electrons are accelerated through $V=150\,\text{V}$. Find $\lambda$ and the first‑order diffraction angle $\theta_{1}$ for Ni crystal with $d=0.215\,\text{nm}$.

Using $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$:

$$\lambda = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.60\times10^{-19})(150)}} \approx 1.00\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}=0.100\,\text{nm}$$

Apply Bragg’s law for $n=1$:

$$2d\sin\theta_{1}=\lambda \Rightarrow \sin\theta_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{2d}=\frac{0.100}{2\times0.215}=0.233$$

Thus $\theta_{1}=\sin^{-1}(0.233)\approx13.5^{\circ}$.

The observed peak at $\approx13.5^{\circ}$ matched the predicted value, confirming $\lambda$ from de Broglie’s relation.

Matter wave diffraction and electron microscopy

Definition: Matter exhibits wave–like behaviour characterised by the de Broglie wavelength.

The de Broglie relation is

$$\\lambda = \\frac{h}{p} = \\frac{h}{mv}$$

where $h$ (J·s) is Planck’s constant, $m$ (kg) the particle mass, $v$ (m·s$^{-1}$) its speed and $p$ (kg·m·s$^{-1}$) the momentum.

Diffraction condition for a slit of width $d$:

$$d\\sin\\theta = n\\lambda \quad (n=1,2,3\dots)$$

Electron microscope wavelength (non‑relativistic):

From kinetic energy $eV = \\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}$, $v = \\sqrt{\\frac{2eV}{m}}$ and

$$\\lambda = \\frac{h}{\\sqrt{2meV}}$$

SI units: $h$ – J·s, $e$ – C, $V$ – V, $m$ – kg, $\\lambda$ – m.

SymbolMeaningSI Unit
$\\lambda$de Broglie wavelengthm
$h$Planck’s constantJ·s
$m$mass of electronkg
$e$elementary chargeC
$V$accelerating potentialV

Example: Find $\\lambda$ of electrons accelerated through $V=100\\,\\text{kV}$ and the first‑order diffraction \angle for a slit $d=0.1\\,\\text{nm}$.

Solution:

  • Convert $V$: $100\\,\\text{kV}=1.0\\times10^{5}\\,\\text{V}$.
  • $\\lambda = \\frac{h}{\\sqrt{2meV}} = \\frac{6.626\\times10^{-34}}{\\sqrt{2(9.11\\times10^{-31})(1.602\\times10^{-19})(1.0\\times10^{5})}} \\approx 3.7\\times10^{-12}\\,\\text{m}$.
  • First order ($n=1$): $\\sin\\theta = \\frac{\\lambda}{d}=\\frac{3.7\\times10^{-12}}{1.0\\times10^{-10}}=0.037$ \rightarrow $\\theta \\approx 2.1^{\\circ}$.

Thus a 100 kV electron beam has a wavelength of about $3.7\\,$\pm and produces a measurable diffraction \angle of $2.1^{\\circ}$ for a 0.1 nm slit, enabling high‑resolution imaging in transmission electron microscopes.

Wave‑particle duality of photons and electrons

Dual nature of radiation & matter – Both photons and electrons exhibit wave‑particle duality. A particle shows wave‑like behaviour when its de‑Broglie wavelength $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$ is comparable to the dimensions of the experimental setup.

Key postulates

  • Einstein’s photon hypothesis: Energy of a photon $E = h\nu$ and momentum $p = \frac{h}{\lambda}$.
  • de Broglie hypothesis: Any moving particle of momentum $p$ has an associated wavelength $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$.

Important formulas

QuantityFormulaSI unit
Photon energy$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$J
Photon momentum$p = \frac{h}{\lambda}$kg·m·s$^{-1}$
de Broglie wavelength$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$m
Kinetic energy (electron)$K = \frac{p^{2}}{2m}=\frac{h^{2}}{2m\lambda^{2}}$J

Derivation (de Broglie wavelength)

Starting from $E = h\nu$ and $E = pc$ for a photon, replace $c$ by particle velocity $v$ and use $p = mv$. Hence $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$.

Solved example

Problem: An electron is accelerated through a potential difference of $150\,\text{V}$. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

Solution: Kinetic energy $K = eV = (1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{C})(150\,\text{V}) = 2.40\times10^{-17}\,\text{J}$. Momentum $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ with $m = 9.11\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, giving $p = 5.23\times10^{-24}\,\text{kg·m·s}^{-1}$. Thus $\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{5.23\times10^{-24}} = 1.27\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}$.

Thus photons and electrons can be treated as particles or waves depending on the experimental context, a cornerstone of quantum mechanics.

Applications of wave‑particle duality in modern physics

Wave‑Particle Duality – Every quantum entity exhibits both wave‑like and particle‑like properties. The wave aspect is characterized by the de Broglie wavelength

SymbolMeaningSI Unit
$\lambda$de Broglie wavelengthmetre (m)
$h$Planck’s constantjoule·second (J·s)
$p$linear momentumkilogram·metre per second (kg·m·s$^{-1}$)

Key relations

  • $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$
  • For a non‑relativistic particle $p = mv$, so $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$.
  • Photon energy $E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$, where $c$ is the speed of light.

Major Applications

  • Electron diffraction (Davisson‑Germer): confirms $\lambda = h/p$ for electrons.
  • X‑ray diffraction (Bragg’s law): $n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$ used \to determine crystal structures.
  • Photoelectric effect: $E_{max}=h\nu-\phi$ validates particle nature of light.
  • Compton scattering: wavelength shift $\Delta\lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)$.
  • Electron microscopy: short $\lambda$ of high‑energy electrons yields atomic‑scale imaging.

Solved Example

Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through $V = 150\,\text{V}$.

Energy gained $E = eV$, where $e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. Hence kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}=eV$ giving $p = \sqrt{2m_e eV}$.

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV}} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.60\times10^{-19})(150)}} \approx 1.0\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}$$

Thus accelerated electrons behave as waves with wavelength comparable to inter‑atomic spacings, enabling electron diffraction.
