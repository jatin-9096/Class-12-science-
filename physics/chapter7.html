Introduction to alternating current and its generation

Alternating Current (AC) – Introduction

AC is a time‑varying electric current that periodically reverses its direction. The standard sinusoidal form is

QuantityExpressionSI Unit
Instantaneous current$i = I_m \sin \omega t$A
Instantaneous voltage$v = V_m \sin \omega t$V
RMS current$I_{rms}=\frac{I_m}{\sqrt{2}}$A
RMS voltage$V_{rms}=\frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}}$V
Average power$P_{avg}=\frac{1}{2} V_m I_m \cos \phi = V_{rms} I_{rms} \cos \phi$W

Generation of AC

When a rectangular coil of $N$ turns, area $A$, rotates with angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform magnetic field $B$, the magnetic flux is $\Phi = N B A \cos \omega t$. By Faraday’s law, the induced emf is

$e = -\frac{d\Phi}{dt}= N B A \omega \sin \omega t$

Thus the generated emf is sinusoidal with peak $E_m = N B A \omega$.

Key Formulas

  • Peak value $X_m$ and RMS value $X_{rms}=\frac{X_m}{\sqrt{2}}$.
  • Phase difference $\phi$ between voltage and current.
  • Power factor $\cos \phi$.

Example

Find the rms voltage of an AC source whose peak voltage is $100\,\text{V}$.

Solution: $V_{rms}=\frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{100}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 70.7\,\text{V}$.

Sinusoidal alternating current: mathematical description and phasors

Definition: A sinusoidal alternating current (AC) is described by a time‑varying quantity of the form $i(t)=I_{m}\sin(\omega t+\phi)$, where $I_{m}$ is the peak (maximum) value, $\omega=2\pi f$ is the angular frequency (rad·s$^{-1}$), $f$ is the frequency (Hz) and $\phi$ is the phase angle (rad).

Phasor representation converts the sinusoid into a rotating vector in the complex plane: $\tilde I=I_{m}\angle\phi$, and the time function is recovered as $i(t)=\Re\{\tilde I e^{j\omega t}\}$. The same applies to voltage $v(t)=V_{m}\sin(\omega t+\theta)$ with phasor $\tilde V=V_{m}\angle\theta$.

Key formulas

  • RMS value: $I_{\text{rms}}=\frac{I_{m}}{\sqrt{2}}$, $V_{\text{rms}}=\frac{V_{m}}{\sqrt{2}}$.
  • Impedance of series $R$–$L$–$C$: $Z=R+jX$, where $X=\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}$ (ohm).
  • Ohm’s law for phasors: $\tilde I=\frac{\tilde V}{Z}$.
  • Average (real) power: $P=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi=\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos\phi$.

SI units

SymbolQuantitySI unit
$I$CurrentA
$V$VoltageV
$\omega$Angular frequencyrad·s$^{-1}$
$Z$Impedance\Omega

Solved example

Given a sinusoidal source $v(t)=100\sin(2\pi\,50\,t)$ V applied across a resistor $R=10\,\Omega$, find the rms voltage, rms current and average power.

  1. Peak voltage $V_{m}=100\,$V.
  2. RMS voltage $V_{\text{rms}}=\frac{100}{\sqrt{2}}=70.71\,$V.
  3. RMS current $I_{\text{rms}}=\frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R}=\frac{70.71}{10}=7.07\,$A.
  4. Average power $P=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}=70.71\times7.07\approx5.0\times10^{2}\,$W.

Thus $V_{\text{rms}}=70.71\,$V, $I_{\text{rms}}=7.07\,$A and $P=500\,$W.

RMS and average values of alternating quantities

Definition: The root‑mean‑square (RMS) value of a periodic quantity $x(t)$ is the square root of its mean square value over one complete cycle.

Mathematically, $$\text{RMS}= \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}x^{2}(t)\,dt}$$ where $T$ is the period. The average (mean) value is $$\overline{x}= \frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}x(t)\,dt$$.

For a sinusoid $x(t)=X_{0}\sin\omega t$ (or $X_{0}\cos\omega t$):

  • Mean square $=\frac{X_{0}^{2}}{2}$, hence $$\text{RMS}= \frac{X_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}$$.
  • Average over a full cycle is zero; over a half‑cycle (absolute value) $$\overline{|x|}= \frac{2X_{0}}{\pi}$$.

Key formulas

QuantityRMS valueAverage value (full cycle)
Voltage $v(t)$$V_{\text{rms}}= \dfrac{V_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}$$0$
Current $i(t)$$I_{\text{rms}}= \dfrac{I_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}$$0$

SI Units: Voltage – volt (V), Current – ampere (A), Power – watt (W). RMS values are expressed \in the same units as the instantaneous quantity.

Example: A resistor of $10\ \Omega$ is connected \to an AC source $v(t)=100\sin(2\pi 50t)\,$V. Find the RMS current and the average power dissipated.

Solution:

  • Peak voltage $V_{0}=100\,$V. $V_{\text{rms}}=100/\sqrt{2}=70.71\,$V.
  • RMS current $I_{\text{rms}}=V_{\text{rms}}/R=70.71/10=7.07\,$A.
  • Average power $P_{\text{avg}}=I_{\text{rms}}^{2}R= (7.07)^{2}\times10\approx 500\,$W.

Thus RMS quantities simplify AC analysis by allowing direct use of DC formulas.

Power in AC circuits: instantaneous, average, apparent, real and reactive power

Instantaneous Power: $p(t)=v(t)i(t)$ (unit: W). For sinusoidal sources $v=V_{m}\sin\omega t$, $i=I_{m}\sin(\omega t+\phi)$.

Average (Real) Power: $P_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}p(t)dt=\frac{1}{2}V_{m}I_{m}\cos\phi=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi$ (unit: W). $\cos\phi$ is the power factor.

Apparent Power: $S=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}$ (unit: VA).

Reactive Power: $Q=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\sin\phi$ (unit: var). Relationship: $S^{2}=P^{2}+Q^{2}$.

QuantitySymbolFormulaUnit
Instantaneous Power$p$$p=v i$W
Average (Real) Power$P$$P=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos\phi$W
Apparent Power$S$$S=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}$VA
Reactive Power$Q$$Q=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\sin\phi$var

Example:

  • Given $V_{\text{rms}}=230\,\text{V}$, $I_{\text{rms}}=5\,\text{A}$, power factor $\cos\phi=0.8$ (lagging).
  • Apparent power $S=230\times5=1150\,\text{VA}$.
  • Real power $P=S\cos\phi=1150\times0.8=920\,\text{W}$.
  • $\phi=\cos^{-1}(0.8)\approx36.87^{\circ}$, so $\sin\phi\approx0.6$.
  • Reactive power $Q=S\sin\phi=1150\times0.6=690\,\text{var}$.

Thus $S=1150\,\text{VA}$, $P=920\,\text{W}$, $Q=690\,\text{var}$ and power factor $=0.8$.

Phase relationships between voltage and current in resistive, inductive and capacitive circuits

Phase relationship between voltage and current

  • Resistive (R) circuit: $v(t)=V_0\sin\omega t$, $i(t)=I_0\sin\omega t$. Voltage and current are in phase ($\phi=0^\circ$). Power factor $=1$.
  • Inductive (L) circuit: $v(t)=L\frac{di}{dt}=V_0\sin\omega t$, $i(t)=I_0\sin(\omega t-\phi)$ with $\phi=+90^\circ$ (current lags voltage). $\tan\phi=\frac{\omega L}{R}$ for RL series.
  • Capacitive (C) circuit: $i(t)=C\frac{dv}{dt}=I_0\sin\omega t$, $v(t)=V_0\sin(\omega t-\phi)$ with $\phi=-90^\circ$ (current leads voltage). $\tan\phi=-\frac{1}{\omega C R}$ for RC series.

Key formulas

QuantityExpressionSI unit
Impedance $Z$$\sqrt{R^{2}+(\omega L)^{2}}$ (pure $L$) or $\sqrt{R^{2}+(\frac{1}{\omega C})^{2}}$ (pure $C$)$\Omega$
Phase angle $\phi$$\tan\phi=\frac{\omega L}{R}$ (RL) , $\tan\phi=-\frac{1}{\omega C R}$ (RC)radian or degree
Power factor$\cos\phi$—

Example

In a series RL circuit $R=50\ \Omega$, $L=0.2\ \text{H}$ and the supply frequency $f=50\ \text{Hz}$. Find $\phi$ and the power factor.

Solution: $\omega=2\pi f=2\pi\times50=100\pi\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$. $\tan\phi=\frac{\omega L}{R}= \frac{100\pi\times0.2}{50}= \frac{20\pi}{50}=0.4\pi\approx1.257$. $\phi=\arctan(1.257)\approx51.3^\circ$. Power factor $=\cos\phi\approx0.63$ (lagging).

Series LCR circuit: impedance, resonance and quality factor

Series LCR Circuit – Impedance, Resonance and Quality Factor

In a series LCR circuit the resistor $R$, inductor $L$ and capacitor $C$ are connected end‑to‑end and the same current $i(t)$ flows through each element. The total opposition to the alternating current is called the impedance $Z$.

  • Impedance: $Z = \sqrt{R^{2} + (X_{L} - X_{C})^{2}}$ where $X_{L}=\omega L$ (inductive reactance) and $X_{C}=\frac{1}{\omega C}$ (capacitive reactance). Unit: $\Omega$.
  • Phase angle: $\phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{X_{L}-X_{C}}{R}$; current lags voltage if $\phi>0$.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when $X_{L}=X_{C}$, i.e. $\omega_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$. At resonance:

  • $Z_{\text{res}} = R$ (minimum impedance).
  • Current amplitude $I_{\max}=\frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R}$.
  • Voltage across $L$ or $C$ can be $Q$ times the source voltage.

Quality Factor (Q)

$Q = \frac{\omega_{0} L}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega_{0} C R}=\frac{X_{L}}{R}=\frac{X_{C}}{R}$.

It measures the sharpness of resonance; higher $Q$ \u2192 narrower bandwidth $\Delta \omega = \frac{\omega_{0}}{Q}$.

QuantityExpressionSI Unit
Inductive reactance $X_{L}$$\omega L$$\Omega$
Capacitive reactance $X_{C}$$\frac{1}{\omega C}$$\Omega$
Resonant frequency $f_{0}$$\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$Hz
Quality factor $Q$$\frac{\omega_{0} L}{R}$dimensionless

Example

Given $R=10\,\Omega$, $L=0.2\,\text{H}$, $C=50\,\mu\text{F}$ and $V_{\text{rms}}=100\,\text{V}$, find the resonant frequency, current at resonance and $Q$.

Resonant angular frequency $\omega_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.2\times 50\times10^{-6}}}=1000\,\text{rad s}^{-1}$, so $f_{0}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}\approx159\,\text{Hz}$. At resonance $Z=R$, thus $I_{\max}=\frac{100}{10}=10\,\text{A}$. $Q=\frac{\omega_{0}L}{R}=\frac{1000\times0.2}{10}=20$.

Power factor, its correction and applications

Power Factor (PF) – the ratio of real power $P$ to apparent power $S$ in an AC circuit, $\text{PF}=\frac{P}{S}=\cos\phi$, where $\phi$ is the phase angle between voltage and current. SI unit: dimensionless (range $-1$ to $+1$). A lagging PF (<0) indicates inductive load, leading PF (>0) indicates capacitive load.

Key Relations

  • Apparent power $S = VI$ (VA)
  • Real power $P = VI\cos\phi$ (W)
  • Reactive power $Q = VI\sin\phi = P\tan\phi$ (VAR)
  • Power triangle: $S^{2}=P^{2}+Q^{2}$

PF Correction

To improve a lagging PF, a capacitor supplies leading reactive power $Q_{c}$, reducing net $Q$:

QuantityFormula
Initial reactive power $Q_{1}$$Q_{1}=P\tan\phi_{1}$
Desired reactive power $Q_{2}$$Q_{2}=P\tan\phi_{2}$
Capacitor reactive power $Q_{c}$$Q_{c}=Q_{1}-Q_{2}$
Capacitance $C$ (single‑phase)$C=\frac{Q_{c}}{2\pi f V^{2}}$

Example

A 3 kW motor operates at 230 V, 50 Hz with lagging PF $0.6$. Find the capacitance needed to raise PF to $0.9$.

Solution:

  1. Calculate $\phi_{1}=\cos^{-1}(0.6)$, $\tan\phi_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{1-0.6^{2}}}{0.6}=1.333$.
  2. $Q_{1}=P\tan\phi_{1}=3000\times1.333=4000\,\text{VAR}$.
  3. For PF $0.9$, $\tan\phi_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{1-0.9^{2}}}{0.9}=0.484$.
  4. $Q_{2}=3000\times0.484=1452\,\text{VAR}$.
  5. $Q_{c}=Q_{1}-Q_{2}=4000-1452=2548\,\text{VAR}$.
  6. $C=\frac{2548}{2\pi\times50\times230^{2}}\approx9.6\times10^{-6}\,\text{F}=9.6\,\mu\text{F}$.

Thus a $9.6\,\mu\text{F}$ capacitor connected across the motor will correct the PF to $0.9$.
