1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Definition and Generation of Alternating Current

Alternating current (AC) is a type of electric current in which the flow of charge periodically reverses direction. In most practical situations the variation of voltage and current with time is sinusoidal, and therefore the term “sinusoidal alternating current” is often used synonymously with AC. The instantaneous value of a sinusoidal voltage can be expressed as $v(t)=V_{m}\sin \omega t$, where $V_{m}$ is the peak (maximum) voltage, $\omega =2\pi f$ is the angular frequency and $f$ is the frequency in hertz. The corresponding current is $i(t)=I_{m}\sin (\omega t+\phi )$, where $I_{m}$ is the peak current and $\phi$ is the phase difference between voltage and current. The reversal of direction occurs because the source – typically an alternator or an electronic oscillator – produces a rotating magnetic field that induces an electromotive force (emf) according to Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction. In a rotating machine the emf generated in the armature windings varies sinusoidally with the angular position of the rotor, leading naturally to a sinusoidal output. The period $T$ of the waveform is the time taken for one complete cycle and is related to frequency by $T=1/f$. In the Indian power system, the standard frequency is $50\ \text{Hz}$, which means the direction of current changes $100$ times per second. The peak value $V_{m}$ is related to the root‑mean‑square (r.m.s.) value $V_{\text{rms}}$ by $V_{\text{rms}}=V_{m}/\sqrt{2}$, a relationship that follows from the definition of r.m.s. as the square root of the mean of the square of the instantaneous value over one complete cycle. Similarly, $I_{\text{rms}}=I_{m}/\sqrt{2}$. The r.m.s. values are of paramount importance because they allow AC quantities to be compared directly with their DC counterparts: a resistor dissipates the same power when a DC voltage $V_{\text{dc}}$ is applied as when an AC voltage of $V_{\text{rms}}=V_{\text{dc}}$ is applied. The average value of a pure sinusoid over a full cycle is zero, which reflects the fact that the positive and negative halves cancel each other. However, the average of the absolute value (or the average of the square) is non‑zero and leads to the concept of r.m.s. values. Understanding these fundamental definitions is essential before proceeding to more complex phenomena such as impedance, power factor, and resonance.

Physics · Core Law
Mathematical Representation and Impedance

The sinusoidal nature of AC permits a compact representation using complex numbers and phasors. A sinusoidal quantity $x(t)=X_{m}\sin (\omega t+\theta )$ can be represented by a rotating vector (phasor) $\tilde{X}=X_{m}\angle \theta$ in the complex plane. By Euler’s formula $e^{j\theta }=\cos \theta +j\sin \theta$, the time‑domain expression can be written as the real part of a complex exponential: $x(t)=\Re\{ \tilde{X}e^{j\omega t}\}$. This approach simplifies the analysis of circuits containing resistors ($R$), inductors ($L$) and capacitors ($C$). The voltage‑current relationship for each element in the phasor domain is linear and can be expressed as $ \tilde{V}=Z\tilde{I}$, where $Z$ is the complex impedance. For a resistor, $Z_R=R$ (purely real); for an inductor, $Z_L=j\omega L$ (purely imaginary and positive); for a capacitor, $Z_C=-j/(\omega C)$ (purely imaginary and negative). The total impedance of a series combination is the algebraic sum $$Z_{\text{series}}=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right).$$ For a parallel combination, the admittance $Y=1/Z$ is summed: $$Y_{\text{parallel}}=\frac{1}{R}+j\left(\omega C-\frac{1}{\omega L}\right),\qquad Z_{\text{parallel}}=\frac{1}{Y_{\text{parallel}}}.$$ The magnitude $|Z|$ determines the ratio of r.m.s. voltage to r.m.s. current, while the argument $\arg(Z)=\phi$ gives the phase difference between them. The phase angle is positive for inductive circuits (current lags voltage) and negative for capacitive circuits (current leads voltage). The concept of reactance $X=\omega L$ for inductors and $X_C=1/(\omega C)$ for capacitors quantifies the opposition to the change of current due to stored magnetic or electric energy. The total reactance $X$ of a series $R$–$L$–$C$ circuit is $X=\omega L-1/(\omega C)$. When $X=0$, the circuit is said to be at resonance, a condition explored in detail later. The use of complex impedance enables the application of Ohm’s law, Kirchhoff’s voltage and current laws, and network theorems in the same way as for DC circuits, but with the added benefit of automatically accounting for phase relationships.

Physics · Core Law
Power in AC Circuits: Real, Reactive and Apparent Power

The instantaneous power delivered to a load is the product of instantaneous voltage and current: $p(t)=v(t)i(t)$. Substituting the sinusoidal expressions $v(t)=V_{m}\sin \omega t$ and $i(t)=I_{m}\sin (\omega t+\phi )$ yields $$p(t)=V_{m}I_{m}\sin \omega t\;\sin (\omega t+\phi ).$$ Using the trigonometric identity $\sin A\sin B=\frac{1}{2}[\cos (A-B)-\cos (A+B)]$, the instantaneous power can be written as $$p(t)=\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\big[\cos \phi -\cos (2\omega t+\phi )\big].$$ The first term $\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos \phi$ is a constant (time‑independent) component, while the second term oscillates at twice the supply frequency and averages to zero over a full cycle. The average (real) power $P$ is therefore $$P=\frac{V_{m}I_{m}}{2}\cos \phi =V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\cos \phi,$$ where $\cos \phi$ is called the power factor. The power factor indicates the fraction of apparent power that is actually converted into useful work (real power). The apparent power $S$ is defined as the product of the r.m.s. voltage and r.m.s. current, irrespective of phase: $$S=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\quad [\text{VA}].$$ The reactive power $Q$ represents the power that oscillates between source and reactive elements without being dissipated: $$Q=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}\sin \phi\quad [\text{VAR}].$$ These three quantities satisfy the power triangle relationship $$S^{2}=P^{2}+Q^{2}.$$ In purely resistive circuits $\phi=0$, $\cos \phi=1$, $Q=0$, and all supplied power is real. In purely inductive or capacitive circuits $\phi=\pm 90^{\circ}$, $\cos \phi=0$, $P=0$, and the power is entirely reactive. The sign of $Q$ distinguishes inductive ($Q>0$, current lags) from capacitive ($Q<0$, current leads) loads. Power factor correction, typically achieved by adding capacitors to an inductive load, reduces $|\phi|$, thereby decreasing $Q$, improving the efficiency of power transmission, and lowering the current drawn for a given real power. Understanding these concepts is essential for designing efficient AC distribution systems and for interpreting instrument readings such as those of wattmeters, voltmeters, and power factor meters.

Physics · Core Law
Resonance in RLC Circuits and Quality Factor

Resonance occurs in an $R$–$L$–$C$ circuit when the inductive reactance exactly cancels the capacitive reactance, i.e., when $\omega L=1/(\omega C)$. Solving for the angular frequency gives the resonant angular frequency $$\omega_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad f_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}.$$ At resonance the total reactance $X$ becomes zero and the impedance of a series circuit reduces to the resistance alone: $Z_{\text{series}}=R$. Consequently, the current reaches its maximum value $I_{\text{max}}=V_{\text{rms}}/R$, and the voltage across $L$ and $C$ can be many times larger than the source voltage because the individual reactances are large but opposite in sign. In a parallel resonant circuit the admittance $Y$ becomes minimum at $\omega_{0}$, and the circuit behaves as a high‑impedance branch, drawing minimal current from the source. The sharpness of the resonance is quantified by the quality factor $Q$, defined for a series circuit as $$Q=\frac{\omega_{0}L}{R}=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}RC}.$$ A high $Q$ indicates a narrow bandwidth $\Delta f$ over which the circuit responds strongly. The bandwidth is related to $Q$ by $$\Delta f=\frac{f_{0}}{Q}.$$ This relationship is crucial in the design of tuned circuits such as radio receivers, where selectivity (the ability to isolate a particular frequency) is governed by $Q$. The phase angle $\phi$ also varies rapidly around resonance: it passes through zero at $\omega_{0}$, being negative (capacitive) for $\omega<\omega_{0}$ and positive (inductive) for $\omega>\omega_{0}$. In practical circuits, resistance in the coil and dielectric losses in the capacitor limit $Q$, and the presence of parasitic elements can shift the resonant frequency slightly. Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In alternating current (AC) circuits the voltage and current vary sinusoidally with time. The most fundamental expressions are the instantaneous voltage $v(t)$ and instantaneous current $i(t)$ for a source of angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, where $f$ is the frequency in hertz. For a sinusoidal source of peak (maximum) value $V_{\rm m}$ the voltage is written as $$v(t)=V_{\rm m}\sin(\omega t+\phi_v)$$ where $\phi_v$ is the phase angle of the voltage with respect to a chosen reference. Similarly, the instantaneous current is $$i(t)=I_{\rm m}\sin(\omega t+\phi_i)$$ with $I_{\rm m}$ the peak current and $\phi_i$ its phase angle. The phase difference $\phi = \phi_v-\phi_i$ determines whether the current leads or lags the voltage. In many practical problems we work with the root‑mean‑square (RMS) values because they directly relate to power dissipation in resistive elements. The RMS value of a sinusoid is defined as the square root of the time‑average of the square of the quantity over one complete cycle. For voltage, $$V_{\rm rms}= \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v^2(t)\,dt} = \frac{V_{\rm m}}{\sqrt{2}}$$ and similarly for current, $$I_{\rm rms}= \frac{I_{\rm m}}{\sqrt{2}}.$$ These relations are derived by evaluating the integral $$\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)\,dt = \frac{1}{2},$$ which holds for any phase $\phi$ because the average of $\sin^2$ over a full period is $1/2$. The RMS values are the quantities that appear in the familiar power formula $P_{\rm av}=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi$, where $\cos\phi$ is the power factor.

Derivation of the average power in a sinusoidal AC circuit
The instantaneous power delivered to a load is the product of instantaneous voltage and current: $$p(t)=v(t)i(t)=V_{\rm m}I_{\rm m}\sin(\omega t+\phi_v)\sin(\omega t+\phi_i).$$ Using the trigonometric identity $$\sin A\sin B=\frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)],$$ we obtain $$p(t)=\frac{V_{\rm m}I_{\rm m}}{2}\big[\cos(\phi_v-\phi_i)-\cos(2\omega t+\phi_v+\phi_i)\big].$$ The first term, $\frac{V_{\rm m}I_{\rm m}}{2}\cos\phi$, is a constant, while the second term oscillates at twice the supply frequency and averages to zero over a full cycle. Therefore the average (or real) power is $$P_{\rm av}= \frac{V_{\rm m}I_{\rm m}}{2}\cos\phi = V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi,$$ establishing the importance of the power factor $\cos\phi$ in AC power calculations. This derivation is central to the CBSE curriculum because it connects the phase relationship between voltage and current to the actual usable power.

Impedance of series LCR circuit
Consider a series circuit containing a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$, and a capacitor $C$, all connected to a sinusoidal source of angular frequency $\omega$. The voltage drops across each element are: \begin{align*} v_R(t) &= i(t)R,\\ v_L(t) &= L\frac{di(t)}{dt},\\ v_C(t) &= \frac{1}{C}\int i(t)\,dt. \end{align*} In the phasor domain, differentiation corresponds to multiplication by $j\omega$ and integration to division by $j\omega$, where $j=\sqrt{-1}$. Hence the phasor voltages become \begin{align*} \tilde V_R &= I R,\\ \tilde V_L &= j\omega L I,\\ \tilde V_C &= \frac{I}{j\omega C}= -j\frac{I}{\omega C}. \end{align*} Adding these phasor voltages gives the total phasor voltage $\tilde V$ across the series combination: $$\tilde V = I\big(R + j\omega L - j\frac{1}{\omega C}\big)=I Z,$$ where the complex quantity $$Z = R + j\big(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\big)$$ is called the impedance of the series LCR circuit. The magnitude of $Z$ is $$|Z| = \sqrt{R^2+\big(\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}\big)^2}$$ and the phase angle $\phi$ (voltage leading current) is $$\phi = \tan^{-1}\!\Bigg(\frac{\omega L-\frac{1}{\omega C}}{R}\Bigg).$$ These expressions are derived directly from Kirchhoff’s voltage law applied in the phasor domain and are indispensable for solving any AC network problem in the CBSE syllabus.

Resonance condition in series LCR circuit
Resonance occurs when the reactive part of the impedance vanishes, i.e. when the inductive reactance $X_L=\omega L$ equals the capacitive reactance $X_C=1/(\omega C)$. Setting $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$ gives the resonant angular frequency $$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.$$ The corresponding resonant frequency in hertz is $$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}.$$ At resonance the impedance reduces to the pure resistance $Z=R$, the current reaches its maximum value $I_{\rm max}=V_{\rm rms}/R$, and the power factor becomes unity ($\cos\phi=1$). The derivation follows directly from the condition $X_L-X_C=0$, and it is a cornerstone concept for understanding tuned circuits, radio receivers, and many practical AC applications.

Derivation of quality factor (Q) for a series resonant circuit
The quality factor quantifies how sharply a circuit responds at resonance. For a series LCR circuit, $$Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 R C}.$$ To derive this, start from the definition $$Q = \frac{\text{reactive power at resonance}}{\text{average power dissipated}} = \frac{X_L}{R} = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R}.$$ Using the resonance condition $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, we can replace $L$ by $1/(\omega_0^2 C)$, leading to the alternative form $Q = 1/(\omega_0 R C)$. This relationship is frequently asked in CBSE board examinations because it links the three fundamental components $R$, $L$, and $C$ to the bandwidth of the resonant peak.

Power in purely inductive and purely capacitive circuits
For a pure inductor ($R=0$, $C\to\infty$) the voltage leads the current by $90^\circ$, i.e. $\phi=+90^\circ$, so $\cos\phi=0$. Consequently the average power $$P_{\rm av}=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos 90^\circ = 0.$$ The same result holds for a pure capacitor where the current leads the voltage by $90^\circ$ ($\phi=-90^\circ$). Although the average power is zero, energy is continuously exchanged between the source and the reactive element, which is evident from the instantaneous power expression $p(t)=V_{\rm m}I_{\rm m}\sin(\omega t)\sin(\omega t\pm 90^\circ)$. The derivation emphasizes the role of phase difference in determining real power.

Derivation of the expression for apparent power, reactive power and power factor
In AC circuits the complex power $\tilde S$ is defined as $$\tilde S = \tilde V \tilde I^*,$$ where the asterisk denotes complex conjugation. Writing $\tilde V = V_{\rm rms}\angle\phi_v$ and $\tilde I = I_{\rm rms}\angle\phi_i$, we obtain $$\tilde S = V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\angle(\phi_v-\phi_i)=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}(\cos\phi+j\sin\phi).$$ The real part of $\tilde S$ is the average (real) power $P$, and the imaginary part is the reactive power $Q$: $$P = V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi,\qquad Q = V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\sin\phi.$$ The magnitude $| \tilde S| = V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}$ is called the apparent power $S$, measured in volt‑ampere (VA). The power factor is defined as $$\text{PF} = \cos\phi = \frac{P}{S}.$$ This derivation uses Euler’s formula and the concept of phasors, both of which are part of the CBSE AC curriculum.

Derivation of the voltage across an inductor in terms of RMS values
Starting from Faraday’s law $v_L(t)=L\,di/dt$, let the current be $i(t)=I_{\rm m}\sin(\omega t)$. Differentiating, $$v_L(t)=L I_{\rm m}\omega\cos(\omega t)=L\omega I_{\rm m}\sin\!\Big(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{2}\Big).$$ The peak voltage across the inductor is therefore $V_{L{\rm m}} = \omega L I_{\rm m}$. Converting to RMS, $$V_{L{\rm rms}} = \frac{V_{L{\rm m}}}{\sqrt{2}} = \omega L \frac{I_{\rm m}}{\sqrt{2}} = \omega L I_{\rm rms}.$$ This shows that the inductive reactance $X_L$ is defined as $X_L=\omega L$, giving the compact relation $V_{L{\rm rms}} = I_{\rm rms}X_L$. The same procedure applied to a capacitor yields $V_{C{\rm rms}} = I_{\rm rms}X_C$ with $X_C=1/(\omega C)$.
