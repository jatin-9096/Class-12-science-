Topic 1.1 – 1.4 · NCERT Basics

1. Electric Charge, Conductors, Insulators & Charging

Electrostatics is the branch of physics that deals with forces, fields, and potentials arising from static (stationary) charges. When you comb dry hair with a plastic comb or pull off a synthetic sweater in winter, you hear a faint crackle and see sparks—that is static electricity caused by the transfer of electrons from one surface to another.

Definition: Electric Charge ($q$)

Electric charge is an intrinsic property of elementary particles (such as electrons and protons) by virtue of which they exert and experience electrostatic forces.

Nature: Scalar quantity · SI Unit: Coulomb ($\text{C}$) · Dimensional Formula: $[\text{A T}]$ · Elementary Charge: $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$

Experiments show there are only two kinds of charges in nature, named positive and negative by Benjamin Franklin:

  • Like charges repel each other ($+$ repels $+$, $-$ repels $-$).
  • Unlike charges attract each other ($+$ attracts $-$).
  • A body becomes positively charged when it loses electrons (slight decrease in mass), and negatively charged when it gains electrons (slight increase in mass). Protons remain locked inside the nucleus and are never transferred during ordinary charging.

Definition: Conductors & Insulators

Conductors are substances that allow electric charges to move freely through them because they have a large number of loosely bound free electrons (e.g., metals like copper, silver, aluminium, human body, earth).
Insulators (Dielectrics) are substances that offer very high resistance to the passage of electricity because their electrons are tightly bound to parent atoms (e.g., glass, rubber, plastic, dry wood, porcelain).

Important distinction: If you place extra charge on a conductor, it immediately spreads over its entire outer surface. If you place charge on an insulator, it stays trapped right where you put it.

Three Methods of Charging a Body

  • 1. Charging by Friction: Rubbing two suitable insulating bodies transfers electrons from the material with lower work function to the one with higher work function (e.g., glass rod rubbed with silk → glass becomes $+$, silk becomes $-$).
  • 2. Charging by Conduction: Touching a charged conductor to an uncharged conductor causes charge to flow until both reach the same electric potential. Both end up with the same sign of charge.
  • 3. Charging by Induction: Bringing a charged body near an isolated neutral conductor without touching it. The near end develops an opposite bound charge and the far end develops a similar free charge. Earthing (grounding) the far end lets the free charge escape to earth; removing the earth connection first and then the inducing rod leaves the conductor permanently charged with the opposite sign.

Definition: Earthing (Grounding)

The process of sharing the excess charge of a body with the Earth through a conducting wire. Because the Earth is an enormous conductor, it can absorb or supply virtually unlimited charge without any noticeable change in its own potential.

CBSE Concept Check: Can two similarly charged bodies ever attract each other? Yes—if one body has a very large charge and the other has a very small charge of the same sign, the strongly charged body induces an opposite charge on the nearer side of the smaller body, and the resulting attraction can exceed the weak repulsion! That is why repulsion is the surest test of electrification, not attraction.
Topic 1.5 · Fundamental Laws

2. Basic Properties of Electric Charge

Definition 1: Additivity of Electric Charge

If a system contains $n$ point charges $q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots, q_n$, the total charge of the system is simply their algebraic sum (keeping proper $+$ and $-$ signs): $$q_{\text{total}} = q_1 + q_2 + q_3 + \dots + q_n$$

Definition 2: Conservation of Electric Charge

The total electric charge of an isolated system always remains constant. Charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another, or created/destroyed in equal and opposite pairs (e.g., pair production: $\gamma \to e^- + e^+$).

Definition 3: Quantisation of Electric Charge

All free charges observed in nature are always an integral multiple of a basic unit of charge $e$ (the magnitude of charge on an electron or proton): $$q = \pm n e \qquad \text{where } n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots \text{ and } e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$$

Why does charge look continuous at the macroscopic level? A charge of just $1\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ contains $n = \frac{10^{-6}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 6.25 \times 10^{12}$ electrons. Adding or removing a few electrons makes zero noticeable difference at that scale, so for large charges we ignore the "graininess" and treat charge as continuous. At the atomic/microscopic level, however, quantisation is strict and cannot be ignored.

Definition 4: Invariance of Charge

The magnitude of electric charge on a particle does not depend on its speed ($q_{\text{moving}} = q_{\text{rest}}$). By contrast, relativistic mass increases with velocity ($m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$).

Worked Example 1 (NCERT): How many electrons must be removed from a neutral metal sphere to give it a positive charge of $+1\text{ C}$?

Solution: Using $q = ne$: $$n = \frac{q}{e} = \frac{1\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} = 6.25 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}$$ Since the sphere needs a positive charge, $6.25 \times 10^{18}$ electrons must be removed from it.
Topic 1.6 · Inverse-Square Law

3. Coulomb's Law, Permittivity & Vector Form

Definition: Coulomb's Law

The electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion between two stationary point charges in vacuum is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the two charges, inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, and acts along the straight line joining the two charges.

$$F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$
  • $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ C}^2\text{ N}^{-1}\text{ m}^{-2}$ (Permittivity of free space / vacuum).
  • Dimensional formula of $\varepsilon_0$: $[\text{M}^{-1}\text{L}^{-3}\text{T}^{4}\text{A}^{2}]$.
  • $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^9\text{ N m}^2\text{ C}^{-2}$ (in vacuum).

Definition: One Coulomb ($1\text{ C}$)

One coulomb is that charge which, when placed at a distance of $1\text{ m}$ from an equal and similar charge in vacuum, repels it with a force of $9 \times 10^9\text{ N}$.

Coulomb's Law in Vector Form (Derivation & Significance)

Let two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ be located at position vectors $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ from origin $O$. Define the displacement vector from $q_1$ to $q_2$ as $\vec{r}_{21} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1$, and from $q_2$ to $q_1$ as $\vec{r}_{12} = \vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2 = -\vec{r}_{21}$.

The unit vectors are $\hat{r}_{21} = \frac{\vec{r}_{21}}{r}$ and $\hat{r}_{12} = \frac{\vec{r}_{12}}{r} = -\hat{r}_{21}$, where $r = |\vec{r}_{21}| = |\vec{r}_{12}|$.

Force on $q_2$ due to $q_1$: $$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\hat{r}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3}(\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1)$$ Force on $q_1$ due to $q_2$: $$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\hat{r}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}(-\hat{r}_{21}) = -\vec{F}_{21}$$

Two crucial conclusions from the vector form:

  1. Obeys Newton's Third Law: $\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$—the two forces are always equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, regardless of whether the charges have equal or unequal magnitudes.
  2. Central Force: The force always acts along the line joining the centres of the two charges. If $q_1 q_2 > 0$ (like charges), $\vec{F}_{21}$ is along $\hat{r}_{21}$ (repulsive). If $q_1 q_2 < 0$ (unlike charges), $\vec{F}_{21}$ is along $-\hat{r}_{21}$ (attractive).

Definition: Dielectric Constant / Relative Permittivity ($K$ or $\varepsilon_r$)

When the two charges are placed in an insulating medium of permittivity $\varepsilon$, the force drops to $F_{\text{med}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon}\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$. The ratio of permittivity of the medium to permittivity of vacuum is called the dielectric constant ($K$): $$K = \varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{F_{\text{vac}}}{F_{\text{med}}}$$ For vacuum, $K = 1$; for air, $K \approx 1.00059$; for pure water, $K \approx 80$; for metals, $K = \infty$.

Limitations of Coulomb's Law: Valid only for point charges that are stationary (or very slowly moving), and breaks down at nuclear distances ($r < 10^{-15}\text{ m}$) where strong nuclear forces dominate.

Topic 1.7 · Multi-Charge Systems

4. Forces Between Multiple Charges: Principle of Superposition

Definition: Principle of Superposition

The net electrostatic force on any given charge due to a number of other charges is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted on it by each charge taken one at a time. The force between any two charges is completely unaffected by the presence of other charges.

For a system of $n$ charges $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n$, the net force $\vec{F}_1$ on charge $q_1$ is:

$$\vec{F}_1 = \vec{F}_{12} + \vec{F}_{13} + \dots + \vec{F}_{1n} = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{1i}^2}\hat{r}_{1i}$$
Numerical Strategy: Whenever 3 or more charges are arranged on a triangle or square, never add forces directly as scalars. Find the magnitude of each force using Coulomb's law, mark its direction (push or pull), and then combine them using the parallelogram law $F = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2 + 2F_1 F_2 \cos\theta}$ or by resolving into $x$ and $y$ components.
Topic 1.8 – 1.9 · Field Concept

5. Electric Field & Electric Field Lines

Instead of imagining one charge magically pulling another across empty space ("action at a distance"), Faraday introduced the concept of a field: a source charge $Q$ modifies the space around it by creating an electric field $\vec{E}$, and any other charge $q_0$ placed in that space interacts locally with that field.

Definition: Electric Field Intensity ($\vec{E}$)

The electric field at a point is defined as the electrostatic force experienced per unit vanishingly small positive test charge ($q_0 \to 0$) placed at that point: $$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}$$

Nature: Vector quantity · SI Unit: $\text{N C}^{-1}$ or $\text{V m}^{-1}$ · Dimensions: $[\text{M L T}^{-3}\text{A}^{-1}]$

Why must $q_0 \to 0$? If the test charge $q_0$ were large, it would exert a strong force back on the source charges and shift them from their original positions, changing the very field we are trying to measure! Taking $q_0 \to 0$ ensures the source distribution stays undisturbed.

Electric field due to a single point charge $Q$ at distance $r$: $$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2}\hat{r}$$ Force on any charge $q$ placed in an electric field $\vec{E}$: $$\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$$ (For $+q$, force is in the direction of $\vec{E}$; for $-q$, force is opposite to $\vec{E}$.)

Physical Significance of Electric Field: In electrostatics it is a convenient book-keeping tool, but in electrodynamics (moving/accelerating charges), the electric field is a real physical entity that carries energy and momentum and propagates through vacuum at the finite speed of light $c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

Definition: Electric Field Lines

An electric field line is an imaginary smooth curve drawn in an electric field such that the tangent drawn at any point on the curve gives the direction of the net electric field at that point.

7 Essential Properties of Electric Field Lines (CBSE Favourite)

  1. They start from positive charges and terminate on negative charges. For a single isolated charge, they start or end at infinity.
  2. In a charge-free region, field lines are continuous curves without any sudden breaks.
  3. Two electric field lines can NEVER intersect each other. Reason: If they intersected, two tangents could be drawn at the point of intersection, meaning two different directions of the net electric field at the same point—which is physically impossible.
  4. Electrostatic field lines NEVER form closed loops. Reason: The electrostatic field is conservative in nature (work done in a closed loop is zero).
  5. The relative closeness (crowding) of field lines represents the magnitude of field strength: crowded lines = strong field; widely spaced lines = weak field.
  6. Equidistant, parallel, straight field lines represent a uniform electric field.
  7. Electric field lines always enter or leave the surface of a conductor perpendicularly ($90^\circ$), and there are no field lines inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium.

6. Electric Dipole: Axial & Equatorial Field Derivations

Definition: Electric Dipole & Electric Dipole Moment ($\vec{p}$)

An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite point charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by a small fixed distance $2a$.
The electric dipole moment ($\vec{p}$) is a vector measuring the strength of the dipole, equal to the product of the magnitude of either charge and the separation $2a$: $$\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$$

Direction (Physics convention): From $-q$ to $+q$ · SI Unit: $\text{C m}$ · Net charge of dipole: Zero ($+q - q = 0$)

Note: Although the total charge of a dipole is zero, its electric field is not zero because the two charges are located at slightly different positions, so their fields do not completely cancel.

Derivation 1: Electric Field on the Axial Line of a Dipole

Consider an electric dipole with charges $-q$ at $A$ and $+q$ at $B$, separated by distance $2a$, with midpoint $O$. Let $P$ be an observation point on the axial line at distance $r$ from the centre $O$ (on the side of $+q$). Let $\hat{p}$ be the unit vector along $\vec{p}$ (from $A$ to $B$).

  1. Distances from $P$: Distance of $P$ from $+q$ is $BP = (r - a)$. Distance of $P$ from $-q$ is $AP = (r + a)$.
  2. Electric field at $P$ due to $+q$ (directed away from $+q$, i.e., along $\hat{p}$): $$\vec{E}_{+q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(r - a)^2}\hat{p}$$
  3. Electric field at $P$ due to $-q$ (directed towards $-q$, i.e., along $-\hat{p}$): $$\vec{E}_{-q} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(r + a)^2}\hat{p}$$
  4. Net electric field at $P$ by superposition ($\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \vec{E}_{+q} + \vec{E}_{-q}$): $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{1}{(r + a)^2}\right]\hat{p}$$ Taking LCM $(r - a)^2(r + a)^2 = (r^2 - a^2)^2$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{(r + a)^2 - (r - a)^2}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}\right]\hat{p}$$ Expanding $(r^2 + 2ar + a^2) - (r^2 - 2ar + a^2) = 4ar$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{4ar}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}\hat{p} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2(q \cdot 2a)r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}\hat{p}$$ Since $p = q \cdot 2a$ and $\vec{p} = p\hat{p}$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\vec{p}r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}$$
  5. Special Case — Short (Ideal) Dipole ($r \gg a$): Neglecting $a^2$ in comparison to $r^2$, $(r^2 - a^2)^2 \approx r^4$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2\vec{p}}{r^3}$$
Key Takeaway (Axial): $\vec{E}_{\text{axial}}$ points in the same direction as $\vec{p}$ (angle between $\vec{E}_{\text{axial}}$ and $\vec{p}$ is $0^\circ$), and falls off as $1/r^3$ (faster than a point charge's $1/r^2$).

Derivation 2: Electric Field on the Equatorial Line (Perpendicular Bisector)

Consider the same dipole ($-q$ at $A$, $+q$ at $B$, length $2a$, centre $O$). Let $P$ be a point on the equatorial plane at perpendicular distance $r$ from $O$.

  1. Distances from $P$: In right triangles $\triangle AOP$ and $\triangle BOP$, $$AP = BP = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$$ Let $\angle PAB = \angle PBA = \theta$.
  2. Magnitudes of individual fields at $P$: $$E_{+q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2 + a^2} \quad (\text{along } BP \text{ produced})$$ $$E_{-q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2 + a^2} \quad (\text{along } PA)$$ Hence $E_{+q} = E_{-q}$.
  3. Resolving into horizontal and vertical components:
    • Vertical components (perpendicular to dipole axis): $E_{+q}\sin\theta$ acts upwards and $E_{-q}\sin\theta$ acts downwards. Since $E_{+q} = E_{-q}$, they are equal and opposite and cancel out completely.
    • Horizontal components (parallel to dipole axis): $E_{+q}\cos\theta$ and $E_{-q}\cos\theta$ both act from $+q$ towards $-q$ (i.e., along $-\hat{p}$), so they add up.
  4. Net electric field at $P$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{eq}} = -(E_{+q}\cos\theta + E_{-q}\cos\theta)\hat{p} = -2 E_{+q}\cos\theta\,\hat{p}$$ From right triangle $\triangle AOP$, $\cos\theta = \frac{\text{Base}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} = \frac{a}{(r^2 + a^2)^{1/2}}$. Substituting both: $$\vec{E}_{\text{eq}} = -2\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2 + a^2}\right)\left(\frac{a}{(r^2 + a^2)^{1/2}}\right)\hat{p}$$ $$\vec{E}_{\text{eq}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q(2a)}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}\hat{p} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec{p}}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}$$
  5. Special Case — Short Dipole ($r \gg a$): Neglecting $a^2$, $(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2} \approx r^3$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{eq}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec{p}}{r^3}$$
Key Takeaway (Equatorial): $\vec{E}_{\text{eq}}$ is directed antiparallel to $\vec{p}$ (angle is $180^\circ$). For a short dipole at the same distance $r$, the ratio of magnitudes is: $$\frac{E_{\text{axial}}}{E_{\text{eq}}} = 2$$
Topic 1.12 · Derivation 3

7. Electric Dipole in a Uniform External Electric Field

Derivation 3: Torque Acting on an Electric Dipole

Place a permanent electric dipole of dipole moment $\vec{p} = q(2\vec{a})$ in a uniform electric field $\vec{E}$ such that $\vec{p}$ makes an angle $\theta$ with $\vec{E}$.

  1. Forces on the two charges:
    • Force on $+q$: $\vec{F}_+ = +q\vec{E}$ (in the direction of $\vec{E}$).
    • Force on $-q$: $\vec{F}_- = -q\vec{E}$ (opposite to $\vec{E}$).
  2. Net translational force: $$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \vec{F}_+ + \vec{F}_- = q\vec{E} - q\vec{E} = \vec{0}$$ Because the net force is zero, the dipole undergoes no linear (translational) motion in a uniform field.
  3. Torque (Couple): Since the two equal and opposite forces act at two different points, their lines of action do not coincide. They form a couple that rotates the dipole to align it with $\vec{E}$. $$\tau = (\text{Magnitude of either force}) \times (\text{Perpendicular distance between lines of action})$$ Drop a perpendicular from $+q$ to the line of action of $\vec{F}_-$. From right-triangle geometry, the perpendicular arm is $2a\sin\theta$: $$\tau = (qE)(2a\sin\theta) = (q \cdot 2a)E\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta$$
  4. Vector Form: $$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$$ The direction of $\vec{\tau}$ is given by the right-hand screw rule (perpendicular to the plane containing $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{E}$).
Orientation ($\theta$)Torque ($\tau = pE\sin\theta$)Physical State
$\theta = 0^\circ$ ($\vec{p} \parallel \vec{E}$)$\tau = 0$Stable Equilibrium: Slight tilt produces a restoring torque bringing it back to $0^\circ$.
$\theta = 90^\circ$ ($\vec{p} \perp \vec{E}$)$\tau_{\max} = pE$Maximum Torque: Strongest rotational pull toward $\vec{E}$.
$\theta = 180^\circ$ ($\vec{p}$ antiparallel to $\vec{E}$)$\tau = 0$Unstable Equilibrium: Slight disturbance flips the dipole completely around to $0^\circ$.
What if the field is NON-UNIFORM? Then $\vec{F}_+ \neq -\vec{F}_-$, so the dipole experiences both a net translational force AND a torque. If $\vec{p}$ is parallel to an increasing $\vec{E}$, the net force pulls the dipole in the direction of increasing field (this is why a charged comb attracts neutral bits of paper by inducing a dipole in them!).
Topic 1.10 & 1.13 · Macroscopic Distributions

8. Continuous Charge Distributions & Electric Flux

When a huge number of charges are packed closely on a wire, sheet, or solid, we describe them using three charge densities:

Definitions: Linear, Surface & Volume Charge Densities

  • Linear Charge Density ($\lambda$): Charge per unit length of a line/wire: $\lambda = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta l}$, SI unit $\text{C m}^{-1}$.
  • Surface Charge Density ($\sigma$): Charge per unit area of a surface/sheet: $\sigma = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta S}$, SI unit $\text{C m}^{-2}$.
  • Volume Charge Density ($\rho$): Charge per unit volume of a 3D body: $\rho = \frac{\Delta q}{\Delta V}$, SI unit $\text{C m}^{-3}$.

Definition: Area Vector ($d\vec{S}$) & Electric Flux ($\Phi_E$)

A small planar surface element has an area vector $d\vec{S} = dS\,\hat{n}$ whose magnitude is its area $dS$ and direction $\hat{n}$ is along the outward normal to the surface.

Electric Flux ($\Phi_E$) through a surface is the measure of the total number of electric field lines passing normally through that surface: $$d\Phi_E = \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{S} = E\,dS\cos\theta \implies \Phi_E = \int \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S}$$

Nature: Scalar quantity · SI Unit: $\text{N m}^2\text{ C}^{-1}$ (or $\text{V m}$) · Dimensions: $[\text{M L}^{3}\text{T}^{-3}\text{A}^{-1}]$
  • If $\theta = 0^\circ$ ($\vec{E}$ parallel to area vector, i.e., perpendicular to surface plane): $\Phi_E = +ES$ (maximum outward flux).
  • If $\theta = 90^\circ$ ($\vec{E}$ grazing/parallel to surface plane): $\Phi_E = 0$.
  • If $\theta = 180^\circ$ (field lines entering a closed surface): $\Phi_E = -ES$ (negative/inward flux).
Topic 1.14 · Derivation 4

9. Gauss's Law & Proof from Coulomb's Law

Definition: Gauss's Law

The total electric flux through any closed surface (called a Gaussian surface) in vacuum is equal to $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}$ times the net electric charge ($q_{\text{enc}}$) enclosed within that surface: $$\Phi_E = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Derivation 4: Proof of Gauss's Law for a Spherical Surface

Consider an isolated positive point charge $+q$ placed at the centre $O$ of an imaginary sphere of radius $r$.

  1. Electric field on the surface: By Coulomb's law, the field at any point on the sphere is directed radially outward with constant magnitude: $$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\hat{r}$$
  2. Area element: Choose a tiny patch $d\vec{S} = dS\,\hat{r}$ on the sphere. Both $\vec{E}$ and $d\vec{S}$ point radially outward, so the angle between them is $\theta = 0^\circ$.
  3. Flux through $d\vec{S}$: $$d\Phi_E = \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = E\,dS\cos 0^\circ = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\,dS$$
  4. Total flux over the entire closed sphere: Integrating over the closed surface (taking constant $E$ outside the integral): $$\Phi_E = \oint d\Phi_E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\oint dS$$ Since the total surface area of a sphere of radius $r$ is $\oint dS = 4\pi r^2$: $$\Phi_E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}(4\pi r^2) = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$$

5 Key Rules of Gauss's Law (Frequently Tested in 1–2 Mark Qs)

  • Gauss's law holds for any closed surface regardless of its size or shape (sphere, cube, cylinder, or irregular potato shape).
  • If $q_{\text{enc}} = 0$ (e.g., an electric dipole enclosed inside a sphere, or charges lying outside), total flux $\Phi_E = 0$, even if $\vec{E} \neq 0$ at points on the surface.
  • In $\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$, the electric field $\vec{E}$ on the LHS is the resultant field due to all charges (both inside and outside), whereas $q_{\text{enc}}$ on the RHS includes only charges strictly inside the surface.
  • The Gaussian surface must never pass through a discrete point charge (since $\vec{E}$ is undefined right at a point charge), though it may pass through a continuous charge distribution.
  • Gauss's law is fundamentally equivalent to the inverse-square ($1/r^2$) dependence of Coulomb's law.
Topic 1.15 · Derivations 5, 6 & 7

10. Applications of Gauss's Law (All 3 Full Derivations)

Derivation 5: Electric Field due to an Infinitely Long Straight Uniformly Charged Wire

Consider an infinitely long, thin straight wire carrying uniform linear charge density $\lambda$ ($\text{C m}^{-1}$). We want to find the electric field $\vec{E}$ at a perpendicular distance $r$ from the wire.

  1. Symmetry Analysis: Because the wire is infinitely long, every point at perpendicular distance $r$ is physically identical. For any pair of symmetric charge elements above and below the observation point, their vertical field components cancel and horizontal radial components add. Thus $\vec{E}$ is radially outward (for $\lambda > 0$) and has constant magnitude at distance $r$.
  2. Gaussian Surface: Imagine a closed coaxial circular cylinder of radius $r$ and length $l$ around the wire.
  3. Flux through the 3 parts of the cylinder:
    • Top circular cap ($S_1$): $\vec{E}$ is radial while $d\vec{S}_1$ points vertically up ($\theta = 90^\circ$) $\implies \Phi_1 = \int E\,dS\cos 90^\circ = 0$.
    • Bottom circular cap ($S_2$): $\vec{E}$ is radial while $d\vec{S}_2$ points vertically down ($\theta = 90^\circ$) $\implies \Phi_2 = 0$.
    • Curved cylindrical surface ($S_3$): At every point, $\vec{E} \parallel d\vec{S}_3$ ($\theta = 0^\circ$) and $E$ is constant over area $2\pi r l$: $$\Phi_3 = \int E\,dS\cos 0^\circ = E \int dS = E(2\pi r l)$$
  4. Total flux: $\Phi_E = \Phi_1 + \Phi_2 + \Phi_3 = E(2\pi r l)$.
  5. Charge enclosed inside length $l$: $q_{\text{enc}} = \lambda l$.
  6. Applying Gauss's Law ($\Phi_E = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$): $$E(2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$ In vector form: $$\vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\hat{n}$$
Graph Behaviour: $E \propto \frac{1}{r}$ (rectangular hyperbola). Notice $l$ cancels out completely.

Derivation 6: Electric Field due to a Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet

Consider a thin, infinite plane sheet carrying uniform surface charge density $\sigma$ ($\text{C m}^{-2}$).

  1. Symmetry Analysis: By planar symmetry, $\vec{E}$ cannot depend on $y$ or $z$ coordinates parallel to the sheet; it must point strictly perpendicular to the sheet, directed away from it on both sides (for $\sigma > 0$).
  2. Gaussian Surface: Choose a right circular cylinder (or rectangular pillbox) of cross-sectional area $A$ piercing the sheet perpendicularly and extending equally on both sides.
  3. Flux through the pillbox:
    • Curved side wall: $\vec{E}$ is parallel to the wall, so $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{S}$ ($\theta = 90^\circ$) $\implies \Phi_{\text{curved}} = 0$.
    • Left & Right flat end faces (area $A$ each): On both faces, $\vec{E}$ and outward normal $d\vec{S}$ point in the same direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$): $$\Phi_E = \int_{\text{left}} E\,dS + \int_{\text{right}} E\,dS = EA + EA = 2EA$$
  4. Charge enclosed by the pillbox: $q_{\text{enc}} = \sigma A$.
  5. Applying Gauss's Law: $$2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\hat{n}$$
Special Board Case — Two Parallel Infinite Sheets ($+\sigma$ and $-\sigma$):
  • Region outside (left of $+\sigma$ or right of $-\sigma$): Fields are equal and opposite, so $E_{\text{out}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} - \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = 0$.
  • Region between the sheets: Both fields point from $+\sigma$ to $-\sigma$, so they add up: $E_{\text{between}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$ (this is the exact basis of a parallel-plate capacitor in Chapter 2!).

Derivation 7: Electric Field due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell

Consider a thin spherical shell of radius $R$ carrying uniform surface charge density $\sigma$. The total charge on the shell is $q = \sigma(4\pi R^2)$. We determine $\vec{E}$ at a distance $r$ from the centre $O$ by choosing a concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$.

Case 1: Point Outside the Shell ($r > R$)

  1. By spherical symmetry, $\vec{E}$ is radially outward and has the same magnitude at every point on the Gaussian sphere of radius $r$.
  2. Total electric flux: $\Phi_E = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = E\oint dS = E(4\pi r^2)$.
  3. Charge enclosed: Since the entire shell lies inside the Gaussian sphere, $q_{\text{enc}} = q = 4\pi R^2\sigma$.
  4. By Gauss's Law: $$E(4\pi r^2) = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} = \frac{\sigma R^2}{\varepsilon_0 r^2}$$

Physical conclusion: For all external points, a uniformly charged spherical shell behaves exactly as if its entire charge $q$ were concentrated as a point charge at its centre.

Case 2: Point on the Surface of the Shell ($r = R$)

Putting $r = R$ in the outside expression gives the maximum field:

$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{R^2} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Case 3: Point Inside the Shell ($r < R$)

  1. The concentric Gaussian sphere of radius $r < R$ lies completely inside the hollow shell. Because all charge resides on the outer surface at $R$, the enclosed charge is zero: $q_{\text{enc}} = 0$.
  2. Applying Gauss's Law: $$E(4\pi r^2) = \frac{0}{\varepsilon_0} = 0 \implies E = 0$$
$E$ vs $r$ Graph Summary (Board 1-Mark Favourite):
  • For $0 \le r < R$: $E = 0$ (flat line along the $r$-axis).
  • At $r = R$: Sharp jump to maximum value $E_{\max} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{R^2}$.
  • For $r > R$: Smooth hyperbolic fall as $E \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$.
