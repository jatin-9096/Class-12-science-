Comprehensive Academic Theory

1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Definition of Charge and Quantisation

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that gives rise to electric forces. It exists in two distinct types, conventionally called positive and negative. The sign convention was introduced by Benjamin Franklin: bodies that attract a glass rod when rubbed are called positively charged, while those that attract a silk thread are called negatively charged. Charge is quantised; it occurs only in integral multiples of the elementary charge $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. Hence any macroscopic charge $Q$ can be expressed as $Q = n e$, where $n$ is an integer (positive, negative or zero). This quantisation is a direct consequence of the existence of electrons (negative charge $-e$) and protons (positive charge $+e$) as the smallest indivisible carriers in ordinary matter. The conservation of charge states that the total charge in an isolated system remains constant; charge can be transferred from one body to another but cannot be created or destroyed. Mathematically, for any closed system, $$\sum_i Q_i = \text{constant}.$$ The law of conservation of charge underlies all electrostatic phenomena and is experimentally verified to a precision better than one part in $10^{20}$.

Physics · Coulomb’s Law
Coulomb’s Interaction Between Point Charges

Coulomb’s law quantifies the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges. If $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are separated by a distance $r$, the magnitude of the force is $$F = k \frac{|Q_1 Q_2|}{r^2},$$ where $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.988 \times 10^9\,\text{N·m}^2\text{/C}^2$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$ is the permittivity of free space. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. The direction of the force is along the line joining the two charges, making it a central force. Coulomb’s law is analogous to Newton’s law of gravitation, differing only in the presence of both attractive and repulsive interactions and the much larger magnitude of the electrostatic constant $k$. The law holds strictly for point charges; for extended charge distributions, the principle of superposition must be applied, integrating the contributions of infinitesimal charge elements $dq$ over the volume or surface of the object.

Physics · Electric Field Concept
Electric Field Intensity and Its Vector Nature

The electric field $\vec{E}$ at a point in space is defined as the force $\vec{F}$ experienced by a unit positive test charge $q_0$ placed at that point: $$\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}.$$ It is a vector quantity, possessing both magnitude and direction. For a point charge $Q$, the field at a distance $r$ is $$\vec{E} = k \frac{Q}{r^2}\,\hat{r},$$ where $\hat{r}$ is a unit vector directed radially outward from a positive charge (inward for a negative charge). The principle of superposition applies to electric fields as well: the net field due to multiple charges is the vector sum of the individual fields, $$\vec{E}_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \vec{E}_i.$$ Field lines provide a visual representation of $\vec{E}$. They originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges, never intersect, and their density is proportional to the field magnitude. The electric flux $\Phi_E$ through a closed surface $S$ is related to the enclosed charge $Q_{\text{enc}}$ by Gauss’s law, $$\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}.$$ This integral form is particularly powerful for systems with high symmetry (spherical, cylindrical, planar), allowing rapid calculation of $\vec{E}$ without explicit summation of forces.

Physics · Conductors, Insulators & Polarisation
Behaviour of Materials in Electrostatic Equilibrium

Materials are classified based on the mobility of their charge carriers. Conductors (e.g., metals) contain free electrons that can move readily under an external field, while insulators (e.g., glass, rubber) have tightly bound electrons, restricting charge motion. In electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside a perfect conductor is zero; any excess charge resides on its surface, distributing itself so that the surface field is perpendicular to the surface at every point. This results from the fact that any tangential component would cause surface charges to move, contradicting equilibrium. For insulators, charges cannot migrate freely; however, an external field can induce a slight displacement of bound electrons relative to nuclei, creating induced dipoles—a phenomenon known as polarisation. The net effect is a reduction of the internal field, quantified by the dielectric constant $\kappa$, where $\varepsilon = \kappa \varepsilon_0$. The relationship between the electric displacement $\vec{D}$ and the field $\vec{E}$ in a linear, isotropic dielectric is $$\vec{D} = \varepsilon \vec{E} = \kappa \varepsilon_0 \vec{E}.$$ Exceptions to ideal behaviour arise in semiconductors, where charge carriers are limited but not absent, leading to intermediate conductivity and the basis for modern electronic devices.

Physics · Worked Example
Electric Field of a Uniformly Charged Ring on Its Axis

Consider a thin circular ring of radius $R$ carrying a total charge $Q$ uniformly distributed. We wish to find the electric field at a point $P$ on the axis of the ring at a distance $x$ from its centre. By symmetry, the horizontal components of the field due to opposite charge elements cancel, leaving only a vertical component along the axis. An infinitesimal charge element $dq = \frac{Q}{2\pi R}\,dl$ at a point on the ring produces a field magnitude $$dE = k \frac{dq}{r^2},$$ where $r = \sqrt{R^2 + x^2}$ is the distance from $dq$ to $P$. The vertical component is $$dE_{\parallel} = dE \cos\theta = k \frac{dq}{r^2}\frac{x}{r} = k \frac{Q}{2\pi R}\frac{x\,dl}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}.$$ Integrating around the entire ring, $$E_{\parallel} = \int_0^{2\pi R} dE_{\parallel} = k \frac{Qx}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \int_0^{2\pi R}\frac{dl}{2\pi R} = k \frac{Qx}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}.$$ Hence the electric field on the axis is $$\boxed{E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Qx}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}}$$ directed away from the ring if $Q$ is positive and towards the ring if $Q$ is negative. This result illustrates the power of symmetry and superposition in solving electrostatic problems, and it serves as a stepping stone to more complex configurations such as charged disks and solenoids.

1. Coulomb’s Law – Derivation from Symmetry and Experiment
Coulomb’s law is the cornerstone of electrostatics. It states that the electrostatic force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The derivation can be sketched in two parts: (i) experimental observation of the inverse‑square dependence, and (ii) symmetry arguments that fix the direction and the vector nature of the force.
(i) Experimental Observation
By measuring the force between two charged spheres suspended from a torsion balance, Coulomb found that the force varied as $1/r^2$. The proportionality constant was later denoted $k$ and expressed in terms of the permittivity of free space: $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$.
(ii) Symmetry Argument
The force must act along the line joining the two charges because of isotropy of space. Therefore, the force vector can be written as $\mathbf{F} = f(r)\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$, where $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the unit vector from one charge to the other and $f(r)$ is a scalar function of the separation $r$. By the experimental result, $f(r) = k\,q_1q_2/r^2$. Hence, the vector form of Coulomb’s law is $$\mathbf{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}.$$
This expression is valid for static point charges in vacuum or any linear, isotropic, homogeneous medium with permittivity $\varepsilon$.

2. Electric Field of a Point Charge – Derivation from Coulomb’s Law
The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is defined as the force per unit test charge: $$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{q_{\text{test}}}.$$ Substituting Coulomb’s law for a test charge $q_{\text{test}}$ placed at $\mathbf{r}$ due to a source charge $q$ at the origin gives $$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}.$$ The magnitude is $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{|q|}{r^2}$ and the direction is radially outward for $q>0$ and inward for $q<0$.
The field is a vector field that satisfies the superposition principle: for multiple point charges, $$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i \frac{q_i}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|^2}\,\widehat{(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i)}.$$

3. Electric Field of Continuous Charge Distributions
For a continuous distribution of charge, the point‑charge expression is replaced by an integral over the charge density.
(a) Line Charge
Consider a straight line of length $L$ carrying a uniform linear charge density $\lambda$ (C/m). The infinitesimal charge element is $dq = \lambda\,dl$. The field at a point $P$ a perpendicular distance $x$ from the line is obtained by integrating the contribution from each element: $$\mathbf{E}(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\frac{\lambda\,dl}{(x^2+l^2)}\,\frac{x\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}}{(x^2+l^2)^{1/2}}.$$ Performing the integral yields $$E(x) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,x}\,\left(1-\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+(L/2)^2}}\right).$$ In the limit $L\to\infty$, the field reduces to $$E_{\infty} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,x}.$$
(b) Surface Charge
For a uniformly charged infinite plane with surface charge density $\sigma$ (C/m$^2$), the field at a distance $z$ from the plane is obtained by integrating over an infinitesimal ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$: $$d\mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr}{(r^2+z^2)}\,\frac{z\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}}{(r^2+z^2)^{1/2}}.$$ Integrating from $0$ to $\infty$ gives $$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\,\hat{\mathbf{z}},$$ independent of $z$. The direction is normal to the plane.
(c) Volume Charge
For a volume charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r}')$, the field at $\mathbf{r}$ is $$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_V \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^3}\,d^3r'.$$ This is the general integral expression used for any arbitrary charge distribution.
