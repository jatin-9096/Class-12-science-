Part 1: Electric Charges & Coulomb's Interaction

Intrinsic properties of charge, charging mechanisms, quantisation domains, and vector force derivations.

1. Fundamentals of Electric Charge

Electric Charge is an intrinsic scalar physical property of fundamental particles (electrons and protons) that causes matter to experience electrostatic force when placed in an electromagnetic field. Its SI unit is Coulomb ($C$), derived as $1\text{ C} = 1\text{ A}\cdot\text{s}$, with dimensional formula $[M^0 L^0 T^1 A^1]$.

  • Polarity of Charge: The property distinguishing positive charge (protons/electron-deficit) and negative charge (electrons/electron-excess). Established historically by Benjamin Franklin.
  • Gold-Leaf Electroscope: Classical apparatus used to detect the presence and sign of charge. Divergence angle of leaves is directly proportional to charge magnitude.
  • Methods of Charging a Body:
    • Charging by Friction: Rubbing two insulators causes electron transfer due to differences in work functions (e.g., glass rod rubbed with silk cloth leaves glass rod positively charged and silk negatively charged).
    • Charging by Conduction: Contact transfer where an uncharged conductor touches a charged body and shares free electrons until potentials equalize.
    • Charging by Induction: Redistribution of charges in a conductor induced by an external charge nearby without physical contact, followed by temporary grounding.

2. Material Classification: Conductors vs Insulators vs Semiconductors

Property Conductors Insulators (Dielectrics) Semiconductors
Charge Carrier Density High density of free electrons ($\sim 10^{28}/\text{m}^3$) Negligible free electrons; tightly bound valence electrons Moderate; increases exponentially with temperature
Charge Distribution Resides strictly on the outer metallic boundary surface Remains strictly localized at point of application Controlled by impurity doping ($p$-type, $n$-type)
Examples Metals (Cu, Ag, Al), Human Body, Acidified Water Glass, Rubber, Mica, Ebonite, Dry Wood Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge), GaAs

3. Three Core Postulates of Electric Charge

  • Postulate I: Additivity of Charges: Total electric charge of an extended system is the algebraic sum of all point charges: $$Q_{\text{net}} = \sum_{i=1}^n q_i = q_1 + q_2 + \dots + q_n$$
  • Postulate II: Conservation of Charge: For an electrically isolated system, net charge remains strictly constant over time ($Q_{\text{initial}} = Q_{\text{final}}$). Charges can be pair-produced or annihilated (e.g., $e^- + e^+ \to \gamma + \gamma$), but net algebraic sum is invariant.
  • Postulate III: Quantisation of Charge: Charge exists exclusively in discrete integral multiples of elementary quantum charge $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$:
    Quantisation Formula
    $$q = \pm n e \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$$
    Microscopic vs Macroscopic Domain: At microscopic scales (atomic collisions), quantization is vital. At macroscopic levels ($q \sim 1\,\mu\text{C} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{12}e$), individual increments of $e$ are so tiny that charge is treated as a continuous fluid.

4. Coulomb's Law (Scalar & Vector Derivations)

Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of charge magnitudes and inversely proportional to the square of separation distance $r$ along their joining line.

Scalar Magnitude Formula
$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$
Where $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}$ is vacuum permittivity, and $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 8.987 \times 10^9\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
Step-by-Step Derivation: Coulomb's Law in Vector Form
Step 1 Let position vectors of point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ be $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$. The displacement vector pointing from $q_1$ to $q_2$ is: $$\vec{r}_{21} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1, \quad |\vec{r}_{21}| = r$$
Step 2 The unit vector along this direction is $\hat{r}_{21} = \frac{\vec{r}_{21}}{r}$. Thus, electrostatic force exerted on $q_2$ by $q_1$ is: $$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3} (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1)$$
Step 3 Similarly, force on $q_1$ exerted by $q_2$ with unit vector $\hat{r}_{12} = -\hat{r}_{21}$ is: $$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$$
Conclusion: Electrostatic force strictly satisfies Newton's Third Law ($\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$) and acts along the central axis, confirming it is a central conservative force.

5. Dielectric Constant / Relative Permittivity ($K$ or $\varepsilon_r$)

In a dielectric medium of permittivity $\varepsilon$, Coulomb force is weakened due to medium polarization:

$$F_{\text{med}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} = \frac{F_{\text{vac}}}{K} \implies K = \varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{F_{\text{vac}}}{F_{\text{med}}}$$

For vacuum $K=1$; air $K \approx 1.00054$; water $K \approx 80.4$; ideal metals $K = \infty$.

Part 2: Electric Field, Field Lines & Dipole Dynamics

Vector field superpositions, field line axioms, axial & equatorial field derivations, and dipole torque.

1. Electric Field Intensity ($\vec{E}$)

The Electric Field Intensity $\vec{E}$ at a point in space is defined as the limiting electrostatic force experienced per unit positive infinitesimal test charge ($q_0 \to 0$ to prevent modifying the source field distribution):

Electric Field Expression
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$
SI Unit: $\text{N/C}$ or $\text{V/m}$. Dimensional Formula: $[M^1 L^1 T^{-3} A^{-1}]$. Radially outward for $+Q$; radially inward for $-Q$.

2. Fundamental Axioms of Electric Field Lines

  • Start and Terminate Points: Emerge from positive charges and end at negative charges. They cannot form closed loops, proving electrostatic field is conservative ($\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l} = 0$).
  • Non-Intersection Law: Two field lines never intersect. If they did, two tangents could be drawn at the intersection point, which would imply two conflicting directions for the electric field at one point—physically impossible.
  • Normal Incidence on Conductors: Field lines are always perpendicular to the surface of a conductor in equilibrium. Any tangential component would accelerate free surface electrons, violating static equilibrium.
  • Relative Density: Number of lines crossing unit perpendicular area is directly proportional to field magnitude ($|\vec{E}|$). Closer lines indicate stronger fields.

3. Electric Dipole & Dipole Moment Vector ($\vec{p}$)

An Electric Dipole consists of a pair of equal and opposite point charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by a microscopic distance $2\vec{a}$. The Electric Dipole Moment is defined as:

Dipole Moment Equation
$$\vec{p} = q \cdot (2\vec{a})$$
Vector Direction: By convention, points from negative charge ($-q$) to positive charge ($+q$). SI Unit: $\text{Coulomb-meter (C}\cdot\text{m)}$.
Board Derivation 1: Field of a Dipole at an Axial Point ($P$)
Step 1 Place dipole along $x$-axis centered at origin $O$, charges $-q$ at $-a$ and $+q$ at $+a$. Let observation point $P$ be at distance $r$ from $O$ along the dipole axis.
Step 2 Distance of $+q$ to $P$ is $(r - a)$ and distance of $-q$ to $P$ is $(r + a)$. The net axial field is: $$E_{\text{axial}} = E_{+q} - E_{-q} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{1}{(r + a)^2} \right]$$
Step 3 Combining terms with common denominator $[(r-a)(r+a)]^2 = (r^2 - a^2)^2$: $$E_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{4ar}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2(q \cdot 2a)r}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}$$
Step 4 For a short dipole where $r \gg a$ ($a^2 \to 0$): $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\vec{p}}{r^3} \quad (\text{Directed along } \vec{p})$$
Board Derivation 2: Field of a Dipole at an Equatorial Point ($Q$)
Step 1 Let point $Q$ lie on the equatorial perpendicular bisector at distance $r$ from $O$. Distance from both $+q$ and $-q$ to $Q$ is $\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$.
Step 2 Field magnitudes are equal: $E_{+q} = E_{-q} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)}$. Resolving components:
  • Vertical components $E_{+q}\sin\theta$ and $E_{-q}\sin\theta$ cancel out.
  • Horizontal components $E_{+q}\cos\theta$ and $E_{-q}\cos\theta$ reinforce in the direction opposite to $\vec{p}$.
Step 3 The net equatorial field magnitude is: $$E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_{+q} \cos\theta = 2 \left( \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)} \right) \left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} \right) = \frac{q \cdot 2a}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}$$
Step 4 For a short dipole where $r \gg a$: $$\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\vec{p}}{r^3} \quad (\text{Directed opposite to } \vec{p})$$
Golden Ratio for Board Exams: For identical distances $r$: $|\vec{E}_{\text{axial}}| = 2 \times |\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}}|$.

4. Dipole Placed in an External Electric Field

  • Uniform Field ($\vec{E} = \text{const}$): Net translation force $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = (+q\vec{E}) + (-q\vec{E}) = 0$. Rotational torque acting about the center of mass:
    Torque & Potential Energy Equations
    $$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} \implies \tau = pE\sin\theta$$ $$W = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} \tau d\theta = -pE(\cos\theta_2 - \cos\theta_1) \implies U(\theta) = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E} = -pE\cos\theta$$
  • Equilibrium States:
    • Stable Equilibrium: $\theta = 0^\circ \implies \vec{\tau} = 0$, $U_{\text{min}} = -pE$.
    • Unstable Equilibrium: $\theta = 180^\circ \implies \vec{\tau} = 0$, $U_{\text{max}} = +pE$.

Part 3: Continuous Charge Distributions & Gauss's Theorem

Surface integrals, electric flux definitions, Gauss's law proof, and three 5-mark applications.

1. Electric Flux ($\Phi_E$)

Electric Flux is the surface integral of the electric field over an arbitrary closed or open surface, quantifying the number of field lines passing perpendicularly through that surface:

$$\Phi_E = \iint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \iint_S E \cos\theta \, dA$$

SI Unit: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}$ or $\text{Volt}\cdot\text{meter (V}\cdot\text{m)}$. Scalar quantity.

Gauss's Theorem Statement
$$\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$
Total electric flux through any closed Gaussian surface in free space equals $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}$ times the net algebraic charge enclosed within that surface.
Board Derivation 3: Field of an Infinitely Long Straight Uniformly Charged Wire
Step 1 Let an infinite wire possess uniform linear charge density $\lambda = \frac{q}{l}$. Choose a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ and length $l$.
Step 2 Split surface into two flat circular caps ($S_1, S_2$) and one curved surface ($S_3$).
  • On flat caps $S_1$ and $S_2$: $\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A} \implies \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = E dA \cos 90^\circ = 0$.
  • On curved mantle $S_3$: $\vec{E} \parallel d\vec{A} \implies \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = E dA \cos 0^\circ = E dA$.
Step 3 Applying Gauss's Theorem: $$\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = E (2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$
Board Derivation 4: Field of an Infinitely Thin Uniformly Charged Plane Sheet
Step 1 Let an infinite plane carry surface charge density $\sigma = \frac{q}{A}$. Construct a Gaussian cylinder of cross-sectional area $A$ piercing perpendicularly through the sheet.
Step 2 On cylindrical walls flux is zero. On both flat end-caps: $\text{Flux} = EA + EA = 2EA$.
Step 3 By Gauss's Law: $2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ (Independent of distance $r$).
Board Derivation 5: Field of a Thin Uniformly Charged Spherical Shell (Radius $R$)
Case A Outside ($r > R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}$ (Acts as if entire charge is concentrated at the center).
Case B Surface ($r = R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{R^2} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$ (Maximum).
Case C Inside ($r < R$): $q_{\text{enclosed}} = 0 \implies E_{\text{inside}} = 0$.

Part 4: 5-Mark Solved Board Exemplar Problems

Step-by-step solutions to classical numericals and multi-concept questions frequently asked in CBSE Class 12 Boards.

5 Marks • Superposition & Equilibrium CBSE Previous Year Exemplar
Two point charges $+4e$ and $+e$ are fixed at a distance $a$ apart in vacuum. Where should a third point charge $q$ be placed on the line joining the two charges so that the entire system remains in electrostatic equilibrium? Also determine the magnitude and sign of charge $q$.
Step 1: Equilibrium of third charge $q$:
Let charge $q$ be placed at a distance $x$ from $+4e$. Distance from $+e$ is $(a - x)$. For $q$ to be in equilibrium, forces exerted by $+4e$ and $+e$ must balance each other: $$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(4e)q}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(e)q}{(a - x)^2} \implies \frac{4}{x^2} = \frac{1}{(a - x)^2}$$ Taking square root on both sides: $$\frac{2}{x} = \frac{1}{a - x} \implies 2a - 2x = x \implies 3x = 2a \implies x = \frac{2a}{3}$$ Hence, charge $q$ must be placed at a distance of $\mathbf{\frac{2a}{3}}$ from the $+4e$ charge.
Step 2: Sign and magnitude of charge $q$ for system equilibrium:
For the entire system to be in equilibrium, net force on charge $+e$ must also be zero: $$F_{+e} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(4e)(e)}{a^2} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q(e)}{(a - x)^2} = 0$$ Substitute $(a - x) = a - \frac{2a}{3} = \frac{a}{3}$: $$\frac{4e^2}{a^2} + \frac{qe}{(a/3)^2} = 0 \implies \frac{4e^2}{a^2} + \frac{9qe}{a^2} = 0 \implies 9q = -4e \implies q = -\frac{4e}{9}$$
Final Answer: The charge must be negative with magnitude $\mathbf{q = -\frac{4e}{9}}$, placed at distance $\mathbf{\frac{2a}{3}}$ from $+4e$.
5 Marks • Dipole Field & Gauss Flux CBSE Core Question
An electric dipole consists of two charges $\pm 2\,\mu\text{C}$ separated by a distance of $4\,\text{cm}$. Calculate:
(i) The dipole moment vector $\vec{p}$.
(ii) Electric field intensity at a distance of $20\,\text{cm}$ from the center on its axial line.
(iii) Electric field intensity at the same distance on its equatorial line.
(iv) Total electric flux through a sphere of radius $10\,\text{cm}$ concentric with the dipole.
(i) Dipole Moment ($\vec{p}$):
$$p = q \cdot (2a) = (2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}) \times (0.04\text{ m}) = \mathbf{8 \times 10^{-8}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}} \quad (\text{directed from } -q \text{ to } +q)$$
(ii) Electric field on Axial Line ($r = 0.2\text{ m}$):
Since $r = 20\text{ cm} \gg a = 2\text{ cm}$, we use the short dipole formula: $$E_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p}{r^3} = (9 \times 10^9) \times \frac{2 \times 8 \times 10^{-8}}{(0.2)^3} = \frac{1440}{0.008} = \mathbf{1.8 \times 10^5\text{ N/C}}$$
(iii) Electric field on Equatorial Line ($r = 0.2\text{ m}$):
$$E_{\text{equatorial}} = \frac{E_{\text{axial}}}{2} = \frac{1.8 \times 10^5}{2} = \mathbf{9.0 \times 10^4\text{ N/C}}$$
(iv) Total Electric Flux ($\Phi$):
The sphere encloses both $+2\,\mu\text{C}$ and $-2\,\mu\text{C}$. Net enclosed charge $q_{\text{enc}} = (+2\,\mu\text{C}) + (-2\,\mu\text{C}) = 0$. By Gauss's Theorem: $$\Phi_E = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}}$$
