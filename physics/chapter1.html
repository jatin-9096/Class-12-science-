Part 1: Electric Charges & Coulomb's Interaction

Intrinsic properties of charge, quantisation limits, dielectric constants, and vector force derivations.

1. Fundamentals of Electric Charge

Electric Charge is an intrinsic scalar physical property of elementary matter (electrons and protons) causing it to experience an electrostatic force in an electromagnetic field. Its SI unit is Coulomb ($C$), with dimensional formula $[M^0 L^0 T^1 A^1]$.

  • Polarity of Charge: The property distinguishing positive (electron deficit) and negative (electron excess) charges, discovered by Benjamin Franklin.
  • Gold-Leaf Electroscope: Apparatus detecting presence and nature of electric charge. Leaf divergence angle reflects charge magnitude.
  • Methods of Charging:
    • Friction: Work-function mismatch causes electron transfer when rubbing two insulators (e.g. glass rod on silk).
    • Conduction: Direct physical contact transfer between conductors until potentials equalize.
    • Induction: Redistribution of internal charges caused by an external field without touching, followed by grounding.

2. Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

Property Conductors Insulators (Dielectrics) Semiconductors
Free Carrier Density High free electron density ($\sim 10^{28}/\text{m}^3$) Negligible free electrons; bound valence electrons Moderate; increases exponentially with temperature
Charge Distribution Resides solely on the outer boundary surface Remains localized at point of application Regulated by intentional doping ($p$, $n$)
Examples Copper, Aluminium, Human Body Glass, Rubber, Mica, Dry Wood Silicon, Germanium, GaAs

3. Three Core Postulates of Electric Charge

  • Additivity: Total charge is the scalar algebraic sum: $Q_{\text{net}} = \sum q_i$.
  • Conservation: Net charge in an electrically isolated system remains strictly constant over time ($Q_{\text{initial}} = Q_{\text{final}}$).
  • Quantisation: Charge exists exclusively in integer multiples of elementary quantum charge $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$:
    Quantisation Formula
    $$q = \pm n e \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$$
    At macroscopic scales ($q \sim 1\,\mu\text{C} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{12}e$), discrete steps blur into a continuous fluid.

4. Coulomb's Law (Scalar & Vector Proofs)

Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to their product and inversely proportional to the square of their separation distance $r$.

Scalar Magnitude
$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$
Permittivity of vacuum $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\text{ C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}$; $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 8.99 \times 10^9\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
Board Derivation 1: Coulomb's Law in Vector Form
Step 1 Let displacement vector pointing from $q_1$ to $q_2$ be $\vec{r}_{21} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1$, with unit vector $\hat{r}_{21} = \frac{\vec{r}_{21}}{r}$.
Step 2 The electrostatic force on $q_2$ exerted by $q_1$ is: $$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^3} (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1)$$
Step 3 Force on $q_1$ exerted by $q_2$ with $\hat{r}_{12} = -\hat{r}_{21}$: $$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{r}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$$
Conclusion: Electrostatic force satisfies Newton's Third Law and acts along the central axis (central conservative force).

5. Dielectric Constant ($K$ or $\varepsilon_r$)

In a dielectric medium, Coulomb force is reduced by medium polarization:

$$F_{\text{med}} = \frac{F_{\text{vac}}}{K} \implies K = \varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Vacuum $K=1$; air $K \approx 1.00054$; water $K \approx 80$; ideal metals $K = \infty$.

Part 2: Electric Field, Field Lines & Dipole Dynamics

Electric field intensity, field line axioms, axial & equatorial proofs, and dipole torque.

1. Electric Field Intensity ($\vec{E}$)

The electrostatic force experienced per unit infinitesimal positive test charge:

Field Equation
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$
SI Unit: $\text{N/C}$ or $\text{V/m}$. Dimensions: $[M^1 L^1 T^{-3} A^{-1}]$. Radially outward for $+Q$; inward for $-Q$.

2. Electric Dipole & Dipole Moment Vector ($\vec{p}$)

Pair of equal and opposite charges ($\pm q$) separated by distance $2\vec{a}$:

Dipole Moment
$$\vec{p} = q \cdot (2\vec{a}) \quad (\text{Directed from } -q \text{ to } +q)$$
Board Derivation 2: Field of a Dipole at an Axial Point ($P$)
Step 1 Place dipole along $x$-axis centered at $O$. Observation point $P$ is at distance $r$ along the axis.
Step 2 Distance from $+q$ is $(r - a)$ and from $-q$ is $(r + a)$: $$E_{\text{axial}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{1}{(r + a)^2} \right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{4ar}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \right]$$
Step 3 For short dipole ($r \gg a$): $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\vec{p}}{r^3} \quad (\text{Directed along } \vec{p})$$
Board Derivation 3: Field of a Dipole at an Equatorial Point ($Q$)
Step 1 Let point $Q$ be on the perpendicular bisector at distance $r$. Distance from each charge is $\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$.
Step 2 Vertical sine components cancel. Horizontal cosine components reinforce in direction opposite to $\vec{p}$: $$E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_{+q} \cos\theta = 2 \left( \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)} \right) \left( \frac{a}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} \right) = \frac{q \cdot 2a}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}$$
Step 3 For short dipole ($r \gg a$): $$\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\vec{p}}{r^3} \quad (\text{Directed opposite to } \vec{p})$$
Key Golden Ratio: At equal distances $r$, $|\vec{E}_{\text{axial}}| = 2 \times |\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}}|$.

3. Dipole in Uniform Field ($\vec{E}$)

Torque & Potential Energy
$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} \implies \tau = pE\sin\theta$$ $$U(\theta) = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E} = -pE\cos\theta$$
Stable equilibrium at $\theta = 0^\circ$ ($U_{\min} = -pE$). Unstable at $\theta = 180^\circ$ ($U_{\max} = +pE$).

Part 3: Gauss's Law & Core Applications

Electric flux integration, Gauss's theorem proof, and three 5-mark board derivations.

1. Gauss's Law

Gauss Equation
$$\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0} = \Phi_{\text{net}}$$
Flux depends strictly on net enclosed charge, not on the geometric shape or size of the Gaussian surface.
Board Derivation 4: Field of an Infinite Uniformly Charged Straight Wire
Step 1 Consider an infinite line of linear charge density $\lambda$. Choose a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ and length $l$.
Step 2 Flux through circular caps is zero ($\vec{E} \perp d\vec{A}$). On curved mantle: $\vec{E} \parallel d\vec{A}$. $$\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = E (2\pi r l) = \frac{\lambda l}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$
Board Derivation 5: Field of an Infinite Thin Plane Sheet
Step 1 Let surface charge density be $\sigma$. Choose a Gaussian cylinder of cross-sectional area $A$ piercing perpendicularly through the sheet.
Step 2 Flux emerges through both end-caps: $2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ (Independent of distance $r$).
Board Derivation 6: Field of a Thin Spherical Shell (Radius $R$)
Case A Outside ($r > R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}$.
Case B Surface ($r = R$): $E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{R^2} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}$ (Maximum).
Case C Inside ($r < R$): Enclosed charge $q_{\text{enc}} = 0 \implies E_{\text{inside}} = 0$.

Part 4: 5-Mark Solved Board Numericals

Full solutions to high-probability numericals with step marking breakdown.

5 Marks • Superposition & Equilibrium CBSE Board Exemplar
Two point charges $+4e$ and $+e$ are fixed at distance $a$ apart in vacuum. Where should a third charge $q$ be placed on the line joining them so that the whole system remains in equilibrium? Find the magnitude and sign of $q$.
Step 1: Equilibrium of third charge $q$:
Let $q$ be placed at distance $x$ from $+4e$. Distance from $+e$ is $(a - x)$.
$$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(4e)q}{x^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(e)q}{(a - x)^2} \implies \frac{2}{x} = \frac{1}{a - x} \implies x = \frac{2a}{3}$$
Step 2: Equilibrium of $+e$ for system stability:
$$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{(4e)(e)}{a^2} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q(e)}{(a/3)^2} = 0 \implies \frac{4e^2}{a^2} + \frac{9qe}{a^2} = 0 \implies q = -\frac{4e}{9}$$
Final Result: Place $q = -\frac{4e}{9}$ at distance $\frac{2a}{3}$ from $+4e$.
5 Marks • Dipole & Gauss Flux CBSE Core Question
An electric dipole has charges $\pm 2\,\mu\text{C}$ separated by $4\,\text{cm}$. Calculate:
(i) Dipole moment $\vec{p}$.
(ii) Field at $20\,\text{cm}$ on the axial line.
(iii) Field at $20\,\text{cm}$ on the equatorial line.
(iv) Total flux through a concentric sphere of radius $10\,\text{cm}$.
(i) Dipole Moment: $p = q(2a) = (2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C})(0.04\text{ m}) = \mathbf{8 \times 10^{-8}\text{ C}\cdot\text{m}}$ ($-q$ to $+q$).

(ii) Axial Field ($r = 0.2\text{ m}$):
$$E_{\text{axial}} = \frac{2kp}{r^3} = \frac{2 \times (9 \times 10^9) \times (8 \times 10^{-8})}{(0.2)^3} = \mathbf{1.8 \times 10^5\text{ N/C}}$$
(iii) Equatorial Field: $E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{E_{\text{axial}}}{2} = \mathbf{9.0 \times 10^4\text{ N/C}}$.

(iv) Total Flux: Enclosed charge $q_{\text{enc}} = (+2\,\mu\text{C}) + (-2\,\mu\text{C}) = 0 \implies \Phi_E = \mathbf{0\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}}$.
