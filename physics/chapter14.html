Intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors

Intrinsic Semiconductor – a pure semiconductor crystal with no intentional impurity. At thermal equilibrium the electron ($n$) and hole ($p$) concentrations are equal:

  • $n = p = n_i$
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration $$n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\,\exp\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right)$$ where $N_c$, $N_v$ are effective density of states, $E_g$ band gap, $k$ Boltzmann constant, $T$ absolute temperature.

Extrinsic Semiconductor – semiconductor doped with donor ($N_D$) or acceptor ($N_A$) atoms.

  • n‑type: $N_D \gg N_A$, majority carriers electrons $n \approx N_D$, minority holes $p = \frac{n_i^2}{n}$.
  • p‑type: $N_A \gg N_D$, majority carriers holes $p \approx N_A$, minority electrons $n = \frac{n_i^2}{p}$.
  • Mass‑action law: $$n p = n_i^2$$
PropertyIntrinsicExtrinsic (n‑type)
Majority carrierBoth $n$ and $p$Electrons ($n \approx N_D$)
Minority carrierBoth $n$ and $p$Holes ($p = n_i^2 / N_D$)
Conductivity$\sigma = q (n_i \mu_n + n_i \mu_p)$$\sigma = q (N_D \mu_n + p \mu_p)$

SI Units

  • Carrier concentration: $\text{cm}^{-3}$ (or $\text{m}^{-3}$)
  • Mobility $\mu$: $\text{cm}^2\,\text{V}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$
  • Conductivity $\sigma$: $\text{S}\,\text{m}^{-1}$

Example

Given $N_D = 10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, $n_i = 1.5\times10^{10}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ at $300\,$K, find electron concentration $n$ and hole concentration $p$.

  1. For an n‑type, $n \approx N_D = 10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$.
  2. Use mass‑action law: $p = \frac{n_i^2}{n} = \frac{(1.5\times10^{10})^2}{10^{16}} = 2.25\times10^{4}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$.
  3. Thus $\sigma = q (n \mu_n + p \mu_p)$. (Numerical substitution omitted for brevity.)

Charge carriers, concentration and mobility

Charge carriers – In an intrinsic semiconductor the charge carriers are electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band. Their concentrations are equal: $n = p = n_i$.

Carrier concentration

  • Intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\, e^{-E_g/(2kT)}$ (units: $\text{m}^{-3}$).
  • For an n‑type material $n \approx N_D$ and $p = \frac{n_i^2}{n}$.
  • For a p‑type material $p \approx N_A$ and $n = \frac{n_i^2}{p}$.

Mobility ($\mu$) – defined as the drift velocity per unit electric field:

$\mu = \frac{v_d}{E}$ (SI unit: $\text{m}^2\,\text{V}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$).

Conductivity $\sigma$ is related to carrier concentration and mobility:

ParameterExpression
Electron contribution$\sigma_n = q n \mu_n$
Hole contribution$\sigma_p = q p \mu_p$
Total$\sigma = q\,(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$

Example

At $300\,\text{K}$, silicon has $n_i = 1.5\times10^{16}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, $\mu_n = 0.14\,\text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, $\mu_p = 0.05\,\text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$. For an n‑type crystal doped with $N_D = 10^{22}\,\text{m}^{-3}$, find $\sigma$.

Solution: $n \approx N_D = 10^{22}$, $p = n_i^2/n = (1.5\times10^{16})^2/10^{22}=2.25\times10^{10}\,\text{m}^{-3}$. Then $\sigma = q (n\mu_n + p\mu_p) \approx 1.6\times10^{-19}(10^{22}\times0.14 + 2.25\times10^{10}\times0.05) \approx 2.24\times10^{2}\,\text{S m}^{-1}$.

Energy band theory and carrier transport

Energy Band Theory

  • Valence band (VB) – highest band completely filled with electrons at $T=0\\,\\text{K}$.
  • Conduction band (CB) – next higher band that is empty at $T=0\\,\\text{K}$.
  • Band gap $E_g$ – energy difference between the bottom of CB and top of VB. SI unit: joule (J) or electron‑volt (eV).
  • Intrinsic semiconductor – pure crystal; electron concentration $n$ equals hole concentration $p$, denoted $n_i$.

Carrier Concentration

QuantityExpressionSI Unit
Intrinsic carrier density$\\sqrt{N_c N_v}\\,\\exp\\left(-\\frac{E_g}{2kT}\\right)$m$^{-3}$
Electron concentration (n‑type)$n \approx N_D$ (for $N_D \\gg n_i$)m$^{-3}$
Hole concentration (p‑type)$p \approx N_A$ (for $N_A \\gg n_i$)m$^{-3}$

Transport Equations

  • Drift current density: $J_{drift}=q\\,\\mu\\,n\\,E$ (SI: A·m$^{-2}$).
  • Diffusion current density: $J_{diff}=q\\,D\\,\\frac{dn}{dx}$, where $D=\\mu\\,\\frac{kT}{q}$ (Einstein relation).

Example

Find the electron concentration $n$ in intrinsic Si at $300\\,\\text{K}$ given $n_i = 1.5\\times10^{10}\\,\\text{cm}^{-3}$.

  1. For an intrinsic material $n = p = n_i$.
  2. Convert to SI: $n = 1.5\\times10^{10}\\,\\text{cm}^{-3}\\times10^{6}=1.5\\times10^{16}\\,\\text{m}^{-3}$.
  3. Thus $n = 1.5\\times10^{16}\\,\\text{m}^{-3}$.

Key take‑aways: $E_g$ determines intrinsic carrier density; doping shifts $n$ or $p$ according to donor ($N_D$) or acceptor ($N_A$) concentration; drift and diffusion currents together describe carrier transport.

Formation and properties of p‑n junction

Definition: A p‑n junction is formed when a p‑type semiconductor (acceptor concentration $N_A$) is brought into intimate contact with an n‑type semiconductor (donor concentration $N_D$). Diffusion of majority carriers creates a depletion region that is free of mobile charge carriers.

Key results and formulas

  • Built‑in potential (contact potential): $V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}$ (unit: V).
  • Depletion width: $W=\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_s}{q}\frac{N_A+N_D}{N_A N_D}V_{bi}}$ (unit: m).
  • Maximum electric field: $E_{max}=\frac{q N_D x_n}{\epsilon_s}=\frac{q N_A x_p}{\epsilon_s}$ (unit: V/m), where $x_n$ and $x_p$ are the n‑ and p‑side widths ($W=x_n+x_p$).

SI units

QuantitySymbolSI unit
Built‑in potential$V_{bi}$V
Depletion width$W$m
Electric field$E_{max}$V/m

Example

Calculate $V_{bi}$ for a silicon p‑n junction at $T=300\,\text{K}$ with $N_A=10^{16}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, $N_D=10^{15}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ and intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i=1.5\times10^{10}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$. Use $k=1.38\times10^{-23}\,\text{J/K}$ and $q=1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.

Solution: \[V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}=\frac{1.38\times10^{-23}\times300}{1.6\times10^{-19}}\ln\frac{10^{16}\times10^{15}}{(1.5\times10^{10})^2}\approx0.73\,\text{V}.\]

The built‑in potential of about $0.73\,\text{V}$ establishes the electric field that governs charge carrier motion across the junction.

I‑V characteristics of p‑n junction diode (including Zener, LED, photodiode)

Definition: A p‑n junction diode is a semiconductor device formed by joining p‑type and n‑type materials. Its I‑V characteristic shows exponential increase of current in forward bias and a very small leakage in reverse bias until breakdown.

Key formulas

  • Diode equation: $I=I_S\left(e^{\frac{qV}{n k T}}-1\right)$ where $I_S$ (A) is saturation current, $q=1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, $k=1.38\times10^{-23}\,\text{J K}^{-1}$, $T$ (K), $n\approx1$–2.
  • Small‑signal resistance: $r_d=\frac{n k T}{q I}$ (Ω).
  • Zener diode (reverse breakdown): $V_{Z}$ is the breakdown voltage, current obeys $I=I_S\left(e^{\frac{q(V+V_Z)}{n k T}}-1\right)$ for $V\approx -V_Z$.
  • LED forward voltage: $V_F\approx\frac{h c}{\lambda e}$ (V) where $\lambda$ is emission wavelength.
  • Photodiode current: $I=I_S\left(e^{\frac{qV}{n k T}}-1\right)-I_{ph}$, $I_{ph}$ (A) is photocurrent proportional to incident light.

SI Units: Current – ampere (A), Voltage – volt (V), Temperature – kelvin (K), Charge – coulomb (C).

Table: Typical parameters

DeviceBreakdown/Forward Voltage (V)Typical $I_S$ (A)
Zener$V_Z$ = 5.6 – 12$10^{-9}$ – $10^{-6}$
LED (red)$V_F$ ≈ 1.8 – 2.2$10^{-12}$ – $10^{-9}$
PhotodiodeReverse bias $\approx$ 5 – 20$10^{-12}$ – $10^{-9}$

Solved example

Given $I_S=10^{-12}\,\text{A}$, find the forward voltage $V$ for $I=1\,\text{mA}$ at $T=300\,\text{K}$ (assume $n=1$).

  1. Use diode equation rearranged: $V=\frac{n k T}{q}\ln\left(\frac{I}{I_S}+1\right)$.
  2. Thermal voltage $\frac{kT}{q}\approx25.85\,\text{mV}$.
  3. $\frac{I}{I_S}+1=\frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-12}}+1=10^{9}+1\approx10^{9}$.
  4. $V=25.85\,\text{mV}\times\ln(10^{9})=25.85\,\text{mV}\times20.72\approx0.535\,\text{V}$.

Thus the diode conducts at about $0.54\,\text{V}$ forward bias.

Solar cell and its characteristics

Solar cell (photovoltaic cell) is a p‑n junction device that directly converts incident photon energy into electrical energy via the internal photo‑electric effect.

Working principle

  • Photons with energy $h\nu \geq E_g$ generate electron‑hole pairs in the depletion region.
  • Built‑in electric field separates carriers, producing a photocurrent $I_{ph}$.
  • The cell behaves like a current source $I_{ph}$ in parallel with a diode characterized by saturation current $I_0$.

Current–voltage relation

$I = I_{ph} - I_0\left( e^{\frac{qV}{kT}}-1\right)$

From this, key parameters are derived:

  • Open‑circuit voltage: $V_{oc}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_0}+1\right)$
  • Short‑circuit current: $I_{sc}=I_{ph}$ (when $V=0$)
  • Maximum power point: $P_{max}=V_{mp}I_{mp}$
  • Fill factor: $FF=\frac{V_{mp}I_{mp}}{V_{oc}I_{sc}}$
  • Efficiency: $\eta=\frac{P_{max}}{P_{\in}}\times100\%$, where $P_{\in}=G A$ (irradiance $G$ in W/m$^2$, area $A$ in m$^2$)

Typical characteristics

ParameterSymbolUnit
Open‑circuit voltage$V_{oc}$V
Short‑circuit current$I_{sc}$A
Maximum power$P_{max}$W
Fill factor$FF$—
Efficiency$\eta$%

Solved example

Given $I_{ph}=30\,\text{mA}$, $I_0=1\times10^{-12}\,\text{A}$, $T=300\,\text{K}$, find $V_{oc}$.

Constants: $k=1.38\times10^{-23}\,\text{J K}^{-1}$, $q=1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. Compute $\frac{kT}{q}=\frac{1.38\times10^{-23}\times300}{1.6\times10^{-19}}\approx0.02585\,\text{V}$. Ratio $\frac{I_{ph}}{I_0}=\frac{30\times10^{-3}}{1\times10^{-12}}=3\times10^{10}$. Hence $V_{oc}=0.02585\,\ln(3\times10^{10}+1)\approx0.02585\times24.12\approx0.62\,\text{V}$.

Thus the solar cell delivers an open‑circuit voltage of approximately $0.62\,\text{V}$ under the given conditions.

Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT): operation and characteristics

Definition: A Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) is a three‑layer semiconductor device (NPN or PNP) in which current flow in the collector‑emitter circuit is controlled by a small base current.

Key Parameters:

  • $I_C$ – collector current (A)
  • $I_B$ – base current (A)
  • $I_E$ – emitter current (A)
  • $V_{BE}$ – base‑emitter voltage (V)
  • $V_{CE}$ – collector‑emitter voltage (V)
  • $\alpha = \frac{I_C}{I_E}$ – common‑base current gain (unitless)
  • $\beta = \frac{I_C}{I_B}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}$ – common‑emitter current gain (unitless)

Fundamental Relations:

RelationExpression
Emitter current$I_E = I_C + I_B$
Collector current$I_C = \beta I_B$
Transconductance$g_m = \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{BE}} = \frac{I_C}{V_T}$, $V_T \approx 26\,\text{mV}$ at 300\,K
Small‑signal emitter resistance$r_e = \frac{1}{g_m} = \frac{V_T}{I_E}$

Operating Regions:

  • Cut‑off: $I_B \approx 0$, $I_C \approx 0$.
  • Active (forward‑active): $V_{BE} \approx 0.7\,\text{V}$, $V_{CB}$ reverse‑biased, $I_C = \beta I_B$.
  • Saturation: $V_{CE} \le 0.2\,\text{V}$, both junctions forward‑biased.

Example:

Given $I_B = 20\,\mu\text{A}$, $\beta = 100$, $V_{CC}=10\,\text{V}$, $R_C=1\,\text{k}\Omega$, $R_E=500\,\Omega$.

Calculate:

  • $I_C = \beta I_B = 100 \times 20\,\mu\text{A} = 2\,\text{mA}$.
  • $I_E = I_C + I_B \approx 2.02\,\text{mA}$.
  • $V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_C R_C - I_E R_E = 10 - (2\,\text{mA})(1\,\text{k}\Omega) - (2.02\,\text{mA})(500\,\Omega) \approx 5.99\,\text{V}$.

The transistor operates in the active region since $V_{CE} > 0.2\,\text{V}$.

Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor (MOSFET): structure and operation

Definition: A Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor (MOSFET) is a voltage‑controlled unipolar device in which a conductive channel is formed at the surface of a semiconductor (usually $n$‑type) beneath an insulating oxide layer when a gate voltage $V_{GS}$ exceeds a threshold voltage $V_T$.

Structure: The MOSFET consists of a gate (metal or heavily doped polysilicon), a thin oxide ($SiO_2$) of thickness $t_{ox}$, and a source‑drain region on the semiconductor substrate. The channel width $W$ and length $L$ determine current capability.

Operation Regions:

  • Cut‑off: $V_{GS}
  • Linear (triode): $V_{GS}>V_T$ and $V_{DS}$$I_D=\mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS}-V_T)V_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right]$$
  • Saturation: $V_{DS}\ge V_{GS}-V_T$ → channel pinches off at drain \end.
    $$I_D=\frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$$

Key Parameters:

SymbolExpressionSI Unit
$V_T$Threshold voltageV
$\mu_n$Electron mobilitym$^2$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$
$C_{ox}$Oxide capacitance per unit area = $\varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$F m$^{-2}$
$g_m$Transconductance $=\partial I_D/\partial V_{GS}=\mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_T)$S (A V$^{-1}$)

Example (Linear Region):

Given $\mu_n=450\ \text{cm}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, $C_{ox}=10\ \text{nF/cm}^2$, $W/L=10$, $V_T=1\ \text{V}$, $V_{GS}=3\ \text{V}$, $V_{DS}=0.5\ \text{V}$, find $I_D$.

  1. Convert units: $\mu_n=450\times10^{-4}=0.045\ \text{m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, $C_{ox}=10\times10^{-9}/10^{-4}=1\times10^{-4}\ \text{F m}^{-2}$.
  2. Compute $k=\mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}=0.045\times1\times10^{-4}\times10=4.5\times10^{-5}\ \text{A V}^{-2}$.
  3. Use linear‑region formula:
    $$I_D=4.5\times10^{-5}\left[(3-1)\times0.5-\frac{0.5^2}{2}\right]=4.5\times10^{-5}(1-0.125)=3.94\times10^{-5}\ \text{A}$$
  4. Thus $I_D\approx39\ \mu\text{A}$.
