1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator. The defining characteristic of a semiconductor is the presence of a bandgap $E_g$ that is small enough (typically 0.1–4 eV) to allow thermal excitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band at ordinary temperatures, yet large enough to prevent spontaneous conduction in the absence of external stimuli. Intrinsic semiconductors are pure crystals where the number of electrons promoted to the conduction band equals the number of holes left in the valence band; the intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i$ is given by $$n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\,e^{-E_g/(2kT)}$$ where $N_c$ and $N_v$ are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands respectively, $k$ is Boltzmann’s constant, and $T$ is absolute temperature. Extrinsic semiconductors are intentionally doped with donor or acceptor impurities. Donor atoms (n‑type) introduce shallow energy levels just below the conduction band, providing extra electrons; acceptor atoms (p‑type) create levels just above the valence band, creating holes. The resulting majority carrier concentration can be approximated by the dopant concentration, while the minority carrier concentration is suppressed by the law of mass action $np = n_i^2$. Doping shifts the Fermi level $E_F$ toward the conduction band for n‑type or toward the valence band for p‑type, thereby altering the equilibrium carrier distribution. The concept of a quasi‑Fermi level becomes essential under non‑equilibrium conditions such as illumination or applied bias, where separate quasi‑Fermi levels for electrons and holes describe the carrier populations. Understanding these fundamentals is crucial for predicting the behavior of semiconductor devices under various operating conditions.

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

The electronic structure of a crystalline semiconductor is described by the band theory of solids. Electrons in a periodic potential form allowed energy bands separated by forbidden gaps. The valence band is typically filled with electrons, while the conduction band is empty at zero temperature. The width of the bandgap $E_g$ determines the optical and electrical properties: a small $E_g$ allows thermal excitation of carriers, whereas a large $E_g$ results in insulating behavior. The density of states (DOS) function $g(E)$ quantifies the number of electronic states per unit energy per unit volume. For a parabolic band, the DOS in the conduction band is $$g_c(E) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m^*_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{E-E_c}$$ where $m^*_e$ is the effective mass of electrons and $E_c$ is the band edge. A similar expression holds for holes with effective mass $m^*_h$ and valence band edge $E_v$. The effective mass encapsulates the curvature of the band and reflects how carriers respond to external forces. The concept of band bending arises when an external electric field or a junction is introduced, causing the energy bands to tilt spatially. This bending is responsible for the formation of depletion regions and built‑in potentials in p‑n junctions. The interplay between band structure, DOS, and carrier statistics underpins the design of devices such as diodes, transistors, and photovoltaic cells.

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

Carrier concentration in a semiconductor is governed by Fermi–Dirac statistics. The probability that an electronic state of energy $E$ is occupied is $$f(E) = \frac{1}{1+e^{(E-E_F)/kT}}$$ where $E_F$ is the Fermi level. Integrating $f(E)$ over the conduction band yields the electron concentration $$n = \int_{E_c}^{\infty} g_c(E) f(E) \, dE$$ and similarly for holes $$p = \int_{-\infty}^{E_v} g_v(E) [1-f(E)] \, dE$$. In non‑degenerate semiconductors, where $E_F$ lies well within the bandgap, the Maxwell–Boltzmann approximation simplifies these integrals, leading to the familiar expressions $n = N_c e^{-(E_c-E_F)/kT}$ and $p = N_v e^{-(E_F-E_v)/kT}$. The product $np$ remains equal to $n_i^2$ regardless of doping, a consequence of detailed balance. Temperature dependence is crucial: as $T$ increases, $n_i$ rises exponentially, enhancing conductivity. The intrinsic carrier concentration can be approximated as $$n_i \approx 5.2\times10^{15}\,\text{cm}^{-3}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right)$$ for silicon at room temperature. The Fermi level position also dictates the direction of diffusion and drift currents. In equilibrium, diffusion of carriers from high to low concentration is counterbalanced by drift under the internal electric field, resulting in zero net current. This equilibrium condition is the foundation of the diffusion equation and the continuity equation used to model transient behavior in semiconductor devices.

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

Electrical conductivity $\sigma$ in a semiconductor arises from both electrons and holes: $$\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$$ where $q$ is the elementary charge, $\mu_n$ and $\mu_p$ are the mobilities of electrons and holes respectively. Mobility quantifies how quickly carriers drift under an electric field $E$: $v_d = \mu E$. It is determined by scattering mechanisms such as phonon scattering, impurity scattering, and lattice defects. At low temperatures, impurity scattering dominates, leading to higher mobilities; as temperature rises, phonon scattering increases, reducing mobility. The Hall effect provides a powerful experimental method to measure carrier concentration and mobility: applying a magnetic field $B$ perpendicular to the current induces a transverse Hall voltage $V_H$, related to the Hall coefficient $R_H$ by $$R_H = \frac{E_y}{j_x B} = \frac{1}{q(p-n)}$$ for a single carrier type. The sign of $R_H$ indicates whether electrons or holes dominate. Drift and diffusion currents are described by the drift-diffusion equations: $$J_n = q n \mu_n E + q D_n \frac{dn}{dx}$$ and $$J_p = q p \mu_p E - q D_p \frac{dp}{dx}$$ where $D_n$ and $D_p$ are diffusion coefficients linked to mobilities via the Einstein relation $D = \mu kT/q$. These equations form the backbone of semiconductor device modeling, enabling the prediction of current–voltage characteristics under various biasing conditions.

Physics · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

A p‑n junction is formed by joining p‑type and n‑type semiconductors. At the interface, electrons diffuse from the n‑side to the p‑side and holes diffuse in the opposite direction, creating a depletion region devoid of free carriers. The diffusion of carriers establishes an internal electric field, which in turn creates a built‑in potential $V_{bi}$ that opposes further diffusion. The equilibrium condition is expressed by the relation $$V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right)$$ where $N_A$ and $N_D$ are acceptor and donor concentrations. Under forward bias, the external voltage reduces the barrier, allowing carriers to cross the junction and recombine, resulting in a forward current that follows the Shockley diode equation $$I = I_S\left(e^{qV/(kT)}-1\right)$$ where $I_S$ is the saturation current. Reverse bias increases the barrier, widening the depletion region and suppressing current to a small leakage value. The width of the depletion region $W$ depends on the applied voltage: $$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\left(\frac{N_A+N_D}{N_A N_D}\right)(V_{bi}-V)}$$ where $\varepsilon_s$ is the semiconductor permittivity. The p‑n junction underpins all semiconductor devices: diodes, transistors, solar cells, and photodetectors. Understanding the physics of depletion, built‑in potential, and carrier transport is essential for designing efficient devices with desired characteristics such as low forward voltage drop, high reverse breakdown voltage, or high responsivity to light.

Transistor operation relies on the manipulation of carrier flow in a p‑n‑p or n‑p‑n structure. In a bipolar junction transistor (BJT), the emitter injects carriers into the base, which are then swept into the collector by the base–collector junction. The current amplification factor $\beta$ is defined as the ratio of collector current $I_C$ to base current $I_B$. Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

1. Intrinsic Carrier Concentration $n_i$

For an intrinsic semiconductor the electron and hole concentrations are equal: $n = p = n_i$. The carrier concentration is obtained from the Fermi–Dirac distribution integrated over the conduction and valence bands. Under non‑degenerate conditions the Maxwell–Boltzmann approximation is valid and we can write

$$ n = N_C \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_C - E_F}{kT}\right), \qquad p = N_V \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_F - E_V}{kT}\right) $$

Multiplying the two expressions gives the mass action law (see section 2 below). For an intrinsic semiconductor $E_F$ lies close to the middle of the bandgap $E_g$, so $E_C - E_F \approx E_F - E_V \approx E_g/2$. Hence

$$ n_i^2 = N_C N_V \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{kT}\right) $$

Taking the square root yields the intrinsic carrier concentration:

$$ n_i = \sqrt{N_C N_V}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{2kT}\right) $$

where $N_C$ and $N_V$ are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands respectively. They are given by

$$ N_C = 2\left(\frac{2\pi m_n^* kT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}, \qquad N_V = 2\left(\frac{2\pi m_p^* kT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} $$

with $m_n^*$ and $m_p^*$ being the effective masses of electrons and holes, $k$ the Boltzmann constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, and $h$ Planck’s constant.

2. Mass Action Law

For any semiconductor in thermal equilibrium the product of electron and hole concentrations is constant:

$$ np = n_i^2 $$

Derivation: Starting from the expressions for $n$ and $p$ above, we have

$$ np = N_C N_V \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_C - E_F}{kT}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_F - E_V}{kT}\right) $$

which simplifies to

$$ np = N_C N_V \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_C - E_V}{kT}\right) = N_C N_V \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_g}{kT}\right) $$

Thus $np = n_i^2$ as defined in the previous section.

3. Depletion Width $W$ in a One‑Dimensional p–n Junction

Consider a step junction with doping concentrations $N_A$ (acceptor) and $N_D$ (donor). Under depletion approximation the space charge region extends $x_p$ into the p‑side and $x_n$ into the n‑side. Charge neutrality gives

$$ N_A x_p = N_D x_n $$

The electric field satisfies Poisson’s equation. Integrating once gives the potential drop across each side:

$$ V_p = \frac{q N_A x_p^2}{2\varepsilon_s}, \qquad V_n = \frac{q N_D x_n^2}{2\varepsilon_s} $$

where $q$ is the elementary charge and $\varepsilon_s$ is the semiconductor permittivity. The total depletion width is $W = x_p + x_n$ and the built‑in potential $V_{bi} = V_p + V_n$. Eliminating $x_p$ and $x_n$ using the charge neutrality condition yields

$$ W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)V_{bi}} $$

In the special case of an asymmetric junction ($N_A \gg N_D$) the depletion region lies almost entirely in the lightly doped side, giving $W \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s V_{bi}}{q N_D}}$.

4. Built‑In Potential $V_{bi}$

For an abrupt junction the built‑in potential is obtained by equating the Fermi levels on both sides. The equilibrium condition gives

$$ V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right) $$

Derivation: The electron concentration on the n‑side is $n_n = N_D$ and the hole concentration on the p‑side is $p_p = N_A$. Using the mass action law $n_n p_p = n_i^2$ and the definition of the Fermi level shift, we get the above expression. This potential is typically a few tenths of a volt for silicon at room temperature.

5. Shockley Diode Equation

The current through a p–n junction under an applied bias $V$ is dominated by minority carrier injection. The minority carrier concentration at the edge of the depletion region follows

$$ \Delta n(0) = n_{p0}\left(e^{\frac{qV}{kT}} - 1\right), \qquad \Delta p(0) = p_{n0}\left(e^{\frac{qV}{kT}} - 1\right) $$

where $n_{p0} = n_i^2/N_A$ and $p_{n0} = n_i^2/N_D$ are the equilibrium minority concentrations. The diffusion current density is then

$$ J = q\left(\frac{D_n}{L_n}\Delta n(0) + \frac{D_p}{L_p}\Delta p(0)\right) $$

Combining the terms and defining the saturation current density $J_s$ gives the Shockley equation:

$$ J = J_s\left(e^{\frac{qV}{kT}} - 1\right) $$

with

$$ J_s = q\left(\frac{D_n n_i^2}{L_n N_A} + \frac{D_p n_i^2}{L_p N_D}\right) $$

$D_n$ and $D_p$ are the diffusion coefficients, $L_n$ and $L_p$ the diffusion lengths, and $q$ the elementary charge.

6. Minority Carrier Diffusion Length $L$

Minority carriers recombine with a characteristic lifetime $\tau$. The diffusion length is defined as the average distance a minority carrier travels before recombining:

$$ L = \sqrt{D \tau} $$

For electrons in the p‑side $L_n = \sqrt{D_n \tau_n}$ and for holes in the n‑side $L_p = \sqrt{D_p \tau_p}$. The diffusion coefficient is related to mobility $\mu$ by the Einstein relation:

$$ D = \frac{kT}{q}\mu $$

7. Recombination Lifetime $\tau$

In a non‑degenerate semiconductor the recombination rate $R$ is proportional to the excess carrier concentration $\Delta n$:

$$ R = \frac{\Delta n}{\tau} $$

For Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination mediated by deep levels, the lifetime depends on the trap density $N_t$ and capture cross‑sections $\sigma_n$, $\sigma_p$:

$$ \tau = \frac{1}{\sigma_n v_{th} N_t} $$

where $v_{th}$ is the thermal velocity of carriers. In practice, $\tau$ is extracted from time‑resolved photoconductivity or minority carrier lifetime measurements.
