1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Atomic Theory
Foundations of the Atom

The concept of the atom has evolved through a series of scientific revolutions. Dalton’s atomic theory (1803) posited that all matter is composed of indivisible particles called atoms, each element having its own unique type of atom. The discovery of the electron by J. J. Thomson in 1897 (using cathode ray tubes) revealed that atoms are divisible, leading to the plum pudding model. Rutherford’s gold foil experiment (1911) demonstrated that a small, dense, positively charged nucleus is surrounded by a cloud of electrons, giving rise to the planetary model. Bohr’s quantum theory (1913) introduced quantised orbits, where the angular momentum of an electron is an integer multiple of $\hbar$, and the energy levels are given by $$E_n = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}$$, with $n$ being the principal quantum number. The modern quantum mechanical model replaces fixed orbits with probability clouds or orbitals, described by solutions to the Schrödinger equation. Electrons occupy orbitals characterised by four quantum numbers: $n$, $l$, $m_l$, and $m_s$, which determine the size, shape, orientation, and spin of the orbital respectively. The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers. The periodic table is organised such that elements with similar valence electron configurations exhibit similar chemical properties, reflecting the underlying atomic structure. The evolution from Dalton to the quantum mechanical model illustrates how experimental evidence drives theoretical refinement, culminating in a comprehensive description of atomic behaviour that underpins modern physics and chemistry.

Physics · Periodic Trends
Ionisation Energy, Electronegativity and Atomic Size

Ionisation energy (IE) is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion. It generally increases across a period from left to right due to the increasing nuclear charge and decreasing atomic radius, and decreases down a group as additional electron shells are added. The first ionisation energy can be approximated by $$E_{\text{ion}} \approx \frac{Z_{\text{eff}}^2 R_H}{n^2}$$ where $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective nuclear charge and $R_H$ is the Rydberg constant for hydrogen. Electronegativity, a measure of an atom’s ability to attract electrons in a bond, follows a similar trend: it is highest for fluorine and lowest for cesium. Atomic radius, defined as the distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron shell, decreases across a period and increases down a group. These trends are interrelated through the concept of effective nuclear charge, $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$, where $Z$ is the atomic number and $S$ is the shielding constant. Shielding arises because inner electrons partially block the attraction between the nucleus and outer electrons. Understanding these periodic trends is essential for predicting chemical reactivity and bonding behaviour in both inorganic and organic chemistry.

Physics · Quantum Numbers
Intuitive Picture of Electron Configurations

The quantum mechanical model assigns each electron a set of four quantum numbers: the principal quantum number $n$ (energy level), the azimuthal quantum number $l$ (orbital shape), the magnetic quantum number $m_l$ (orbital orientation), and the spin quantum number $m_s$ (electron spin). The Pauli exclusion principle limits each orbital to a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins. The Aufbau principle dictates that electrons fill the lowest available energy states first, following the order $1s \rightarrow 2s \rightarrow 2p \rightarrow 3s \rightarrow 3p \rightarrow 4s \rightarrow 3d \rightarrow 4p$, etc. Hund’s rule further states that electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing. These rules explain the observed electron configurations of elements and the structure of the periodic table. For example, the configuration of iron, $\mathrm{Fe}$, is $[Ar] 3d^6 4s^2$, indicating that the $3d$ orbitals are partially filled while the $4s$ orbital is fully occupied. The distribution of electrons among orbitals determines magnetic properties, ionisation energies, and the types of chemical bonds an element can form.

Physics · Exceptions and Anomalies
Special Cases in Atomic Structure

While the general rules of electron configuration hold for most elements, several exceptions arise due to electron-electron interactions and relativistic effects. Transition metals exhibit partially filled $d$ subshells, leading to variable oxidation states and complex spectra. The lanthanides and actinides have partially filled $f$ subshells, which are shielded by $d$ and $s$ electrons, resulting in similar chemical behaviour across the series. Noble gases, with completely filled $s$ and $p$ shells, are chemically inert. Additionally, the element mercury shows a filled $d$ shell but a partially filled $s$ shell, leading to a stable electron configuration that deviates from the expected order. Relativistic contraction of $s$ and $p$ orbitals in heavy elements such as gold and lead also causes anomalous colours and densities. These exceptions highlight the importance of considering both quantum mechanical principles and empirical observations when analysing atomic structure.

Physics · Worked Example
Calculating the Bohr Radius of Hydrogen

The Bohr model predicts that the radius of the $n$‑th orbit of a hydrogen atom is given by $$r_n = a_0 n^2$$ where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius, approximately $0.529 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}$. For the ground state ($n=1$), the radius is $$r_1 = 0.529 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}.$$ This value matches the experimentally determined average distance between the proton and electron in a hydrogen atom. For the first excited state ($n=2$), the radius becomes $$r_2 = a_0 (2)^2 = 4 a_0 = 2.116 \times 10^{-10}\,\text{m}.$$ The energy difference between these two levels is $$\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = -\frac{13.6}{4} \text{ eV} + 13.6 \text{ eV} = 10.2 \text{ eV},$$ which corresponds to the wavelength of the Lyman-$\alpha$ spectral line. This worked example demonstrates how the Bohr model provides quantitative predictions for atomic dimensions and spectral lines, laying the groundwork for the development of quantum mechanics.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

1. Bohr’s Postulates and Energy Levels of a Hydrogen‑like Atom

Bohr introduced two fundamental postulates to explain the discrete spectral lines of hydrogen:

  1. Electrons move in circular orbits about the nucleus under the Coulomb attraction without radiating energy. The centripetal force is provided by the electrostatic force: $$\frac{m_e v_n^{2}}{r_n}= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^{2}}{r_n^{2}}$$ where $m_e$ is the electron mass, $v_n$ the speed in the $n^{\text{th}}$ orbit, $r_n$ the orbital radius, $Z$ the atomic number ( $Z=1$ for hydrogen) and $e$ the elementary charge.
  2. Only those orbits are allowed for which the angular momentum is an integral multiple of $h/2\pi$: $$m_e v_n r_n = n\hbar \qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$ Here $\hbar = h/2\pi$ and $n$ is the principal quantum number.

From the two equations we eliminate $v_n$ to obtain the allowed radius $r_n$.

From the angular momentum condition, $$v_n = \frac{n\hbar}{m_e r_n}$$ Substituting in the centripetal‑Coulomb balance, $$\frac{m_e}{r_n}\left(\frac{n\hbar}{m_e r_n}\right)^{2}= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^{2}}{r_n^{2}}$$ Simplifying, $$\frac{n^{2}\hbar^{2}}{m_e r_n^{3}} = \frac{Z e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n^{2}}$$ $$\Rightarrow r_n = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0 n^{2}\hbar^{2}}{Z m_e e^{2}}$$ The quantity $$a_0 = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \hbar^{2}}{m_e e^{2}} = 0.529\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}$$ is called the Bohr radius. Hence, $$r_n = \frac{n^{2}a_0}{Z}$$

Next, the total energy $E_n$ of the electron in the $n^{\text{th}}$ orbit is the sum of kinetic and potential energies:

$$E_n = K_n + U_n = \frac{1}{2}m_e v_n^{2} - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^{2}}{r_n}$$

Using the centripetal relation $m_e v_n^{2}= \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^{2}}{r_n}$, we find $K_n = \frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z e^{2}}{r_n}$, therefore

$$E_n = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Z e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n} - \frac{Z e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n}= -\frac{Z e^{2}}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_n}$$

Substituting $r_n$ from the previous result,

$$E_n = -\frac{Z^{2} m_e e^{4}}{32\pi^{2}\varepsilon_0^{2}\hbar^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}$$

Defining the Rydberg constant $R_{\infty}= \frac{m_e e^{4}}{8\varepsilon_0^{2}h^{3}c}$, the energy expression simplifies to the familiar form

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}\; Z^{2}}{n^{2}}$$

This result explains why spectral lines of hydrogen appear at discrete wavelengths.

2. Derivation of the de Broglie Wavelength and Its Consistency with Bohr Quantization

Louis de Broglie postulated that any particle of momentum $p$ possesses an associated wavelength $\lambda$ given by

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

For an electron moving in a circular Bohr orbit of radius $r_n$, the circumference must accommodate an integral number of wavelengths for a standing wave condition:

$$2\pi r_n = n\lambda = n\frac{h}{p}= n\frac{h}{m_e v_n}$$

Rearranging,

$$m_e v_n r_n = n\frac{h}{2\pi}= n\hbar$$

which is precisely Bohr’s second postulate. Hence the de Broglie hypothesis provides a wave‑mechanical justification for the quantisation of angular momentum.

3. Rydberg Formula from Bohr’s Model

The frequency $\nu$ of a photon emitted when an electron makes a transition from an initial orbit $n_i$ to a final orbit $n_f$ ($n_i>n_f$) is obtained from the energy difference:

$$h\nu = E_{n_f} - E_{n_i}= -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}\; Z^{2}}{n_f^{2}} + \frac{13.6\ \text{eV}\; Z^{2}}{n_i^{2}}$$

Dividing by $hc$ and converting eV to joules, we obtain the wavenumber $\tilde{\nu}=1/\lambda$ as

$$\tilde{\nu}= R_Z\left(\frac{1}{n_f^{2}}-\frac{1}{n_i^{2}}\right)$$

where the Rydberg constant for a nucleus of charge $Z$ is

$$R_Z = R_{\infty} Z^{2},\qquad R_{\infty}=1.097373\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}$$

This is the celebrated Rydberg formula, valid for hydrogen‑like ions (single‑electron systems).

4. Heisenberg’s Uncertainty Principle – A Qualitative Derivation

Consider a wave packet formed by superposing plane waves with a spread in wave‑numbers $\Delta k$. The spatial extent $\Delta x$ of the packet is inversely related to $\Delta k$:

$$\Delta x\;\Delta k \approx 1$$

Since momentum $p=\hbar k$, the spread in momentum is $\Delta p = \hbar\Delta k$, giving

$$\Delta x\;\Delta p \approx \hbar$$

Replacing the approximate sign with an inequality (the rigorous derivation uses Fourier analysis) yields the Heisenberg uncertainty relation

$$\boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}}$$

Similarly, for energy and time, one obtains

$$\Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

These relations set fundamental limits on the simultaneous measurability of conjugate variables.

5. Time‑Independent Schrödinger Equation for a Particle in a One‑Dimensional Infinite Potential Well

For a particle of mass $m$ confined between $x=0$ and $x=a$ with $V(x)=0$ inside and $V(x)=\infty$ outside, the time‑independent Schrödinger equation reads

$$-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}\psi(x)}{dx^{2}} = E\psi(x)$$

Rearranging,

$$\frac{d^{2}\psi}{dx^{2}} + k^{2}\psi =0,\qquad k^{2}= \frac{2mE}{\hbar^{2}}$$

The general solution is

$$\psi(x)=A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$$

Applying the boundary condition $\psi(0)=0$ forces $B=0$. The second boundary condition $\psi(a)=0$ gives

$$\sin(ka)=0 \;\Rightarrow\; ka = n\pi,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$

Thus the allowed wave‑numbers are $k_n = n\pi/a$ and the quantised energies become

$$E_n = \frac{\hbar^{2}k_n^{2}}{2m}= \frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2m a^{2}} = \frac{n^{2}h^{2}}{8m a^{2}}$$

The normalized eigenfunctions are

$$\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)$$

This simple model illustrates how confinement leads to discrete energy levels, a cornerstone of atomic physics.
