Bohr’s model of hydrogen atom and postulates

Bohr’s postulates for hydrogen‑like atoms

  • Electrons move in certain stable circular orbits about the nucleus without radiating energy.
  • Only those orbits are allowed for which the angular momentum is an integral multiple of reduced Planck constant: $L=n\hbar$, where $n=1,2,3\dots$.
  • Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps between two allowed orbits. The frequency of the emitted photon is given by $\nu=\dfrac{E_i-E_f}{h}$.

Key formulas (SI units)

QuantityExpressionUnit
Radius of $n^{th}$ orbit$r_n=\dfrac{n^2 h^2}{4\pi^2 m_e k e^2}=n^2 a_0$metre (m)
Energy of $n^{th}$ level$E_n=-\dfrac{m_e k^2 e^4}{2 h^2}\dfrac{1}{n^2}= -\dfrac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}$joule (J) or electron‑volt (eV)
Bohr radius$a_0=\dfrac{h^2}{4\pi^2 m_e k e^2}=5.29\times10^{-11}$metre (m)
Rydberg constant (in wave number)$R_H=\dfrac{m_e k^2 e^4}{8 h^3 c}=1.097\times10^7\ \text{m}^{-1}$m$^{-1}$

Example: Find the wavelength of the photon emitted when an electron in hydrogen falls from $n_i=3$ to $n_f=2$.

Energy difference $\Delta E=E_{3}-E_{2}= -\dfrac{13.6}{9}+\dfrac{13.6}{4}=1.89\text{ eV}$.

Photon wavelength $\lambda=\dfrac{hc}{\Delta E}=\dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}\times3.00\times10^{8}}{1.89\times1.602\times10^{-19}}=6.56\times10^{-7}\text{ m}=656\text{ nm}$ (Balmer series).

Hydrogen spectrum and Rydberg’s formula

Hydrogen Spectrum: When a hydrogen atom transitions between two energy levels $E_n$ and $E_m$, a photon of frequency $\nu$ is emitted or absorbed given by $\nu=\frac{E_n-E_m}{h}$, where $h$ is Planck's constant ($6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$). The corresponding wavelength $\lambda$ satisfies $\frac{1}{\lambda}=\frac{\nu}{c}=\frac{E_n-E_m}{hc}$.

Rydberg’s Formula (empirical):

$$\frac{1}{\lambda}=R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$$

where $R_H=1.097\times10^7\,\text{m}^{-1}$ is the Rydberg constant for hydrogen, $n_2>n_1$, and $n_1,n_2$ are integers (principal quantum numbers). SI unit of $\lambda$ is metre (m).

Derivation from Bohr Model:

  • Energy of $n^{\text{th}}$ orbit: $E_n=-\frac{m_e e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^2}\frac{1}{n^2}= -\frac{13.6\,\text{eV}}{n^2}$.
  • Energy difference $\Delta E=E_{n_2}-E_{n_1}=13.6\,\text{eV}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$.
  • Using $\frac{1}{\lambda}=\frac{\Delta E}{hc}$ gives Rydberg’s formula with $R_H=\frac{m_e e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^3 c}=1.097\times10^7\,\text{m}^{-1}$.

Series \in Hydrogen:

SeriesFinal $n_1$Region
Lyman1UV
Balmer2Visible
Paschen3IR

Solved Example: Find the wavelength of the Balmer line corresponding \to transition $n_2=4\to n_1=2$.

Solution:

  1. Use Rydberg’s formula: $\frac{1}{\lambda}=R_H\left(\frac{1}{2^2}-\frac{1}{4^2}\right)=1.097\times10^7\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{16}\right)$.
  2. Compute the bracket: $\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{16}=\frac{4-1}{16}=\frac{3}{16}=0.1875$.
  3. $\frac{1}{\lambda}=1.097\times10^7\times0.1875=2.058\times10^6\,\text{m}^{-1}$.
  4. Hence $\lambda=\frac{1}{2.058\times10^6}=4.86\times10^{-7}\,\text{m}=486\,\text{nm}$ (blue‑green line).

Key points: Rydberg’s constant $R_H$ is universal for hydrogen; the formula predicts all spectral lines across series and matches experimental data within $10^{-4}$ relative error.

De Broglie hypothesis and matter waves

De Broglie hypothesis: Every particle with momentum $p$ exhibits wave‑like behaviour characterised by a wavelength $\lambda$ given by

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

The associated frequency $f$ is

$$f = \frac{E}{h}$$

with $E$ the total energy of the particle.

  • SI unit of $\lambda$: metre (m).
  • Momentum $p = mv$ (non‑relativistic) or $p = \gamma mv$ (relativistic).
  • For photons $p = \frac{h}{\lambda}$ and $E = hf$.
QuantitySymbolFormulaSI Unit
De Broglie wavelength$\lambda$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$m
Momentum$p$$p = mv$ (non‑rel.)kg·m·s$^{-1}$
Frequency$f$$f = \frac{E}{h}$Hz

Example: An electron is accelerated through a potential difference $V = 150\,\text{V}$. Find its de Broglie wavelength.

Solution:

  1. Kinetic energy $K = eV$, where $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}$. Hence $K = 1.602\times10^{-19}\times150 = 2.403\times10^{-17}\,\text{J}$.
  2. Non‑relativistic momentum $p = \sqrt{2mK}$, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$. So $p = \sqrt{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times2.403\times10^{-17}} = 6.62\times10^{-24}\,\text{kg·m·s}^{-1}$.
  3. De Broglie wavelength $\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{6.62\times10^{-24}} = 1.00\times10^{-10}\,\text{m}$ (≈ 0.1 nm).

Thus the electron exhibits wave‑like behaviour with wavelength comparable to inter‑atomic spacings, justifying electron diffraction experiments.

Quantum numbers and their significance

Quantum Numbers are a set of four integers that uniquely specify the state of an electron in an atom.

  • Principal quantum number ($n$): $n=1,2,3,\dots$; determines energy level and size of orbital. Energy (hydrogen‑like) $E_n = -\dfrac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}$.
  • Azimuthal (orbital) quantum number ($l$): $l=0,1,\dots ,n-1$; defines shape of orbital (s, p, d, f). Number of subshells in a shell = $n$.
  • Magnetic quantum number ($m_l$): $m_l = -l,-l+1,\dots ,+l$; gives orientation of orbital. Number of orbitals in a subshell = $2l+1$.
  • Spin quantum number ($m_s$): $m_s = +\dfrac{1}{2}$ or $-\dfrac{1}{2}$; represents intrinsic angular momentum of electron.

All quantum numbers are dimensionless (SI unit: –). The maximum electrons that can occupy a shell is $2n^2$, and a subshell can hold $2(2l+1)$ electrons.

Quantum numberSymbolAllowed values
Principal$n$$1,2,3,\dots$
Azimuthal$l$$0\le l\le n-1$
Magnetic$m_l$$-l\le m_l\le +l$
Spin$m_s$$\pm\dfrac{1}{2}$

Example: Find all possible sets of quantum numbers for an electron in the $3p$ subshell.

Solution: For $3p$, $n=3$ and $l=1$ (p‑orbital). Hence $m_l$ can be $-1,0,+1$ and $m_s$ can be $+\dfrac{1}{2}$ or $-\dfrac{1}{2}$. The six allowed states are:

  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=-1,m_s=+\dfrac{1}{2}\}$
  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=-1,m_s=-\dfrac{1}{2}\}$
  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=0,m_s=+\dfrac{1}{2}\}$
  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=0,m_s=-\dfrac{1}{2}\}$
  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=+1,m_s=+\dfrac{1}{2}\}$
  • $\{n=3,l=1,m_l=+1,m_s=-\dfrac{1}{2}\}$

Thus a $3p$ subshell can accommodate $6$ electrons, consistent with $2(2l+1)=6$.

Schrödinger equation for hydrogen atom

Schrödinger Equation for Hydrogen Atom

Time‑independent Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates for a single electron moving in the Coulomb potential $V(r)= -\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}$ is

$$ -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\nabla^{2}\psi(r,\theta,\phi) - \frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}\psi = E\psi $$

Separation of variables gives $\psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n l}(r)Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\phi)$ where $Y_{l}^{m}$ are spherical harmonics and the radial part satisfies

$$ -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{e}}\left[\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{2}\frac{dR}{dr}\right)-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}R\right] - \frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}R = ER $$

Solution leads \to quantised energy levels

$$ E_{n}= -\frac{m_{e}e^{4}}{8\varepsilon_{0}^{2}h^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}} = -13.6\,\text{eV}\,\frac{1}{n^{2}} $$

Example

Find the energy of the electron \in the $n=3$ state.

Using $E_{n}= -13.6\,\text{eV}/n^{2}$,

$$ E_{3}= -\frac{13.6}{3^{2}}\,\text{eV}= -\frac{13.6}{9}\,\text{eV}= -1.51\,\text{eV} $$

The result matches the observed spectral line at 102.6 nm (Lyman series).

Atomic orbitals and probability density

Atomic orbital: A region in an atom where the probability of finding an electron is high. It is described by the wave‑function $\psi(r,\theta,\phi)$ obtained from the Schrödinger equation.

Probability density is $|\psi|^2$, having SI unit $\text{m}^{-3}$. The wave‑function is normalized as $$\int |\psi|^2 d\tau =1.$$

Key relations:

  • Radial part $R_{nl}(r)$ and angular part $Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)$: $\psi_{nlm}=R_{nl}(r)Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)$.
  • Radial probability distribution $P_{nl}(r)=4\pi r^2|R_{nl}(r)|^2$.
  • Energy of a hydrogen‑like atom $$E_n=-\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}$$ (SI unit J).

Quantum numbers:

SymbolRangePhysical meaning
$n$$1,2,3\dots$Principal – size and energy
$l$$0\le l\le n-1$Azimuthal – shape
$m_l$$-l\le m_l\le l$Magnetic – orientation
$m_s$$\pm\frac{1}{2}$Spin

Example: Find the radial distance at which the $2s$ orbital ($n=2,l=0$) has its first maximum.

For hydrogen, $R_{20}(r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a_0^3}}\left(2-\frac{r}{a_0}\right)e^{-r/2a_0}$, where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius. The radial distribution is $P(r)=4\pi r^2|R_{20}|^2$. Setting $\frac{dP}{dr}=0$ gives $\left(2-\frac{r}{a_0}\right)\left(1-\frac{r}{2a_0}\right)=0$, leading \to $r=a_0$ (first maximum) and $r=4a_0$ (second maximum). Thus the most probable distance for a $2s$ electron is $r=a_0\approx5.29\times10^{-11}\text{ m}$.

Electronic configuration and Aufbau principle

Electronic configuration is the distribution of $Z$ electrons among the atomic orbitals in the ground state of a neutral atom.

Aufbau principle states that electrons occupy the lowest‑energy orbital available before filling higher‑energy ones.

  • Orbitals are filled in the order given by increasing $n+\ell$ value (Madelung rule). If two subshells have the same $n+\ell$, the one with lower $n$ fills first.
  • Pauli exclusion principle: No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers.
  • Hund’s rule: For degenerate orbitals, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing.

The maximum number of electrons that a subshell with azimuthal quantum number $l$ can hold is derived from spin multiplicity:

$$N_{max}=2(2l+1)$$ where $l=0,1,2,3$ correspond \to $s,p,d,f$ respectively. SI unit of electron binding energy is the joule (J), commonly expressed \in electron‑volts (eV, $1\,\text{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}\,\text{J}$).

Example: Find the ground‑state configuration of oxygen ($Z=8$).

  1. Fill orbitals following the order: $1s$, $2s$, $2p$.
  2. $1s$ holds $2$ electrons → $1s^{2}$.
  3. $2s$ holds $2$ electrons → $2s^{2}$.

Thus, the electronic configuration of oxygen is $\boxed{1s^{2}\,2s^{2}\,2p^{4}}$.

Fine structure, Zeeman effect and Lamb shift

Fine Structure – Small splitting of hydrogen‑like spectral lines due to relativistic correction, spin‑orbit coupling and Darwin term.

Energy correction (Dirac theory):

Formula
$$\Delta E_{fs}= \frac{Z^4\alpha^2}{n^3}\left(\frac{1}{j+1/2}-\frac{3}{4n}\right)E_h$$

SI unit: joule (J). $E_h=4.36\times10^{-18}\,\text{J}$.

Zeeman Effect

  • Normal Zeeman (spin ignored): $$\Delta E = \mu_B B m_l$$
  • Anomalous Zeeman: $$\Delta E = \mu_B B g_J m_J$$ with $$g_J = 1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}$$

Bohr magneton $$\mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}=9.274\times10^{-24}\,\text{J/T}$$.

Lamb Shift – Difference between $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ levels \in hydrogen caused by vacuum fluctuations.

Approximate expression:

Formula
$$\Delta E_{Lamb}\approx \frac{\alpha^5 m_e c^2}{6\pi}\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}$$

Measured value: $1057\,\text{MHz}=4.37\times10^{-6}\,\text{eV}$.

Example: Normal Zeeman splitting for hydrogen $n=2$, $l=1$ \in $B=1\,\text{T}$.

Using $$\Delta E = \mu_B B m_l$$ with $m_l = -1,0,+1$:

  • $m_l=+1$: $$\Delta E = 9.274\times10^{-24}\,\text{J}$$
  • $m_l=0$: $$\Delta E = 0$$
  • $m_l=-1$: $$\Delta E = -9.274\times10^{-24}\,\text{J}$$

Corresponding wavelength shift $\Delta \lambda = \frac{\lambda^2}{hc}\Delta E$ (use $\lambda=656.3\,\text{nm}$ for H$_\alpha$).
