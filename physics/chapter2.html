NCERT Definitions Commonly asked in 1 Mark

1. Conservative Force

A specialized force field wherein the total work done in transporting an object between any two given points is completely independent of the specific path taken.

2. Electric Field

An aura or region surrounding a charged particle or object where another test charge experiences a definitive electrostatic force.

3. Electrostatic Potential ($V$)

The amount of external mechanical work required to bring a unit positive test charge from infinity up to a specific point without acceleration.

4. Work Done ($W$)

Energy transferred to or from a system via a force operating during displacement ($W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$).

5. Potential Energy ($U$)

The intrinsic energy stored inside a collective configuration of charges governed by their relative spatial positions.

6. Test Charge ($q_0$)

An infinitesimally small charge magnitude chosen expressly so it does not distort or perturb the primary electric field being measured.

7. Point Charge Potential

The localized electrical potential established at any point in space due to the presence of an isolated single point charge.

8. Electric Dipole

A structural pairing of two equal and opposite point charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated across a very small microscopic distance ($2a$).

9. Dipole Moment ($\vec{p}$)

A fundamental vector quantity measuring dipole strength, directed conventionally from negative charge toward positive charge.

10. Potential of Multi-Charges

The total scalar potential accumulated at a point $P$ resulting from an entire spatial configuration or system of discrete charges.

Electric Potential Due to a Point Charge Core Derivation

Derivation Objective: Derive an exhaustive mathematical expression for the electrostatic potential at any arbitrary distance $r$ from a source point charge $q$, and analyze the physical nature of this scalar potential field.

1. Conceptual Setup & Definition

Consider a positive source point charge $+q$ rigidly fixed at the origin $O$. Our objective is to determine the absolute electric potential at a designated observation point $P$ situated at a radial distance $r$ from $O$.

By fundamental definition, the electric potential $V$ at point $P$ equals the total external mechanical work done in transporting a unit positive test charge ($q_0 = +1$) slowly and without acceleration from infinity ($\infty$) up to point $P$ against electrostatic repulsive forces.

FIG 2.2: Coordinate Layout for Point Charge Potential

Fig 2.2: Electrostatic repulsion force vector $\vec{F}$ and incremental displacement $dx$ configuration.

2. Mathematical Derivation

Suppose a test charge $q_0$ is temporarily positioned at intermediate point $A$ at a distance $x$ from origin $O$. According to Coulomb's Law, the repulsive electrostatic force acting on charge $q_0$ is:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q q_0}{x^2}$$

This force $\vec{F}$ pushes radially outward away from charge $q$. If we move test charge $q_0$ infinitesimally through a small displacement $dx$ from point $A$ to point $B$ against this electrostatic force, the small work done $dW$ is:

$$dW = \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{x} = F dx \cos(180^\circ) = -F dx$$

To compute the total work $W$ required to bring charge $q_0$ all the way from infinity ($\infty$) to the observation point $P$ (distance $r$), we integrate the incremental work expression between limits $x = \infty$ to $x = r$:

$$W = \int_{\infty}^{r} dW = -\int_{\infty}^{r} F dx = -\int_{\infty}^{r} \left( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q q_0}{x^2} \right) dx$$

Factoring out constants and evaluating the integral:

$$W = -\frac{q q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int_{\infty}^{r} x^{-2} dx = -\frac{q q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_{\infty}^{r} = \frac{q q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{\infty} \right]$$

Since $\frac{1}{\infty} = 0$, the total work expression simplifies to:

$$W = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q q_0}{r}$$

By definition, electric potential $V$ is work done per unit test charge ($V = \frac{W}{q_0}$):

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r}$$

3. Nature & Physical Properties of Potential

  • Inverse Distance Dependency: Clearly, $V \propto \frac{1}{r}$, meaning potential decreases linearly with increasing distance from the charge.
  • Spherically Symmetric: The potential depends exclusively on the radial scalar distance $r$ from the source charge and is completely independent of angular direction, making it spherically symmetric.
  • Reference State: Potential at infinite distance ($r \to \infty$) is conventionally taken as zero ($V = 0$).

4. Graphical Variation: Potential ($V$) vs Field ($E$)

Notice how the electric field curve ($E$) drops off much steeper than the potential curve ($V$) because of the inverse-square law versus inverse-linear law. Both curves decay smoothly and asymptotically approach zero without ever rising back up.

Electric Potential Due to an Electric Dipole Axial, Equatorial & General Derivations

Derivation Objective: Derive mathematical expressions for the electrostatic potential produced by an electric dipole at an axial point, an equatorial point, and any general point in space, along with comparative analytical insights.

1. Potential at an Axial Point of a Short Dipole

Setup: Consider an electric dipole consisting of two point charges $-q$ (at point $A$) and $+q$ (at point $B$) separated by a small distance $2a$. Let $O$ be the center of the dipole. We wish to calculate the electrostatic potential at an observation point $P$ lying on the axis of the dipole at a distance $r$ from its center $O$.

Fig 2.4: Geometry for calculating electrostatic potential at an axial point $P$ of an electric dipole.

Distance from charge $+q$ (point $B$) to point $P$ is $BP = r - a$, and distance from charge $-q$ (point $A$) to point $P$ is $AP = r + a$. By superposition, the net potential $V$ at point $P$ is the algebraic sum of potentials due to both charges:

$$V = V_1 + V_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{+q}{r - a} + \frac{-q}{r + a} \right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{r - a} - \frac{1}{r + a} \right)$$

Taking common denominator:

$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{(r + a) - (r - a)}{(r^2 - a^2)} \right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2a}{(r^2 - a^2)} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q \cdot 2a}{r^2 - a^2}$$

Substituting dipole moment magnitude $p = q \cdot 2a$:

$$V_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2 - a^2}$$

For a short dipole ($a \ll r$), $a^2$ can be safely neglected compared to $r^2$:

$$V_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2}$$

2. Potential at an Equatorial Point of an Electric Dipole

Consider an observation point $P$ lying on the perpendicular bisector (equatorial line) of the electric dipole at distance $r$ from center $O$.

Fig 2.5: Geometry for electrostatic potential at an equatorial point.

The distance from both charges to point $P$ is equal: $AP = BP = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$. Evaluating total potential $V$:

$$V = V_1 + V_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{-q}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} + \frac{+q}{\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}} \right) = 0$$
$$V_{\text{equatorial}} = 0$$

Note: The electric potential at any point on the equatorial line is strictly zero, even though the electric field intensity at those points is non-zero.

3. Potential at Any General Point in Space

Consider any general observation point $P$ at distance $r$ from center $O$, where the position vector $\vec{r}$ makes an angle $\theta$ with the dipole moment vector $\vec{p}$.

Using spherical coordinate approximations for a short dipole ($r \gg a$), the potential expression evaluates to:

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p \cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\vec{p} \cdot \hat{r}}{r^2}$$
  • Axial Case ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$): $V = \pm \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2}$ (Maximum magnitude).
  • Equatorial Case ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $\cos(90^\circ) = 0 \implies V = 0$.

4. Contrast Between Dipole Potential and Point Charge Potential

Feature / Parameter Electric Dipole Potential Single Point Charge Potential
Angular Dependence Depends on both distance $r$ and orientation angle $\theta$ ($\propto \cos\theta$). Depends solely on scalar distance $r$ (spherically symmetric).
Distance Decay Rate Falls off much faster as inverse-square distance ($V \propto 1/r^2$). Falls off gradually as inverse-linear distance ($V \propto 1/r$).
Spatial Symmetry Cylindrically symmetric about the dipole axis. Spherically symmetric about the point charge center.

Analytical Coupling Between Electric Field and Potential

Advanced Field-Potential Dynamics: Rigorous spatial derivation establishing how electrostatic field intensity acts as the negative spatial gradient of potential, supported by integral formulations and dimensional unit harmonization.

1. Spatial Gradient Framework Setup

To uncover the direct quantitative link connecting electric field intensity ($\vec{E}$) and scalar potential ($V$), consider two closely spaced adjacent points $A$ and $B$ separated by an infinitesimal displacement vector $d\vec{r}$ inside an electric field. Let the absolute potential at point $B$ be $V$ and at point $A$ be $V + dV$.

Spatial displacement $d\vec{r}$ between adjacent points $A$ and $B$ within electric field $\vec{E}$.

When a test charge $q_0$ is transported slowly without acceleration from point $A$ to point $B$ against the ambient electric field, the external mechanical force required is:

$$\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = -q_0 \vec{E}$$

Consequently, the incremental external work executed during this microscopic displacement is:

$$dW = \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} \cdot d\vec{r} = -q_0 \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r}$$

2. Derivation of Potential Gradient

By fundamental definition, work done per unit test charge across an infinitesimal interval equals the corresponding potential difference:

$$\frac{dW}{q_0} = V_B - V_A = V - (V + dV) = -dV$$

Equating both expressions for incremental work per unit charge ($-q_0 \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} / q_0 = -dV$):

$$-\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} = -dV \implies \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} = dV$$

For one-dimensional displacement along coordinate axis $dr$, this yields the definitive differential relation:

$$E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$$

Physical Interpretation: The term $\frac{dV}{dr}$ represents the spatial rate of change of potential (known as the potential gradient). The negative sign (-) signifies that the direction of the electric field vector always points strictly toward regions of steepest potential decrease.

3. Computing Potential from Known Electric Field

Conversely, if the electric field distribution is known throughout space, we can evaluate absolute potential by integrating the differential form between two spatial points $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$:

$$\int_{V_1}^{V_2} dV = -\int_{\vec{r}_1}^{\vec{r}_2} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} \implies V_2 - V_1 = -\int_{\vec{r}_1}^{\vec{r}_2} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r}$$

By establishing reference infinity ($\infty$) as the zero potential boundary where initial position $\vec{r}_1 \to \infty$ ($V_1 = 0$), and setting final position $\vec{r}_2 = \vec{r}$, we arrive at the general integral equation:

$$V(\vec{r}) = -\int_{\infty}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r}$$

4. Unit Equivalency Verification

From the gradient relation ($E = -dV/dr$), the SI unit of electric field can be expressed as $\text{Volt per metre } (\text{Vm}^{-1})$. Simultaneously, from Coulombic force definitions ($E = F/q$), field units are $\text{Newton per Coulomb } (\text{NC}^{-1})$. We prove their exact equivalence below:

$$\frac{\text{volt}}{\text{metre}} = \frac{\text{joule} / \text{coulomb}}{\text{metre}} = \frac{(\text{newton} \cdot \text{metre}) / \text{coulomb}}{\text{metre}}$$
$$= \frac{\text{newton} \cdot \text{metre}}{\text{coulomb} \cdot \text{metre}} = \frac{\text{newton}}{\text{coulomb}} = \text{N}\cdot\text{C}^{-1}$$
$$\therefore 1 \text{ Vm}^{-1} = 1 \text{ NC}^{-1}$$

Electrostatic Potential Energy of Discrete Charge Assemblies

1. Derivation for a Two-Charge Configuration

The electrostatic potential energy associated with a localized system of discrete charges represents the total mechanical work accomplished by an external agent in assembling those charges from infinity to their respective coordinate positions.

Sequential positioning of charge $q_1$ followed by the arrival of charge $q_2$ from infinity.

Step 1: Transporting the First Charge ($q_1$)

Bringing the initial charge $q_1$ from infinity to point $P_1$ requires zero mechanical work because no pre-existing electric field exists to oppose its movement ($W_1 = 0$).

Step 2: Establishing Electric Potential Due to $q_1$

The scalar electric potential established by charge $q_1$ at a distance $r_{12}$ where point $P_2$ is located evaluates to:

$$V_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r_{12}}$$
Step 3: Transporting the Second Charge ($q_2$)

When bringing second charge $q_2$ from infinity to point $P_2$, work must be performed against the electrostatic field generated by $q_1$:

$$W_2 = \text{Potential} \times \text{Charge} = V_1 \times q_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$
Total Stored System Energy ($U$)
$$U = W_1 + W_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$

Analytical Principles & Sign Conventions

PRINCIPLE 01

Electrostatic potential energy is strictly a scalar quantity. Consequently, while computing its total value, individual charge magnitudes must always be substituted with their proper mathematical signs (+ or -).

PRINCIPLE 02

For like charges ($q_1 q_2 > 0$), the potential energy is strictly positive because repulsive forces demand external mechanical work to bring them together from infinity.

PRINCIPLE 03

For unlike charges ($q_1 q_2 < 0$), the potential energy is strictly negative because attractive forces assist their movement from infinity, requiring negative external work.

PRINCIPLE 04

Because electrostatic forces are fundamentally conservative, the stored potential energy of any spatial charge configuration is completely independent of assembly history; it depends exclusively on the final state geometry.

Behavior of Conductors & Electrostatic Shielding Framework

Comprehensive Electrostatic Equilibrium Analysis: In-depth investigation of metallic conductor properties in external fields, internal field cancellation, surface normal field distributions via Gaussian pillboxes, and Faraday cage isolation principles.

1. Fundamental Equilibrium Properties of Conductors

Property I: Internal Electric Field Cancellation

When a metallic conductor is placed inside an external electric field $\vec{E}_{\text{ext}}$, free electrons redistribute instantly to the boundaries, generating an internal induced field $\vec{E}_{\text{ind}}$ that completely cancels the external field inside the conductor. Thus, the net electrostatic field interior to any conductor is identically zero ($\vec{E} = 0$).

Property II: Surface Normal Field Orientation

Just outside the boundary surface of a charged conductor, the local electric field vector is strictly normal (perpendicular) to the surface. If any tangential component existed, it would instantly trigger surface charge motion and continuous conduction currents, violating static equilibrium conditions.

Property III: Absence of Net Interior Charge

The net electrostatic charge residing in the interior volume of any conductor is strictly zero. Applying Gauss's theorem to a Gaussian surface lying just beneath the outer boundary where $\vec{E} = 0$ confirms that any excess charge supplied must reside entirely on the outer surface.

Property IV: Equipotential Volume and Surface

Since the electric field inside a conductor is zero ($\vec{E} = -\frac{dV}{dr} = 0$), the potential gradient vanishes, establishing that potential remains completely constant throughout the interior volume and across the boundary surface of a conductor ($V = \text{constant}$).

2. Electric Field at the Surface of a Charged Conductor

To determine the precise electric field intensity at any point right outside a charged conductor of arbitrary shape, we construct a small cylindrical Gaussian surface (commonly known as a pill box) lying partly inside and partly outside the conductor.

Gaussian pillbox intersecting the boundary surface of a charged conductor.

Since the electric field inside the conductor is zero and tangential components vanish on the surface, total electric flux passes exclusively through the upper outer cross-sectional area ($\Delta S$) of the pillbox:

$$\Phi_E = E \cdot \Delta S$$

The total charge $q$ enclosed inside the pillbox of surface charge density $\sigma$ is $q = \sigma \Delta S$. Invoking Gauss's theorem ($\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$):

$$E \cdot \Delta S = \frac{\sigma \Delta S}{\varepsilon_0}$$
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} \quad \text{or} \quad \vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\hat{n}$$

3. Electrostatic Shielding & Faraday Cage Principles

Electrostatic Shielding is the phenomenon of isolating and protecting a specific interior region of space from external electric fields by enclosing it within a hollow conducting shell (cavity). Regardless of how intense external fields are or what shape the conductor takes, the electric field inside an empty interior cavity remains strictly zero ($E = 0$). This field-free shielded cavity is widely known as a Faraday cage.

When a storage unit is filled completely with $n$ distinct dielectric slabs of thicknesses $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n$ and dielectric constants $K_1, K_2, \dots, K_n$ arranged sequentially between terminals $A$ and $B$, this composite geometry is equivalent to $n$ individual capacitors connected in series.

Tandem series composite arrangement featuring $n$ dielectric slabs of thicknesses $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n$.

Step A: Generalizing Partial Insertion

Expanding the single-slab formula $C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - t + \frac{t}{K}}$ for $n$ slabs:

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d - (t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_n) + \left(\frac{t_1}{K_1} + \frac{t_2}{K_2} + \dots + \frac{t_n}{K_n}\right)}$$
Step B: Applying Total Thickness Boundary Condition

Since total plate separation equals the sum of individual slab thicknesses ($d = t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_n$):

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{\frac{t_1}{K_1} + \frac{t_2}{K_2} + \dots + \frac{t_n}{K_n}}$$

2. Side-by-Side Parallel Arrangement with $n$ Dielectric Slabs

When the space between plates is divided into $n$ parallel vertical sections possessing fractional plate areas $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ and dielectric constants $K_1, K_2, \dots, K_n$ spanning mutual separation $d$, the composite assembly functions as $n$ capacitors connected in parallel.

Side-by-side parallel arrangement featuring $n$ dielectric sections of fractional areas $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$.

Step A: Summing Individual Branch Capacities

The equivalent parallel capacity equals the sum of branch capacities ($C = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n$):

$$C = \frac{K_1 \varepsilon_0 A_1}{d} + \frac{K_2 \varepsilon_0 A_2}{d} + \dots + \frac{K_n \varepsilon_0 A_n}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{d} (K_1 A_1 + K_2 A_2 + \dots + K_n A_n)$$
Step B: Equal Area Special Case ($A_1 = A_2 = \dots = A_n = A/n$)

If total plate area $A$ is divided equally among $n$ sections:

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d \cdot n} (K_1 + K_2 + \dots + K_n)$$

Electrostatic Potential Analysis of Suspended Concentric Spherical Systems

Comprehensive Step-by-Step Analytical Framework: In-depth exploration of a small charged sphere suspended concentrically inside a larger charged spherical shell, covering self and induced potential contributions, net potential formulations, terminal potential difference, and charge transfer mechanics upon wire linkage.

1. System Architecture & Component Potentials

Consider a small inner conducting sphere of radius $r$ carrying charge $+q$ suspended concentrically via an insulating rod inside a hollow outer spherical shell of radius $R$ carrying charge $+Q$.

Concentric arrangement showing inner sphere ($r, +q$), outer shell ($R, +Q$), and insulating suspension rod.

A. Total Potential on Outer Sphere ($V_R$)

Comprises potential due to its own charge $Q$ plus potential contributed by inner charge $q$ at distance $R$:

$$V_R = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{Q}{R} + \frac{q}{R} \right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{Q + q}{R} \right)$$
B. Total Potential on Inner Sphere ($V_r$)

Comprises self-potential due to $q$ at radius $r$, plus constant potential contribution throughout the interior cavity due to outer charge $Q$:

$$V_r = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{q}{r} + \frac{Q}{R} \right)$$

2. Potential Difference & Complete Charge Transfer Dynamics

Step A: Evaluating Potential Difference ($V_r - V_R$)

Subtracting outer potential from inner potential:

$$V_r - V_R = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{q}{r} + \frac{Q}{R} \right) - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{Q}{R} + \frac{q}{R} \right)$$
$$V_r - V_R = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{R} \right)$$
Critical Physical Principle & Wire Linkage Result

Since radius $r < R$, the term $(\frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{R})$ remains strictly positive for any positive charge $q$. Consequently, the potential of the inner sphere is always higher than that of the outer sphere ($V_r > V_R$).

Wire Linkage Consequence: If the two spheres are connected by a conducting wire, charge $q$ will flow entirely to the outer sphere, regardless of whatever pre-existing charge $Q$ resides on the outer shell. (This principle applies universally to conductors of any shape).
