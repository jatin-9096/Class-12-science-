Electric Potential and Potential Difference

Electric Potential ($V$) – work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from infinity to a point in an electric field.

Mathematically, $V = \frac{W}{q}$ where $W$ is work (J) and $q$ is charge (C). SI unit: volt (V) = J·C$^{-1}$.

Potential Difference ($\Delta V$) – difference of potentials between two points: $\Delta V = V_B - V_A = \frac{W_{AB}}{q}$.

  • For a uniform electric field $E$, $\Delta V = -E d$ ( $d$ = separation along field).
  • Relation with electric field: $\vec{E} = -\nabla V$ (in 1‑D, $E = -\frac{dV}{dx}$).

Derivation of potential of a point charge

Work to bring $q$ from $\infty$ to distance $r$ from charge $Q$: $W = \int_{\infty}^{r} \frac{k Q q}{r'^2} dr' = k \frac{Q q}{r}$. Hence $V(r) = \frac{W}{q} = k \frac{Q}{r}$, where $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}$.

QuantitySymbolFormulaSI Unit
Electric Potential$V$$V = \frac{W}{q}$volt (V)
Potential Difference$\Delta V$$\Delta V = -\int \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}$volt (V)
Potential of point charge$V$$V = k \frac{Q}{r}$volt (V)

Example

Find the potential at a point 5 cm from a charge $Q = 2\,\mu\text{C}$.

Solution: $V = k \frac{Q}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\times10^{-6}}{0.05} \approx 3.6\times10^{4}\,\text{V}$.

Equipotential Surfaces

Definition: An equipotential surface is a locus of points in space that have the same electric potential, i.e., $V=\text{constant}$. No work is done in moving a test charge $q$ anywhere on this surface because $\Delta V=0$.

Key properties

  • All points are at the same potential; $\Delta V=0$ between any two points on the surface.
  • The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is always perpendicular to the surface: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}=0$ for any displacement $d\mathbf{l}$ lying on the surface.
  • Equipotential surfaces never intersect.
  • Closer surfaces indicate a stronger field magnitude $|\mathbf{E}|=|\nabla V|$.

Fundamental relation

The potential difference between two points $i$ and $f$ is given by $$\Delta V = -\int_{i}^{f}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$$ and for a radial field of a point charge $Q$, the potential at distance $r$ is $$V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{Q}{r}$$ leading \to spherical equipotentials.

SI unit: volt (V) where $1\,\text{V}=1\,\text{J\,C}^{-1}$.

Example

Find the work done \in moving a charge $q=2\,\mu\text{C}$ from $r_{A}=0.10\,\text{m}$ \to $r_{B}=0.20\,\text{m}$ \in the field of a point charge $Q=5\,\mu\text{C}$.

Potential at $r$ is $V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{Q}{r}$. Hence

StepExpression
1$\Delta V = V_{B}-V_{A}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}Q\left(\frac{1}{r_{B}}-\frac{1}{r_{A}}\right)$
2Insert values: $\Delta V = 9\times10^{9}\times5\times10^{-6}\left(\frac{1}{0.20}-\frac{1}{0.10}\right)\,\text{V}= -2.25\times10^{5}\,\text{V}$
3Work $W = q\Delta V = 2\times10^{-6}\times(-2.25\times10^{5})\,\text{J}= -0.45\,\text{J}$

The negative sign indicates that the external agent must do $0.45\,\text{J}$ of work against the attractive field.

Potential due to Point Charge and System of Charges

Definition: Electric potential $V$ at a point is the work done by external agent in bringing a unit positive test charge from infinity to that point without acceleration.

Formula for a point charge: $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}$$ where $q$ is the source charge, $r$ the distance from it and $\varepsilon_0$ the permittivity of free space. SI unit: volt (V) = J/C.

Potential due \to a system of point charges (superposition): $$V = \sum_{i}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_i}{r_i}$$

Relation with electric field: $\vec{E} = -\nabla V$; for radial fields $E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$ and the potential difference between points A and B is $\Delta V = -\int_{A}^{B}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}$.

SymbolQuantitySI Unit
$V$Electric potential$V$ (volt)
$q$Charge$C$ (coulomb)
$r$Distance from charge$m$ (metre)
$\varepsilon_0$Permittivity of free space$F\,m^{-1}$

Solved Example:

  • Two point charges $q_1=+2\,\mu C$ and $q_2=-3\,\mu C$ are placed $0.20\,m$ apart on a straight line.
  • Find the potential at point $P$ which lies $0.10\,m$ from $q_1$ on the line joining the charges.

Distances: $r_1=0.10\,m$, $r_2=0.20-0.10=0.10\,m$. Using superposition,

$$V_P = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1}+\frac{q_2}{r_2}\right)$$

Substituting $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}=9\times10^{9}\,N\,m^{2}C^{-2}$, $q_1=2\times10^{-6}\,C$, $q_2=-3\times10^{-6}\,C$, $r_1=r_2=0.10\,m$,

$$V_P = 9\times10^{9}\left(\frac{2\times10^{-6}}{0.10}+\frac{-3\times10^{-6}}{0.10}\right)$$

$$V_P = 9\times10^{9}\left(2\times10^{-5}-3\times10^{-5}\right) = 9\times10^{9}(-1\times10^{-5}) = -9\times10^{4}\,V$$

Thus the potential at $P$ is $-9\times10^{4}\,\text{V}$ (negative because the larger magnitude charge is negative).

Relation between Electric Field and Potential Gradient

Definition: The electric field $\\vec{E}$ at a point is the force experienced per unit positive test charge, while the potential gradient is the spatial rate of change of the electric potential $V$.

\n

Fundamental relation (vector form):

\n

$\\vec{E}= -\\nabla V$

\n

In one‑dimensional motion along $x$:

\n

$E_{x}= -\\frac{dV}{dx}$

\n
    \n
  • Negative sign indicates that the field points from higher to lower potential.
    • \n
  • For a uniform field, $E$ is constant and $V$ varies linearly with distance.
    • \n
\n\n\n\n\n
FormulaSI Unit
$\\vec{E}= -\\nabla V$$\\text{V m}^{-1}$
$E= -\\frac{dV}{dx}$$\\text{V m}^{-1}$
\n

Derivation (brief): Work done in moving a charge $q$ through a potential difference $dV$ is $dW = q\\,dV$. By definition $dW = \\vec{F}\\cdot d\\vec{s}= q\\vec{E}\\cdot d\\vec{s}$. Equating and dividing by $q\\,ds$ gives $\\vec{E}\\cdot \\hat{s}= -\\frac{dV}{ds}$, leading to $\\vec{E}= -\\nabla V$.

\n

Example: Two parallel plates are 4 cm apart and maintain a potential difference of 200 V. Find the magnitude and direction of $\\vec{E}$ between the plates.

\n

Solution:

\n

$d = 4\\,\\text{cm}=0.04\\,\\text{m}$

\n

$E = -\\frac{\\Delta V}{d}= -\\frac{200\\,\\text{V}}{0.04\\,\\text{m}} = -5.0\\times10^{3}\\,\\text{V m}^{-1}$.

\n

The magnitude is $5.0\\times10^{3}\\,\\text{V m}^{-1}$ and the field points from the positive plate toward the negative plate.

Capacitance: Definition and Units

Definition: Capacitance ($C$) of a conductor is the ability to store electric charge per unit potential difference. It is defined as $C = \\frac{Q}{V}$ where $Q$ is the charge on the conductor and $V$ is the potential difference between the conductor and a reference point.

SI unit: The SI unit of capacitance is the farad (F), where $1\\,\\text{F}=\\frac{\\text{Coulomb}}{\\text{Volt}}$.

  • Symbol: $C$
  • Dimension: $[M^{-1}L^{-2}T^{4}I^{2}]$
  • Common multiples: $1\\,\\mu\\text{F}=10^{-6}\\,\\text{F}$, $1\\,\\text{nF}=10^{-9}\\,\\text{F}$, $1\\,\\text{pF}=10^{-12}\\,\\text{F}$

Parallel‑plate capacitor: For two parallel plates of area $A$ separated by distance $d$ (air or vacuum between them), the capacitance is

$$C = \\varepsilon_{0}\\,\\frac{A}{d}$$

where $\\varepsilon_{0}=8.85\\times10^{-12}\\,\\text{F·m}^{-1}$ is the permittivity of free space.

QuantitySymbolSI Unit
Capacitance$C$farad (F)
Charge$Q$coulomb (C)
Potential difference$V$volt (V)

Solved example:

Find the capacitance of a parallel‑plate capacitor with plate area $A = 2\\,\\text{cm}^{2}$ and separation $d = 0.5\\,\\text{mm}$ (air gap).

Solution:

  1. Convert \to SI units: $A = 2\\times10^{-4}\\,\\text{m}^{2}$, $d = 5\\times10^{-4}\\,\\text{m}$.
  2. Use $C = \\varepsilon_{0}\\,\\frac{A}{d}$.
  3. $$C = 8.85\\times10^{-12}\\,\\frac{2\\times10^{-4}}{5\\times10^{-4}} = 8.85\\times10^{-12}\\times0.4 = 3.54\\times10^{-12}\\,\\text{F}$$
  4. Thus $C \\approx 3.5\\,\\text{pF}$.

Combination of Capacitors

Definition: A capacitor stores electric charge $Q$ at a potential difference $V$. Its capacitance $C$ is defined as $C = \frac{Q}{V}$ (SI unit: farad, F).

Combination of Capacitors

  • Series connection: The same charge $Q$ flows through each capacitor, while the total voltage is the sum of individual voltages. Equivalent capacitance $$C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{\sum \frac{1}{C_i}}$$ or for two capacitors $C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$$.
  • Parallel connection: The voltage across each capacitor is identical, and the total charge is the \sum of individual charges. Equivalent capacitance $$C_{\text{eq}} = \sum C_i$$ or for two capacitors $C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2$$.
ConnectionFormula for $C_{\text{eq}}$Voltage distributionCharge distribution
Series$\frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}}=\sum \frac{1}{C_i}$$V_{\text{total}}=\sum V_i$$Q$ same on each
Parallel$C_{\text{eq}}=\sum C_i$$V$ same on each$Q_i = C_i V$

Example: Two capacitors $C_1 = 4\,\mu\text{F}$ and $C_2 = 6\,\mu\text{F}$ are connected \in series across a $12\,\text{V}$ battery.

  1. Equivalent capacitance: $C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}=\frac{4\times6}{4+6}=\frac{24}{10}=2.4\,\mu\text{F}$.
  2. Charge on the series combination: $Q = C_{\text{eq}} V = 2.4\times10^{-6}\times12 = 28.8\times10^{-6}\,\text{C}$.
  3. Voltage across each: $V_1 = \frac{Q}{C_1}=\frac{28.8\times10^{-6}}{4\times10^{-6}}=7.2\,\text{V}$, $V_2 = 12-7.2 = 4.8\,\text{V}$.

Key points: In series, $C_{\text{eq}}$ is always less than the smallest individual capacitor; \in parallel, $C_{\text{eq}}$ is the arithmetic sum.

Energy Stored in Capacitors

Definition: Energy stored in a capacitor is the work done in moving charge from one plate to the other while charging.

Derivation:

  • At an instant the potential difference is $V = \frac{q}{C}$.
  • Incremental work $dW = V\,dq = \frac{q}{C}\,dq$.
  • Integrating from $0$ to $Q$: $W = \int_0^{Q} \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C}$.

Hence the energy $U$ can be written in three equivalent forms:

FormExpression
$U = \frac{1}{2} C V^2$useful when $C$ and $V$ are known
$U = \frac{Q^2}{2C}$useful when $Q$ and $C$ are known
$U = \frac{1}{2} Q V$general form

SI Unit: Joule (J). $[C] = \mathrm{F}$, $[V] = \mathrm{V}$.

Energy density in a uniform electric field: $u = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 E^2$ (J\,m$^{-3}$).

Example: A $10\,\mu\text{F}$ capacitor is charged to $200\,$V. Find the stored energy.

  1. Convert capacitance: $C = 10\times10^{-6}\,\text{F}$.
  2. Apply $U = \frac{1}{2} C V^2$:
  3. $U = 0.5 \times 10\times10^{-6} \times 200^2 = 0.5 \times 10\times10^{-6} \times 40000 = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2\,\text{J}$.

Thus the capacitor stores $0.2\,$J of energy.

Capacitors with Dielectrics and Spherical Capacitor

Dielectric in a Capacitor

  • Definition: A dielectric is an insulating material that, when placed between the plates of a capacitor, reduces the effective electric field.
  • Dielectric constant $k$ (or relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$) is defined as $k = \frac{C_{\text{with dielectric}}}{C_{\text{vacuum}}}$ (unitless).
  • Capacitance with dielectric: $C = k\,\epsilon_0 \frac{A}{d}$, where $A$ (m$^2$) is plate area, $d$ (m) separation, $\epsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}$ F·m$^{-1}$.
  • Maximum voltage: $V_{\max} = \frac{E_{\text{break}} d}{k}$, $E_{\text{break}}$ is dielectric strength (V·m$^{-1}$).

Spherical Capacitor

  • Two concentric spherical conductors of radii $a$ (inner) and $b$ (outer, $b>a$).
  • Capacitance in vacuum: $C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 \frac{ab}{b-a}$.
  • With dielectric filling the region $a
  • Energy stored: $U = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}$.

Capacitance$C$farad (F)
Dielectric constant$k$unitless
Permittivity of free space$\epsilon_0$F·m$^{-1}$
Charge$Q$coulomb (C)
Potential difference$V$volt (V)

Example

Find the capacitance of a spherical capacitor with $a=2\,$cm, $b=5\,$cm when the space between the spheres is filled with a dielectric of $k=2.5$.

Solution:

Using $C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 k \frac{ab}{b-a}$, convert \to metres: $a=0.02\,$m, $b=0.05\,$m.

$C = 4\pi (8.85\times10^{-12}) (2.5) \frac{0.02\times0.05}{0.05-0.02}$

$= 4\pi (8.85\times10^{-12}) (2.5) \frac{1.0\times10^{-3}}{0.03}$

$\approx 4\pi (8.85\times10^{-12}) (2.5) (3.33\times10^{-2})$

$\approx 9.2\times10^{-12}\,\text{F}$ (≈9.2 pF).
