1. Conceptual Framework

Physics · Core Law
Definition of Electric Potential and Potential Difference

Electric potential $V$ at a point in an electrostatic field is defined as the work done by an external agent in bringing a unit positive test charge $q_0=1\ \text{C}$ from infinity to that point, without any acceleration, divided by the magnitude of the test charge. Mathematically, $$V = \frac{W_{\text{ext}}}{q_0} = -\frac{1}{q_0}\int_{\infty}^{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\int_{\infty}^{\mathbf{r}} \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}.$$ The negative sign arises because the external agent must oppose the electrostatic force $\mathbf{F}=q_0\mathbf{E}$. Potential is a scalar quantity, measured in volts (V), where $1\ \text{V}=1\ \text{J/C}$.

Potential difference (or voltage) between two points $A$ and $B$ is the work done per unit charge in moving a test charge from $A$ to $B$: $$V_{AB}=V_B-V_A = -\int_{A}^{B}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}.$$ This expression shows that the line integral of the electric field between two points depends only on the end points, confirming that electrostatic fields are conservative. The concept of potential difference is central to circuit analysis, where it drives current through resistive elements according to Ohm’s law $I = V/R$.

The potential due to a point charge $Q$ at a distance $r$ is obtained by integrating the field of a point charge: $$V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}.$$ For a system of discrete charges, potentials add algebraically because they are scalars: $$V_{\text{total}}=\sum_i \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q_i}{r_i}.$$ For continuous charge distributions, the integral form is used: $$V(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}\,d\tau'.$$ Understanding these definitions provides the foundation for later topics such as capacitance, energy storage, and electrostatic shielding.

Physics · Core Law
Equipotential Surfaces and Their Properties

An equipotential surface is a locus of points in space that all have the same electric potential. Because the potential is constant on such a surface, the work done in moving a test charge anywhere along the surface is zero. Consequently, the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is always perpendicular to an equipotential surface. This orthogonality can be expressed as $$\mathbf{E}\cdot \mathbf{t}=0,$$ where $\mathbf{t}$ is any tangent vector to the surface.

Several important properties follow:

  • Perpendicularity: The field lines intersect equipotential surfaces at right angles, providing a convenient graphical method to infer field direction from a potential map.
  • Spacing and Field Strength: Where equipotential surfaces are closely spaced, the potential changes rapidly over a short distance, indicating a strong electric field. Conversely, widely spaced surfaces correspond to a weak field.
  • No Work Along Surface: Since $dV=0$ for any displacement $d\mathbf{l}$ tangent to the surface, the line integral $\int \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}=0$.
In the case of a point charge, the equipotential surfaces are concentric spheres centered at the charge. For an infinite uniformly charged plane, the equipotentials are parallel planes, reflecting the uniform field $\mathbf{E}= \sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)\,\hat{n}$.

Equipotential concepts are essential when analyzing conductors. Inside a perfect conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field is zero; therefore the entire volume of the conductor is an equipotential region. Any excess charge resides on the surface, where the field lines are perpendicular to the surface, preserving the equipotential condition inside.

The mathematical relationship between the electric field and potential is given by the gradient: $$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V.$$ This compact vector expression encapsulates both magnitude and direction, allowing one to compute $\mathbf{E}$ from a known potential distribution or vice‑versa. Mastery of equipotential surfaces thus bridges the scalar potential description with the vector field picture of electrostatics.

Physics · Core Law
Capacitance of Isolated Conductors and Capacitors

Capacitance $C$ quantifies a system’s ability to store electric charge for a given potential difference. For an isolated conductor, the capacitance is defined as $$C = \frac{Q}{V},$$ where $Q$ is the charge placed on the conductor and $V$ is the resulting potential relative to infinity. Because the geometry of the conductor determines the distribution of charge and thus the potential, capacitance depends solely on shape, size, and surrounding medium, not on the amount of charge.

For a spherical conductor of radius $R$ in vacuum, $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R.$$ This simple linear relationship shows that larger spheres possess greater capacitance. For a conducting cylinder of length $l$ and radius $r$ ($l\gg r$), the capacitance per unit length is $$\frac{C}{l}= \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(l/r)}.$$ These formulas are derived by integrating the electric field of the respective charge distributions.

A capacitor consists of two conductors (plates) separated by an insulating medium (dielectric). The capacitance of a parallel‑plate capacitor, the most common configuration in textbooks, is $$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d},$$ where $A$ is the plate area and $d$ is the separation. If a dielectric material of relative permittivity $\kappa$ fills the space, the capacitance increases by a factor $\kappa$: $$C = \kappa\frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon A}{d},$$ where $\varepsilon = \kappa\varepsilon_0$ is the absolute permittivity of the dielectric.

The electric field between the plates of an ideal parallel‑plate capacitor is uniform: $$E = \frac{V}{d},$$ and the surface charge density $\sigma = Q/A$ relates to the field by $$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\quad\text{(vacuum)}\qquad\text{or}\qquad E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\quad\text{(dielectric)}.$$ These relations are pivotal when calculating energy storage, breakdown voltage, and the effect of inserting dielectrics.

Physics · Core Law
Energy Stored in Capacitors and Electrostatic Systems

The work required to assemble a charge $Q$ on a capacitor, or equivalently to raise the potential from $0$ to $V$, is stored as electrostatic potential energy $U$. Starting from an uncharged state, the incremental work $dW$ to bring an infinitesimal charge $dq$ onto a plate at potential $V$ is $dW = V\,dq$. Since $V = q/C$, integration yields $$U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2}QV.$$ For a parallel‑plate capacitor, substituting $C = \varepsilon A/d$ gives $$U = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\frac{A}{d}V^2 = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon A d\,E^2,$$ where $E = V/d$ is the uniform field between the plates. The latter expression demonstrates that the energy can be regarded as being stored in the electric field itself, with an energy density $$u = \frac{U}{\text{volume}} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon E^2.$$ This field‑energy viewpoint is essential for understanding energy transfer in transmission lines and the operation of devices such as capacitive sensors.

When multiple capacitors are connected, the total stored energy depends on the configuration. For series connection, the equivalent capacitance is $$\frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \sum_i \frac{1}{C_i},$$ and the charge on each capacitor is the same, while the voltage divides. For parallel connection, $$C_{\text{eq}} = \sum_i C_i,$$ the voltage across each capacitor is identical, and the total charge is the sum of individual charges. The energy expressions adapt accordingly: $$U_{\text{series}} = \frac{1}{2}C_{\text{eq}}V^2,\qquad U_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{1}{2}C_{\text{eq}}V^2.$$ Notably, the same total voltage applied to a set of capacitors yields more stored energy when they are connected in parallel because $C_{\text{eq}}$ is larger.

Dielectrics not only increase capacitance but also affect energy storage. Inserting a dielectric reduces the electric field for a given charge (since $E = \sigma/\varepsilon$), thereby lowering the energy density. However, because the capacitance increases, the total energy for a fixed voltage actually rises: $$U_{\text{dielectric}} = \frac{1}{2}\kappa C_0 V^2 = \kappa U_0,$$ where $C_0$ and $U_0$ are the capacitance and energy without the dielectric. This apparent paradox resolves when recognizing that the external source does work while the dielectric is inserted, supplying the extra energy.

1. Electric Potential Due to a Point Charge

Consider a point charge $q$ located at the origin of a Cartesian coordinate system. The electric field at a field point $\mathbf{r}$ is given by Coulomb’s law:

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{q}{r^{2}}\hat{\mathbf{r}},$$ where $r=|\mathbf{r}|$ and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{r}/r$ is the radial unit vector. The electric potential $V$ is defined as the negative line integral of the electric field from a reference point (usually taken at infinity where $V=0$) to the field point: $$V(\mathbf{r})=-\int_{\infty}^{\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}.$$ Since the field is radial, we choose a straight radial path from $\infty$ to $r$, so that $d\mathbf{l}=dr\,\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Substituting the expression for $\mathbf{E}$, we get $$V(r)=-\int_{\infty}^{r}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{q}{r'^{2}}\,dr'.$$ Performing the integration yields $$V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{q}{r}.$$ Thus, the potential due to a point charge decreases inversely with distance and is positive for a positive charge and negative for a negative charge.

2. Potential Difference Between Two Points

The potential difference $\Delta V$ between two points $A$ and $B$ is defined as the work done per unit positive charge in moving a test charge from $A$ to $B$ against the electric field:

$$\Delta V=V(B)-V(A)=-\int_{A}^{B}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}.$$ For a uniform electric field $\mathbf{E}=E\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, the potential difference between two points separated by a distance $d$ along the field direction is simply $$\Delta V=-E\,d.$$ The negative sign indicates that the potential decreases in the direction of the field. In the case of a non‑uniform field, the integral must be evaluated along the chosen path, but the result is path independent because the electric field is conservative.

3. Potential Due to a Continuous Charge Distribution

For a continuous distribution of charge, the potential at a field point $\mathbf{r}$ is obtained by superposition of contributions from infinitesimal charge elements $dq$. The general expression is

$$V(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int\frac{dq}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}.$$ The integration is carried out over the entire charge distribution. Depending on the symmetry of the distribution, the integral can be simplified by choosing appropriate coordinates.

We now consider three common types of continuous distributions: line, surface, and volume charges.

3.1 Line Charge

Let a straight line segment of length $L$ carry a uniform linear charge density $\lambda$ (charge per unit length). The infinitesimal charge element is $dq=\lambda\,dl'$, where $dl'$ is an element of the line. The potential at a point $P$ located at a perpendicular distance $r$ from the line is

$$V(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\frac{\lambda\,dl'}{\sqrt{r^{2}+l'^{2}}}.$$ Performing the integration gives $$V(r)=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\ln\left(\frac{L/2+\sqrt{r^{2}+(L/2)^{2}}}{-L/2+\sqrt{r^{2}+(L/2)^{2}}}\right).$$ In the limit $L\to\infty$ (an infinitely long line), the potential diverges logarithmically, but the potential difference between two points at different distances from the line remains finite.

3.2 Surface Charge

For a surface $S$ carrying a surface charge density $\sigma$ (charge per unit area), the infinitesimal charge element is $dq=\sigma\,dS'$. The potential at a point $P$ is

$$V(P)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{S}\frac{\sigma\,dS'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}.$$ For a uniformly charged infinite plane, symmetry dictates that the potential varies linearly with distance from the plane. However, because the potential at infinity is undefined for an infinite plane, we usually consider potential differences or the electric field directly.

3.3 Volume Charge

For a volume charge density $\rho(\mathbf{r}')$ filling a region $V$, the infinitesimal charge element is $dq=\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,dV'$. The potential at a point $P$ is

$$V(P)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\int_{V}\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,dV'}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|}.$$ For spherically symmetric charge distributions, the integral simplifies dramatically. 4. Work Done in Moving a Test Charge

The work $W$ done by an external agent in moving a test charge $q_{0}$ from point $A$ to point $B$ against the electric field is

$$W=q_{0}\,\Delta V=q_{0}\left[V(B)-V(A)\right].$$ Because the electric field is conservative, $W$ depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken. This property is fundamental to the definition of electric potential.

5. Electric Potential Energy of a System of Charges

For a system of $N$ discrete charges $\{q_{i}\}$, the total electrostatic potential energy $U$ is the sum of the work required to bring each charge from infinity to its position in the presence of all other charges:

$$U=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{r_{ij}},$$ where $r_{ij}$ is the separation between charges $i$ and $j$.
