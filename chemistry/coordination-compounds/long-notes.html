1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Theoretical Principles

The foundation of modern chemistry rests upon a set of quantitative laws that describe the behavior of matter. Avogadro’s postulate, formulated in 1811, states that equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain an equal number of molecules. This leads to the concept of the mole, defined as the amount of substance that contains exactly $6.022\times10^{23}$ elementary entities. The ideal gas law, $PV=nRT$, combines Avogadro’s postulate with Boyle’s, Charles’s, and Gay-Lussac’s empirical observations. Here, $P$ is pressure, $V$ volume, $n$ the number of moles, $R$ the universal gas constant ($8.314\,\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$), and $T$ temperature in Kelvin. The law holds accurately for dilute gases but deviates for real gases at high pressures or low temperatures, where intermolecular forces become significant. A worked example: determine the volume occupied by $2.00$ mol of an ideal gas at $25\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $1.00$ atm. Converting temperature to Kelvin ($298\,\text{K}$) and using $R=0.08206\,\text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, we find $V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{2.00\times0.08206\times298}{1.00} \approx 48.9\,\text{L}$. This calculation illustrates how the ideal gas law provides a direct link between macroscopic observables and microscopic quantities. Exceptions to the law, such as the van der Waals equation, introduce correction terms for finite molecular size and attractive forces: $$\left(P+\frac{a}{V^2}\right)(V-b)=RT,$$ where $a$ and $b$ are substance-specific constants. Understanding these deviations is crucial for accurately predicting the behavior of real gases in industrial and laboratory settings.

Chemistry · Periodic Table
Periodic Trends and Their Implications

The periodic table is not merely a catalog of elements; it is a map of electronic structure that governs chemical reactivity. Atomic radius decreases across a period due to the increasing nuclear charge pulling electrons closer, while it increases down a group as additional electron shells are added. Electronegativity, a measure of an atom’s ability to attract shared electrons, follows a similar trend: it rises across a period and falls down a group. Ionization energy, the energy required to remove an electron, also increases across a period, reflecting stronger nuclear attraction, and decreases down a group where outer electrons are further from the nucleus. These trends are interrelated through the effective nuclear charge, $Z_{\text{eff}}$, which can be approximated by Slater’s rules. A practical example: compare the ionization energies of sodium (first ionization energy $5.14\,\text{eV}$) and magnesium ($7.64\,\text{eV}$). The higher value for magnesium reflects its greater $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and the fact that its outer electron resides in a more tightly bound $3s$ orbital. Exceptions to these trends arise in transition metals where d-electrons contribute to shielding, leading to irregularities in atomic radii and ionization energies. Understanding these nuances is essential for predicting oxidation states, bond types, and the behavior of complex ions in solution.

Chemistry · Bonding
Chemical Bonding: Types and Characteristics

Chemical bonds are the glue that holds atoms together, and their nature determines the properties of substances. Ionic bonds form when a metal donates electrons to a non-metal, resulting in electrostatic attraction between cations and anions. The lattice energy, which quantifies the strength of an ionic crystal, can be estimated using the Born–Landé equation: $$U = \frac{N_A M z^+ z^- e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_0}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right),$$ where $M$ is the Madelung constant, $z^+$ and $z^-$ are ionic charges, $r_0$ the interionic distance, and $n$ the Born exponent. Covalent bonds arise from the sharing of electron pairs between non-metals, and the bond order (number of shared pairs) directly influences bond length and strength. Hybridization concepts, such as $sp^3$, $sp^2$, and $sp$, explain the geometry of molecules like methane, ethylene, and acetylene, respectively. Coordinate covalent bonds, where both electrons in the bond originate from the same atom, are common in complex ions like $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^{4-}}$. Metallic bonding, characterized by a delocalized sea of electrons, accounts for the high electrical conductivity and malleability of metals. Worked example: calculate the bond dissociation energy of the $\ce{H-H}$ bond. The experimental value is $436\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, which can be derived from the difference between the enthalpy of formation of $\ce{H2}$ and two isolated hydrogen atoms. This example illustrates how bond energies are integral to thermodynamic calculations in reaction mechanisms.

Chemistry · Thermodynamics
Thermodynamic Principles in Chemical Processes

Thermodynamics provides the framework to analyze energy changes in chemical reactions. The first law, $\Delta U = Q - W$, states that the change in internal energy equals heat added to the system minus work done by the system. For processes at constant pressure, the enthalpy change, $\Delta H$, is a more convenient measure: $$\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V.$$ The second law introduces entropy, $S$, as a measure of disorder, and dictates that for spontaneous processes, $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} > 0$. The Gibbs free energy, $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, combines these concepts to predict spontaneity: $\Delta G < 0$ indicates a spontaneous reaction. Hess’s law allows the calculation of reaction enthalpies by summing the enthalpies of constituent steps. Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In CBSE Class 12 Chemistry, the ability to derive and manipulate key equations is essential for solving numerical problems and for a deeper conceptual understanding. The following derivations are presented in a step‑by‑step manner, adhering strictly to the CBSE syllabus. Each derivation is accompanied by a concise formula list that specifies the symbols, their SI units, and the conditions under which the formula holds true.

1. Rate Law for an Elementary Reaction

Consider an elementary reaction of the type

$$\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}$$

For an elementary step, the molecularity directly determines the order of reaction. The rate of disappearance of A (or B) is proportional to the product of the concentrations of the reacting species raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients.

  1. Write the differential rate expression:

    2. $$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt}=k[\text{A}]^{\alpha}[\text{B}]^{\beta}$$

  2. Since the step is elementary, the reaction orders equal the stoichiometric coefficients, i.e., $\alpha=1$ and $\beta=1$.
  3. Thus the rate law simplifies to:

    4. $$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt}=k[\text{A}][\text{B}]$$

  4. The rate constant $k$ has dimensions that ensure the rate expression has units of concentration per unit time (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹).

2. Integrated Rate Law – First Order Reaction

For a first‑order reaction, the rate law is

$$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt}=k[\text{A}]$$

Integrate between $t=0$ (when $[\text{A}]=[\text{A}]_0$) and a generic time $t$ (when $[\text{A}]=[\text{A}]_t$):

  1. Separate variables:

    2. $$\frac{d[\text{A}]}{[\text{A}]}=-k\,dt$$

  2. Integrate both sides:

    3. $$\int_{[\text{A}]_0}^{[\text{A}]_t}\frac{d[\text{A}]}{[\text{A}]}=-k\int_{0}^{t}dt$$

  3. Resulting in:

    4. $$\ln\frac{[\text{A}]_t}{[\text{A}]_0}=-kt$$

  4. Rearrange to the commonly used form:

    5. $$\ln[\text{A}]_t=\ln[\text{A}]_0-kt$$

  5. Or in base‑10 logarithm:

    6. $$\log[\text{A}]_t=\log[\text{A}]_0-\frac{k}{2.303}t$$

3. Integrated Rate Law – Second Order Reaction (Two‑Reactant Case)

For a second‑order reaction involving two different reactants:

$$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt}=k[\text{A}][\text{B}]$$

Assuming the initial concentrations are $[\text{A}]_0$ and $[\text{B}]_0$, and that the stoichiometry is 1:1, we have $[\text{B}]=[\text{B}]_0-([\text{A}]_0-[\text{A}])$.

  1. Express $[\text{B}]$ in terms of $[\text{A}]$:

    2. $$[\text{B}]=[\text{B}]_0-[\text{A}]_0+[\text{A}]$$

  2. Substitute into the rate law and separate variables:

    3. $$\frac{d[\text{A}]}{([\text{A}]_0-[\text{A}])+[\text{B}]_0}= -k\,dt$$

  3. Integrate from $t=0$ to $t$ and $[\text{A}]_0$ to $[\text{A}]_t$:

    4. $$\frac{1}{[\text{B}]_0-[\text{A}]_0}\ln\frac{[\text{A}]([\text{B}]_0-[\text{A}]_0)}{[\text{A}]_0([\text{B}]_0-[\text{A}])}=kt$$

  4. If $[\text{A}]_0=[\text{B}]_0$, the expression simplifies to the classic second‑order form:

    5. $$\frac{1}{[\text{A}]_t}-\frac{1}{[\text{A}]_0}=kt$$

4. Derivation of the Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation relates the cell potential to the activities (or concentrations) of the reacting species. Starting from the fundamental thermodynamic relation:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + RT\ln Q$$

where $Q$ is the reaction quotient. For an electrochemical cell, the Gibbs free energy change is also linked to the cell potential $E$ by:

$$\Delta G = -nFE$$

and similarly for the standard state:

$$\Delta G^{\circ} = -nFE^{\circ}$$

Equating the two expressions for $\Delta G$:

$$-nFE = -nFE^{\circ} + RT\ln Q$$

Rearrange to isolate $E$:

$$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

At $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ (298 K), the factor $\frac{RT}{F}=0.025693\ \text{V}$, and converting natural log to base‑10 log ($\ln x = 2.303\log x$) gives the familiar form:

$$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{0.05916\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q$$

This is the Nernst equation used throughout the CBSE syllabus.

5. Gibbs Free Energy and Equilibrium Constant

Starting again from $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + RT\ln Q$, at equilibrium $Q=K$ and $\Delta G=0$:

$$0 = \Delta G^{\circ} + RT\ln K$$

Thus, the standard Gibbs free energy change is related to the equilibrium constant:

$$\Delta G^{\circ} = -RT\ln K$$

In base‑10 logarithm form:

$$\Delta G^{\circ} = -2.303RT\log K$$

This relationship is vital for calculating $K$ from thermodynamic data and vice‑versa.

6. Van’t Hoff Equation

Differentiate $\Delta G^{\circ} = -RT\ln K$ with respect to temperature while keeping pressure constant:

$$\frac{d(\Delta G^{\circ})}{dT} = -R\ln K - \frac{RT}{K}\frac{dK}{dT}$$

But $\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial \Delta G^{\circ}}{\partial T}\right)_p = -\Delta S^{\circ}$, and $\Delta G^{\circ}= \Delta H^{\circ} - T\Delta S^{\circ}$. Substituting and simplifying yields:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^{\circ}}{RT^{2}}$$

Integrating between two temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$:

$$\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1}= -\frac{\Delta H^{\circ}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

This is the Van’t Hoff equation, which predicts how $K$ varies with temperature.

7. Clapeyron Equation for Phase Equilibria

Consider the coexistence of two phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$. The equality of chemical potentials gives:

$$\mu_{\alpha}= \mu_{\beta}$$

Taking the differential at constant temperature and pressure:

$$d\mu_{\alpha}=d\mu_{\beta}$$

Using the fundamental relation $d\mu = -S_m dT + V_m dP$, we obtain:

$$-S_{m,\alpha}dT + V_{m,\alpha}dP = -S_{m,\beta}dT + V_{m,\beta}dP$$

Rearranging yields the Clapeyron equation:

$$\frac{dP}{dT}= \frac{S_{m,\beta}-S_{m,\alpha}}{V_{m,\beta}-V_{m,\alpha}} = \frac{\Delta S_{tr}}{\Delta V_{tr}}$$

For a liquid–vapor transition where $\Delta V_{tr}\approx V_{m,\text{vapor}}$, the equation simplifies to the Clausius–Clapeyron form:

$$\ln P = -\frac{\Delta H_{vap}}{RT}+C$$

where $C$ is an integration constant.
