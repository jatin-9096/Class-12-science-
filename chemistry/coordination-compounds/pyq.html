Case Study 1 – Ammonia Synthesis and Its Environmental Impact

Passage: Ammonia ($\mathrm{NH_3}$) is a cornerstone of the modern chemical industry, primarily as a feedstock for nitrogenous fertilizers. The industrial production of ammonia is carried out by the Haber‑Bosch process, which combines nitrogen gas ($\mathrm{N_2}$) from the air with hydrogen gas ($\mathrm{H_2}$) derived mainly from natural gas reforming. The overall balanced reaction is:

$$\mathrm{N_2(g) + 3\,H_2(g) \;\rightleftharpoons\; 2\,NH_3(g)}$$

The reaction is exothermic ($\Delta H^\circ = -92.4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) and proceeds with a decrease in the number of gas moles, favouring higher pressures. Commercial plants operate at $150–250\ \text{atm}$ and $400–500^\circ\text{C}$, employing an iron‑based catalyst to accelerate the rate. Despite the high pressure and temperature, the equilibrium conversion per pass is only about $15–20\%$, necessitating recycling of unreacted gases.

From an environmental perspective, the Haber‑Bosch process is energy‑intensive, accounting for roughly $1–2\%$ of global fossil‑fuel consumption. Moreover, the widespread use of ammonia‑based fertilizers leads to nitrogen leaching, eutrophication of water bodies, and emission of nitrous oxide ($\mathrm{N_2O}$), a potent greenhouse gas.

Practice Questions

  1. Practice Question 1: Calculate the standard Gibbs free energy change ($\Delta G^\circ$) for the synthesis of ammonia at $298\ \text{K}$ using the standard formation data: $\Delta G_f^\circ(\mathrm{NH_3}) = -16.45\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, $\Delta G_f^\circ(\mathrm{N_2}) = 0$, $\Delta G_f^\circ(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0$.
  2. Practice Question 2: Determine the equilibrium constant $K_p$ for the reaction at $500^\circ\text{C}$ given that $\Delta H^\circ = -92.4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ and $\Delta S^\circ = -198.5\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$.
  3. Practice Question 3: If a plant operates at $200\ \text{atm}$ and $500^\circ\text{C}$, estimate the equilibrium conversion of $\mathrm{N_2}$ per pass assuming ideal gas behaviour.
  4. Practice Question 4: Discuss two strategies to reduce the carbon footprint of ammonia production without compromising the required production volume.
  5. Practice Question 5: Explain how the leaching of nitrate ions from fertilizers can lead to eutrophication, and suggest one agronomic practice to mitigate this problem.

Solutions

  1. Solution to Practice Question 1: $\displaystyle \Delta G^\circ = \sum \nu_i \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \nu_i \Delta G_f^\circ(\text{reactants})$ $= 2(-16.45) - [0 + 3(0)]\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1} = -32.90\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}.$ The negative value confirms that the formation of ammonia is spontaneous under standard conditions.
  2. Solution to Practice Question 2: $\displaystyle \Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$ At $T = 500^\circ\text{C} = 773\ \text{K}$, $\displaystyle \Delta G^\circ = (-92.4\times10^{3}) - 773(-198.5) = -92.4\times10^{3} + 153.5\times10^{3} = 61.1\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}.$ Then, $K_p = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{61.1\times10^{3}}{8.314\times773}\right) \approx 2.1\times10^{-4}.$
  3. Solution to Practice Question 3: For the reaction $\mathrm{N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3}$, let the initial partial pressures be $p_{\mathrm{N_2}}^0 = p_{\mathrm{H_2}}^0 = 200\ \text{atm}$ (assuming a stoichiometric feed). Let $\alpha$ be the fraction of $\mathrm{N_2}$ that reacts. At equilibrium: $p_{\mathrm{N_2}} = 200(1-\alpha)$, $p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 200(1-3\alpha)$, $p_{\mathrm{NH_3}} = 200(2\alpha)$. Substituting into $K_p = \dfrac{p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}^{\,2}}{p_{\mathrm{N_2}}\,p_{\mathrm{H_2}}^{\,3}}$ and using $K_p = 2.1\times10^{-4}$ gives a cubic equation in $\alpha$. Solving (by iteration) yields $\alpha \approx 0.12$, i.e., about $12\%$ conversion per pass.
  4. Solution to Practice Question 4:
    • Electro‑chemical synthesis (e‑Haber process): Using renewable electricity to electrolyze water for $\mathrm{H_2}$ eliminates the need for natural‑gas reforming, cutting CO₂ emissions dramatically.
    • Carbon capture and utilization (CCU): Integrating CO₂ capture from the reformer flue gas and converting it to value‑added chemicals (e.g., methanol) reduces the net carbon release associated with hydrogen production.
    Both approaches maintain the required $\mathrm{NH_3}$ output while decoupling the process from fossil‑fuel combustion.
  5. Solution to Practice Question 5: Nitrate ($\mathrm{NO_3^-}$) is highly soluble; when excess fertilizer leaches into water bodies, it promotes rapid algal growth (eutrophication). Decomposition of algal blooms depletes dissolved oxygen, harming aquatic life. A practical mitigation is controlled‑release fertilizer, which releases nitrogen slowly, matching plant uptake and reducing leaching. Additionally, adopting the “4R” principle (Right source, Right rate, Right time, Right place) optimises nitrogen use efficiency.

Case Study 2 – Electrochemical Production of Aluminium and Energy Considerations

Passage: Aluminium is extracted from bauxite ore through the Bayer process (to obtain alumina, $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$) followed by the Hall‑Héroult electrolytic reduction. The overall cell reaction is:

$$\mathrm{2\,Al_2O_3(l) + 3\,C(s) \;\longrightarrow\; 4\,Al(l) + 3\,CO_2(g)}$$

In practice, alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite ($\mathrm{Na_3AlF_6}$) at $950^\circ\text{C}$, and a direct current passes between carbon anodes and a steel cathode. The half‑reactions are:

$$\text{Cathode:}\quad \mathrm{Al^{3+} + 3e^- \;\rightarrow\; Al(l)}$$

$$\text{Anode:}\quad \mathrm{C + 2O^{2-} \;\rightarrow\; CO_2 + 4e^-}$$

The cell voltage is typically $4.0–4.5\ \text{V}$, and the theoretical minimum energy requirement (based on $\Delta G^\circ = 1775\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ reduction) is about $13\ \text{kWh kg}^{-1}$ of aluminium. However, actual plants consume $15–20\ \text{kWh kg}^{-1}$ due to over‑potentials, heat losses, and resistance.

Aluminium production is therefore one of the most energy‑intensive processes in the metallurgical sector, accounting for roughly $0.5\%$ of global electricity consumption. The carbon emissions from the anode reaction (CO₂) and the electricity source are major environmental concerns. Recent research focuses on inert anodes (e.g., SnO₂‑based) to eliminate CO₂ generation and on renewable‑energy‑driven smelters.

  1. Practice Question 1: Using the standard Gibbs free energy change $\Delta G^\circ = 1775\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for the reduction of one mole of $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$, calculate the theoretical minimum specific energy (in kWh per kilogram of Al) required for aluminium production.
  2. Practice Question 2: If a smelter operates at a cell voltage of $4.3\ \text{V}$ and a current efficiency of $90\%$, determine the actual specific energy consumption (kWh kg⁻¹) assuming a current of $100\ \text{kA}$ and a production rate of $100\ \text{t day}^{-1}$.
  3. Practice Question 3: Derive the expression for the amount of CO₂ emitted per kilogram of aluminium produced when using a conventional carbon anode.
  4. Practice Question 4: Discuss two advantages and two challenges associated with replacing carbon anodes by inert anodes in the Hall‑Héroult process.
