1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Stoichiometry

In the study of chemical reactions, the most basic concept is that of a chemical equation, which represents the transformation of reactants into products. A typical balanced equation is written as $aA + bB \rightarrow cC + dD$, where the coefficients $a, b, c,$ and $d$ are the stoichiometric coefficients that ensure the conservation of mass. The Law of Conservation of Mass states that the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products, which is mathematically expressed as $\sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}}$. This principle underpins the practice of stoichiometry, allowing chemists to predict the amounts of substances involved in a reaction. The concept of moles, defined as $1\,\text{mol} = 6.022\times10^{23}$ entities, provides a bridge between the microscopic and macroscopic worlds. By converting grams to moles using the molar mass, one can calculate the number of moles of each species in a reaction. The mole ratio derived from the balanced equation is then used to determine limiting reactants, theoretical yields, and percent yields. An intuitive way to remember the mole concept is to think of it as a “chemical unit” that counts particles in a way analogous to how a dozen counts eggs. Exceptions to the straightforward application of stoichiometry arise in redox reactions involving electron transfer, where the number of electrons transferred must be considered, and in reactions that proceed through multiple steps or involve intermediates. A worked example: In the reaction $2H_2 + O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O$, if 4.0 g of $H_2$ reacts with excess $O_2$, the number of moles of $H_2$ is $4.0\,\text{g} / 2.02\,\text{g/mol} = 1.98\,\text{mol}$. Using the mole ratio $2:2$, the theoretical yield of $H_2O$ is also 1.98 mol, which corresponds to $1.98\,\text{mol} \times 18.02\,\text{g/mol} = 35.7\,\text{g}$ of water.

Thermodynamics · Core Law
Laws of Thermodynamics and Energy Transformations

The first law of thermodynamics, also known as the Law of Energy Conservation, states that the change in internal energy of a closed system is equal to the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system: $$\Delta U = Q - W.$$ In differential form, this is expressed as $$dU = \delta Q - \delta W,$$ where $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ are infinitesimal heat and work, respectively. The second law introduces the concept of entropy, asserting that for any spontaneous process, the total entropy change of the universe is positive: $$\Delta S_{\text{univ}} > 0.$$ This leads to the definition of Gibbs free energy, $$G = H - TS,$$ and its differential form $$dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP.$$ A spontaneous process at constant temperature and pressure occurs when $\Delta G < 0$. The third law of thermodynamics states that as the temperature approaches absolute zero, the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero. These laws provide the framework for understanding chemical energetics. Intuitively, the first law ensures that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed, while the second law explains why processes have a preferred direction. Exceptions arise in non-equilibrium thermodynamics, where local decreases in entropy can occur at the expense of larger increases elsewhere. A worked example involves the combustion of methane: $$CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O.$$ The standard enthalpy change $\Delta H^\circ$ is $-890\,\text{kJ/mol}$, indicating an exothermic reaction. Using the standard Gibbs free energy change $\Delta G^\circ = -500\,\text{kJ/mol}$, we confirm the spontaneity of the reaction at standard conditions.

Kinetics · Core Law
Rate Laws, Reaction Orders, and Activation Energy

Chemical kinetics studies the speed at which reactions occur and the factors that influence this rate. The rate of a reaction involving species $A$ and $B$ is generally expressed as $$\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n,$$ where $k$ is the rate constant, and $m$ and $n$ are the reaction orders with respect to $A$ and $B$, respectively. The overall reaction order is the sum $m+n$. Experimental determination of these orders requires monitoring concentration changes over time, often using techniques such as spectrophotometry or chromatography. The Arrhenius equation relates the rate constant to temperature: $$k = A\,e^{-E_a/(RT)},$$ where $A$ is the pre-exponential factor, $E_a$ is the activation energy, $R$ is the gas constant, and $T$ is the absolute temperature. Plotting $\ln k$ versus $1/T$ yields a straight line with slope $-E_a/R$, allowing determination of $E_a$. Intuitively, a higher activation energy corresponds to a slower reaction because fewer molecules possess sufficient energy to overcome the energy barrier. Exceptions occur in reactions that are diffusion-controlled or involve quantum tunneling, where the Arrhenius form may not hold. A worked example: For the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, $2H_2O_2 \rightarrow 2H_2O + O_2$, the rate law is found experimentally to be $$\text{rate} = k[H_2O_2]^2.$$ If the rate constant at 298 K is $1.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$, then the half-life $t_{1/2}$ is given by $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / k \approx 693\,\text{s}$. This example illustrates how the second-order dependence on $H_2O_2$ leads to a concentration-dependent half-life.

Equilibrium · Core Law
Chemical Equilibrium, Le Chatelier's Principle, and Equilibrium Constants

A chemical system at equilibrium has no net change in the concentrations of reactants and products over time. For a general reaction $$aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD,$$ the equilibrium constant in terms of concentrations is $$K_c = \frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}.$$ The value of $K_c$ indicates the position of equilibrium: if $K_c \gg 1$, products dominate; if $K_c \ll 1$, reactants dominate. Le Chatelier's principle predicts how the system responds to changes in concentration, temperature, or pressure. For example, increasing the concentration of $A$ shifts the equilibrium toward the products to reduce the disturbance. Temperature changes affect the equilibrium position depending on whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic, as described by the van ’t Hoff equation: $$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT^2}.$$ A worked example involves the synthesis of ammonia: $$N_2(g) + 3H_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3(g).$$ At 400 K and 1 atm, the equilibrium constant $K_c$ is $1.5\times10^{-5}$. If the system initially contains 1.0 mol of $N_2$ and 3.0 mol of $H_2$ in a 1.0 L vessel, the equilibrium concentrations can be calculated by setting up an ICE table and solving for the extent of reaction $\xi$. This example demonstrates how equilibrium constants and Le Chatelier's principle guide the prediction of product yields in industrial processes such as the Haber process.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In the CBSE Class 12 Chemistry syllabus, a solid grasp of the underlying derivations is essential for scoring full marks in the board examination. The following derivations are presented in a step‑by‑step manner, adhering strictly to the CBSE prescribed methodology. Each derivation is accompanied by a concise formula table that lists the symbols, their SI units, and the specific conditions under which the equations are valid.

1. Derivation of the Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation links the cell potential ($E$) of an electrochemical cell to the activities (or concentrations) of the reactants and products. The derivation starts from the fundamental relationship between Gibbs free energy change ($\Delta G$) and cell potential:

$$\Delta G = -nFE$$

where $n$ is the number of moles of electrons transferred and $F$ is the Faraday constant ($96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$). At equilibrium, $\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$, where $Q$ is the reaction quotient, $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$) and $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin.

Equating the two expressions for $\Delta G$ gives:

$$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Rearranging, we obtain the Nernst equation in its most general form:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

For reactions involving aqueous solutions, it is customary to replace the natural logarithm with base‑10 logarithm using $\ln x = 2.303\log_{10}x$:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} Q$$

At $25^\circ\text{C}$ (298 K), the term $\frac{2.303RT}{F}$ simplifies to $0.0592\ \text{V}$, yielding the widely used expression:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log_{10} Q$$

Key points to remember for the CBSE exam:

  • Use $E^\circ$ for the standard cell potential measured under standard conditions (1 M, 1 atm, 25 °C).
  • Identify $n$ correctly from the balanced redox equation.
  • Replace $Q$ with the appropriate concentration or pressure terms, remembering that activities are approximated by concentrations for dilute solutions.

2. Derivation of the van’t Hoff Equation

The van’t Hoff equation describes how the equilibrium constant ($K$) varies with temperature. Starting from the fundamental thermodynamic identity:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$$

and the temperature dependence of standard Gibbs free energy:

$$\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$$

Equating the two expressions for $\Delta G^\circ$ gives:

$$-RT\ln K = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$$

Rearranging, we obtain:

$$\ln K = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT} + \frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R}$$

To derive the differential form, differentiate both sides with respect to temperature ($T$) while keeping pressure constant:

$$\frac{d(\ln K)}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT^{2}}$$

Multiplying both sides by $R$ yields the classic van’t Hoff equation:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT^{2}}$$

Integration between two temperatures $T_{1}$ and $T_{2}$ gives the integrated form:

$$\ln\frac{K_{2}}{K_{1}} = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_{2}}-\frac{1}{T_{1}}\right)$$

For endothermic reactions ($\Delta H^\circ>0$), $K$ increases with temperature; for exothermic reactions ($\Delta H^\circ<0$), $K$ decreases with temperature. This trend is frequently tested in CBSE practical and theory questions.

3. Derivation of the Rate Law from Collision Theory

Collision theory provides a molecular‑level explanation for the observed rate law of elementary reactions. Consider a simple bimolecular elementary step:

$$\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}$$

The rate of reaction ($r$) is proportional to the number of effective collisions per unit time. The number of collisions ($Z_{AB}$) between molecules of A and B in a unit volume can be expressed as:

$$Z_{AB} = N_{A}N_{B}\sigma_{AB}\bar{v}_{AB}$$

where $N_{A}$ and $N_{B}$ are the number densities (molecules cm$^{-3}$) of A and B, $\sigma_{AB}$ is the collision cross‑section, and $\bar{v}_{AB}$ is the average relative speed.

Only a fraction $f$ of these collisions possess energy equal to or greater than the activation energy ($E_{a}$) and proper orientation. According to the Maxwell‑Boltzmann distribution, this fraction is given by the Arrhenius factor $e^{-E_{a}/RT}$. Hence, the effective collision frequency becomes:

$$Z_{AB}^{\text{eff}} = N_{A}N_{B}\sigma_{AB}\bar{v}_{AB}e^{-E_{a}/RT}$$

Since concentration $[X] = N_{X}/N_{Avo}$ (where $N_{Avo}$ is Avogadro’s number), we can rewrite the expression in terms of molar concentrations:

$$r = k[A][B]$$

where the rate constant $k$ encapsulates all temperature‑dependent terms:

$$k = A e^{-E_{a}/RT}$$

The pre‑exponential factor $A$ (also called the frequency factor) contains $\sigma_{AB}\bar{v}_{AB}$ and a steric factor $p$ that accounts for the orientation requirement. This derivation justifies the observed second‑order rate law for many elementary bimolecular reactions and explains the exponential temperature dependence captured by the Arrhenius equation.

4. Derivation of the Equilibrium Constant Expression from Gibbs Free Energy

Starting again from the fundamental relation $\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$, at equilibrium $\Delta G = 0$ and $Q = K$ (the equilibrium constant). Setting $\Delta G = 0$ gives:

$$0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln K$$

Rearranging yields the well‑known relationship:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$$

To express $K$ in terms of concentrations or partial pressures, consider a generic reversible reaction:

$$a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightleftharpoons c\text{C} + d\text{D}$$

The reaction quotient $Q$ is defined as:

$$Q = \frac{a_{\text{C}}^{c}\,a_{\text{D}}^{d}}{a_{\text{A}}^{a}\,a_{\text{B}}^{b}}$$

where $a_{i}$ denotes the activity of species $i$. For ideal gases, activity is approximated by partial pressure $p_{i}/p^{\circ}$; for solutes in dilute solution, activity is approximated by concentration $[i]/c^{\circ}$. Substituting these approximations into the expression for $K$ yields the familiar equilibrium constant forms:

  • Thermodynamic equilibrium constant ($K_{p}$): $K_{p}= \dfrac{(p_{\text{C}}/p^{\circ})^{c}(p_{\text{D}}/p^{\circ})^{d}}{(p_{\text{A}}/p^{\circ})^{a}(p_{\text{B}}/p^{\circ})^{b}}$
  • Concentration equilibrium constant ($K_{c}$): $K_{c}= \dfrac{([C]/c^{\circ})^{c}([D]/c^{\circ})^{d}}{([A]/c^{\circ})^{a}([B]/c^{\circ})^{b}}$

For reactions occurring in the gas phase, the relationship between $K_{p}$ and $K_{c}$ is given by the ideal gas law:

$$K_{p}=K_{c}(RT)^{\Delta n}$$

where $\Delta n = (c+d)-(a+b)$ is the change in the number of moles of gas.

5. Derivation of the Ionic Product of Water ($K_{w}$)

Water auto‑ionisation is represented by the equilibrium:

$$\text{H}_{2}\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^{+}(aq) + \text{OH}^{-}(aq)$$

The standard Gibbs free energy change for this process at $25^\circ\text{C}$ is $\Delta G^\circ = 79.9\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$. Using $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$, we obtain:

$$K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT}$$

Substituting the numerical values ($R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $T = 298\ \text{K}$) gives:

$$K = e^{-79\,900/(8.314\times298)} \approx 1.0\times10^{-14}$$

Since the activity of pure liquid water is taken as unity, the equilibrium constant reduces to the product of the concentrations of the ions:

$$K_{w} = [\text{H}^{+}][\text{OH}^{-}] = 1.0\times10^{-14}\ \text{(mol}^{2}\text{ L}^{-2})$$
