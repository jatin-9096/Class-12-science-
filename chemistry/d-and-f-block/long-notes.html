Position and general electronic configuration of d‑block elements

Definition: d‑block elements (transition elements) are the elements whose outermost electrons enter the $(n-1)d$ subshell. They occupy groups 3 to 12 of periods 4 to 7.

General electronic configuration

  • Usual pattern: $ (n-1)d^{1-10} ns^{2}$
  • Exceptions: Cr – $[Ar] 3d^{5} 4s^{1}$, Cu – $[Ar] 3d^{10} 4s^{1}$

Key points

  • Number of d‑orbitals = $5$, each holds $2$ electrons → maximum $10$ electrons in $(n-1)d$.
  • Typical oxidation states: $+2$, $+3$, and higher up to $+7$ (e.g., Mn).
  • Atomic radius is expressed in $\pm$ (picometre).

Physical & chemical trends

  • Gradual decrease in atomic radius across a period due to increasing nuclear charge.
  • First ionisation energy shows irregular pattern because removal of a $ns$ electron is easier than a $(n-1)d$ electron.
  • Many d‑block ions are coloured because of $d-d$ electronic transitions; the wavelength $\lambda$ is related to energy gap $\Delta E$ by $\Delta E = hc/\lambda$.
  • Magnetic behaviour depends on unpaired electrons; spin‑only magnetic moment $\mu_{so}=\sqrt{n(n+2)}\,\mu_{B}$ where $n$ = number of unpaired electrons.

SI units (relevant)

  • Atomic radius – $\pm$ (1 pm = $10^{-12}$ m).
  • Ionisation energy – $kJ\,mol^{-1}$.
  • Electronegativity – dimensionless (Pauling scale).
  • Magnetic moment – Bohr magneton ($\mu_{B}=9.274\times10^{-24}$ J T$^{-1}$).
PeriodGroupBlockGeneral config.

Solved example

Find the element having the configuration $[Ar] 3d^{5} 4s^{2}$ and write its oxidation states.

  1. Remove the noble‑gas core $[Ar]$ → remaining electrons $3d^{5} 4s^{2}$.
  2. Sum electrons: $5+2=7$, which corresponds to atomic number $Z=25$.
  3. $Z=25$ is Manganese (Mn).
  4. Typical oxidation states of Mn: $+2$, $+3$, $+4$, $+6$, $+7$.

Anomalous properties and periodic trends (atomic/ionic radii, ionisation enthalpy, electron affinity, electronegativity, metallic character) of d‑block elements

Anomalous properties of d‑block elements

  • Unlike s‑block, d‑block shows irregular variation in atomic/ionic radii, ionisation enthalpy (IE), electron affinity (EA) and electronegativity (EN) across a period.
  • Cause: progressive filling of (n‑1)d orbitals, poor shielding (low screening constant $S$) and variable effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$.

Periodic trends (general)

PropertyTrend across periodTrend down group
Atomic radiusGradual decrease; slight increase at start of d‑block due to added electron in (n‑1)dIncrease (addition of shells)
Ionic radiusDecrease for higher oxidation states; irregular for same chargeIncrease
Ionisation enthalpyOverall increase; dip at start of d‑block (e.g., Sc) because electron removed from (n‑1)dDecrease
Electron affinityIrregular; less exothermic for d‑block due to electron‑electron repulsion in d‑orbitalsBecomes less negative
ElectronegativityIncrease with $Z_{\text{eff}}$; anomalies at groups 3‑5Decrease

Key formula (Mulliken electronegativity)

\[\chi = \frac{I + A}{2}\]

where $I$ = first ionisation enthalpy (kJ·mol$^{-1}$), $A$ = electron affinity (kJ·mol$^{-1}$). SI unit of $\chi$ is dimensionless.

Solved example

Calculate Mulliken EN of Fe (first IE = $762\,\text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$, EA = $-15\,\text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$).

Solution:

\[\chi_{\text{Fe}} = \frac{762 + (-15)}{2} = \frac{747}{2} = 373.5\]

Thus $\chi_{\text{Fe}} \approx 3.74$ on the Pauling scale (after normalising). The relatively low EN reflects the anomalous shielding of (n‑1)d electrons.

Variable oxidation states and colour of compounds of transition metals

Variable oxidation states – Transition metals can exhibit several oxidation states because the energy difference between $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals is small. The most stable oxidation state is usually the one with a half‑filled or completely filled $d$ subshell.

  • General rule: maximum oxidation state = group number; minimum = 0 or +2 for most first‑row TM.
  • Example: Mn shows $+2$, $+4$, $+7$ etc.

Colour of transition‑metal compounds – Originates from d‑d electronic transitions within the split $d$‑orbitals in a ligand field.

TermExpression
Crystal‑field splitting energy$\Delta_0$
Wavelength of absorbed light$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta_0}$
Energy of photon$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$

Where $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\,\text{J·s}$, $c = 3.00\times10^8\,\text{m·s}^{-1}$, $\Delta_0$ is usually expressed in $\text{cm}^{-1}$; conversion $\Delta_0(\text{J}) = hc\times\Delta_0(\text{cm}^{-1})$.

Solved example

For $\text{[Ti(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}$, $\Delta_0 = 2.0\times10^4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$. Find the wavelength of light absorbed and the colour observed.

  1. Convert $\Delta_0$ to joules: $\Delta_0 = hc\times2.0\times10^4 = (6.626\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)(2.0\times10^4) = 3.98\times10^{-20}\,\text{J}$.
  2. $\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta_0}=\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}\times3.00\times10^8}{3.98\times10^{-20}}=5.0\times10^{-7}\,\text{m}=500\,\text{nm}$ (blue‑green region).
  3. The complex absorbs blue‑green light; the complementary colour observed is reddish‑orange.

Key points to remember for exams:

  • Variable oxidation states arise from comparable $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ ionisation energies.
  • Colour depends on $\Delta_0$, which increases with higher oxidation state, stronger field ligands (spectrochemical series), and smaller metal‑ligand distance.
  • Use $\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta_0}$ to link absorption wavelength with colour.

Important compounds and industrial applications of selected d‑block elements

Important compounds & industrial applications of selected d‑block elements

  • Copper (Cu): CuSO4·5H2O – used in electro‑plating and wiring.
  • Iron (Fe): Fe2O3 – raw material for steel (Fe‑C alloy).
  • Zinc (Zn): ZnO – white pigment, galvanisation.
  • Nickel (Ni): NiCl2 – stainless‑steel, batteries.
  • Chromium (Cr): Cr2O3 – leather tanning, pigments.
  • Manganese (Mn): KMnO4 – oxidising agent, dry cell.
  • Molybdenum (Mo): MoS2 – solid lubricant.
  • Titanium (Ti): TiCl4 – aerospace alloys.

Key formula (percentage yield):

$\displaystyle \text{Yield \%} = \frac{\text{mass obtained (g)}}{\text{theoretical mass (g)}}\times 100$

SI unit of mass = $\text{kg}$, concentration = $\text{mol\,L^{-1}}$, pressure = $\text{Pa}$.

ElementImportant compoundMajor use
CuCuSO4·5H2OElectro‑plating, wiring
FeFe2O3Steel making
ZnZnOGalvanisation, pigment
NiNiCl2Stainless steel, batteries

Solved example

Electrolyse $10\ \text{g}$ of CuSO4·5H2O. Molar mass of CuSO4·5H2O = $249.68\ \text{g\,mol^{-1}}$. Moles of CuSO4·5H2O = $\frac{10}{249.68}=0.0401\ \text{mol}$. Each mole yields $1\ \text{mol}$ Cu ($63.55\ \text{g\,mol^{-1}}$). Theoretical Cu mass = $0.0401\times63.55=2.55\ \text{g}$. If $2.30\ \text{g}$ Cu is collected, % yield = $\frac{2.30}{2.55}\times100=90.2\%$.

Position and general electronic configuration of f‑block elements (lanthanides and actinides)

Definition: The f‑block comprises the two rows of elements in which the differentiating electron enters the $4f$ (lanthanides) or $5f$ (actinides) subshell.

Position in the periodic table

  • Located below the main body, separated by a gap.
  • Lanthanides: $Z=57$ to $71$ (Ce to Lu).
  • Actinides: $Z=89$ to $103$ (Ac to Lr).

General electronic configuration

  • Lanthanides: $[\text{Xe}]\,4f^{1-14}\,6s^{2}$ (the $5d$ subshell is empty or contains at most one electron).
  • Actinides: $[\text{Rn}]\,5f^{1-14}\,6d^{0-1}\,7s^{2}$.

For any f‑block element, the number of f‑electrons $n_f$ is $Z-\text{core}$ where the core corresponds to the noble‑gas configuration.

ElementAtomic No.General config.
La57$[\text{Xe}]\,5d^{1}\,6s^{2}$
Ce58$[\text{Xe}]\,4f^{1}\,5d^{1}\,6s^{2}$
Th90$[\text{Rn}]\,6d^{2}\,7s^{2}$
U92$[\text{Rn}]\,5f^{3}\,6d^{1}\,7s^{2}$

Key formula

  • Effective nuclear charge: $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$ (where $S$ is shielding constant).
  • Atomic radius trend: $r_{\text{ionic}}$ (pm) decreases slightly across the series due to lanthanide contraction.

Solved example

Find the number of f‑electrons in $\text{Gd}$ ($Z=64$).

Core configuration up to $\text{Xe}$ accounts for 54 electrons. Remaining electrons = $64-54 = 10$. Hence $\text{Gd}$ has $4f^{7}\,5d^{1}\,6s^{2}$, i.e., $n_f = 7$.

SI units: atomic radius – picometre (pm), ionisation energy – kilojoule per mole (kJ·mol$^{-1}$).

Common oxidation states, electronic configurations and periodic trends in the f‑block series

Common oxidation states

  • Lanthanides (4f): +3 is dominant; early members (Ce, Pr, Nd) also show +4, while later members (Eu, Yb) exhibit +2 due to half‑filled $4f^{7}$ or fully filled $4f^{14}$ stability.
  • Actinides (5f): +3 is most frequent; early actinides (U, Np, Pu) display higher states (+4, +5, +6) because 5f orbitals are more radially extended and participate in bonding.

Electronic configurations

General pattern: $[Rn]5f^{1-14}6d^{0-1}7s^{2}$ for actinides and $[Xe]4f^{1-14}5d^{0-1}6s^{2}$ for lanthanides. The $d$‑electron appears only in Ce ($4f^{1}5d^{1}6s^{2}$) and Th ($5f^{0}6d^{2}7s^{2}$) to accommodate extra stability.

ElementCommon oxidation states
Ce$+3$, $+4$
Eu$+2$, $+3$
Yb$+2$, $+3$
U$+3$, $+4$, $+5$, $+6$
Pu$+3$, $+4$, $+5$, $+6$, $+7$

Periodic trends in the f‑block

  • Atomic/ionic radius: decreases gradually across the series (lanthanide/actinide contraction) due to ineffective shielding by f‑electrons.
  • First ionisation energy: shows a slight increase across the series, reflecting stronger nuclear attraction.
  • Electronegativity: low and almost constant; values are taken from Pauling scale (e.g., Ce = 1.12, Yb = 1.10).

Solved example

Find the ground‑state configuration of Curium (Z = 96).

Start from the noble gas core $[Rn]$ (Z = 86). Add 10 electrons: the 5f subshell fills first, then 6d and 7s. Hence, $\text{Cm}:\;[Rn]5f^{7}6d^{0}7s^{2}$. The $5f^{7}$ half‑filled configuration gives extra stability, explaining the common +3 oxidation state of Cm.

Separation of rare‑earth elements and important compounds of lanthanides and actinides

Definition: Rare‑earth elements (REEs) are the 15 lanthanides ($\text{La}$ to $\text{Lu}$) together with scandium and yttrium. Separation of REEs is required because they occur together in minerals such as bastnäsite and monazite.

  • Fractional crystallisation: Based on slight differences in $K_{sp}= [RE^{3+}]^2[CO_3^{2-}]^3$ of their double‑salts (e.g., $RE_2(CO_3)_3\cdot6H_2O$). The solubility product $K_{sp}$ has unit $mol^5\cdotL^{-5}$.
  • Ion‑exchange method: Distribution coefficient $D = \frac{[RE]_{resin}}{[RE]_{solution}}$ (unitless). Higher $D$ for heavier lanthanides.
  • Solvent extraction: Extraction constant $K_{ex}= \frac{[RE\!\-\!extractant]_{org}}{[RE^{3+}]_{aq}}$ (unit $L\cdotmol^{-1}$).

Important compounds:

LanthanideOxideHalide (X = Cl, Br)
$\text{La}$$\text{La}_2\text{O}_3$$\text{LaX}_3$
$\text{Gd}$$\text{Gd}_2\text{O}_3$$\text{GdX}_3$
$\text{Lu}$$\text{Lu}_2\text{O}_3$$\text{LuX}_3$

Solved example (fractional crystallisation):

Given $K_{sp}$ of $Nd_2(CO_3)_3\cdot6H_2O = 1.2\times10^{-22}$ and $Sm_2(CO_3)_3\cdot6H_2O = 2.5\times10^{-22}$ at $25^\circ C$. If a solution contains $0.01\;mol\;L^{-1}$ of each REE, calculate the concentration of $Nd^{3+}$ remaining after the first crystallisation step.

Solution:

For $Nd^{3+}$, $K_{sp}= [Nd^{3+}]^2[CO_3^{2-}]^3$. Assuming $[CO_3^{2-}] \approx 0.01\;mol\;L^{-1}$, we get $[Nd^{3+}] = \sqrt{\frac{K_{sp}}{[CO_3^{2-}]^3}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.2\times10^{-22}}{(0.01)^3}} = 1.1\times10^{-9}\;mol\;L^{-1}$. Thus $>99.99\%$ of $Nd^{3+}$ is removed, illustrating the efficiency of fractional crystallisation.

All constants are expressed in SI units (mol, L, $^\circ C$).

Uses and significance of d‑ and f‑block elements in industry and technology

Definition: d‑block elements are the transition elements having partially filled $(n-1)d$ subshell; f‑block elements are the inner‑transition series with partially filled $(n-2)f$ subshell.

Key industrial uses:

  • Catalysis – $Pt$, $Pd$, $Ni$, $V_2O_5$ in hydrogenation, reforming and oxidation reactions.
  • Magnetic materials – $Fe$, $Co$, $Ni$ alloys for transformers, $Nd_2Fe_{14}B$ permanent magnets.
  • Corrosion‑resistant alloys – $Cr$, $Mo$ in stainless steel and super‑alloys.
  • Electronic & optical devices – $Cu$, $Ag$ conductors; $Ga$, $In$ in semiconductors; $Y$, $Lu$ in phosphors.
  • Specialty chemicals – $Zr$, $Hf$ in nuclear reactors; $U$, $Pu$ (actinides) as nuclear fuel.

Important quantitative relations:

ParameterExpressionSI unit
Catalytic turnover frequency (TOF)$\\text{TOF}=\\frac{\\text{moles of product}}{\\text{moles of active sites}\\times\\text{time}}$$h^{-1}$
Magnetic susceptibility ($\\chi$)$\\chi=\\frac{M}{H}$$m^{3}\\,mol^{-1}$

Solved example:

Given a $Pt$ catalyst with $\\text{TOF}=5\\times10^{4}\\,h^{-1}$, how much catalyst (in moles of surface Pt atoms) is required to produce $10\\,mol$ of ethylene in $2\\,h$?

Solution: $\\text{required moles of active sites}=\\frac{\\text{product moles}}{\\text{TOF}\\times t}=\\frac{10}{5\\times10^{4}\\times2}=1.0\\times10^{-4}\\,mol$. Thus $1.0\\times10^{-4}\\,mol$ of Pt (≈ $1.96\\,g$) is sufficient.

These quantitative tools help design efficient processes and select appropriate d‑ or f‑block elements for industrial applications.
