Electronic configuration of $d$- and $f$-block elements

Definition: The electronic configuration of $d$‑ and $f$‑block elements describes the distribution of electrons among the $(n-1)d$ and $(n-2)f$ orbitals respectively, following the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle and Hund's rule.

Key Rules (Madelung or $n+\ell$ rule):

  • Each orbital is filled in order of increasing $n+\ell$ value.
  • If two subshells have the same $n+\ell$, the one with lower $n$ is filled first.

For $d$‑block: electrons enter the $(n-1)d$ subshell after the $ns$ subshell is half‑filled, giving the general pattern $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^{2}\,(3d)^{1-10}$ for the first series and $[\text{Kr}]\,5s^{2}\,(4d)^{1-10}$ for the second series.

For $f$‑block: electrons occupy the $(n-2)f$ subshell after the $ns$ subshell, leading to $[\text{Xe}]\,6s^{2}\,(4f)^{1-14}$ and $[\text{Rn}]\,7s^{2}\,(5f)^{1-14}$.

Typical Configurations (selected elements):

ElementConfiguration
Sc ($Z=21$)$[\text{Ar}]\,4s^{2}\,3d^{1}$
Fe ($Z=26$)$[\text{Ar}]\,4s^{2}\,3d^{6}$
Y ($Z=39$)$[\text{Kr}]\,5s^{2}\,4d^{1}$
Ce ($Z=58$)$[\text{Xe}]\,6s^{2}\,4f^{1}\,5d^{1}$ (anomaly)

Solved Example: Write the ground‑state configuration of $\mathrm{Fe}$ ($Z=26$).

  1. Identify the nearest noble gas: $[\text{Ar}]$ (Z=18).
  2. Distribute the remaining 8 electrons: fill $4s$ first (2 e⁻) → $4s^{2}$.
  3. Remaining 6 electrons go to $3d$ (since $4s$ is lower $n+\ell$). → $3d^{6}$.
  4. Thus, $\mathrm{Fe}$: $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^{2}\,3d^{6}$.

Note: Electronic configurations are dimensionless; they merely indicate the count of electrons in each subshell.

Anomalous electron configurations and exceptions

Definition: An anomalous electron configuration occurs when the observed arrangement of electrons in an atom or ion deviates from the order predicted by the Aufbau principle, usually to attain a half‑filled or fully‑filled $d$ or $f$ subshell, which offers extra exchange stability.

  • Typical rule: fill lower $n+\ell$ first; if two subshells have the same $n+\ell$, fill the one with lower $n$.
  • Exception arises because a half‑filled ($d^{5}$, $f^{7}$) or fully‑filled ($d^{10}$, $f^{14}$) subshell gains extra exchange energy.

Key points & formulae:

  • Exchange‑stabilisation energy (qualitative): $\\Delta E_{ex}= -\\frac{1}{2}K n_{\\uparrow} n_{\\downarrow}$, where $K$ is the exchange integral and $n_{\\uparrow}, n_{\\downarrow}$ are numbers of electrons with parallel spins.
  • Energy unit for stability: $\\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
  • Common $d$‑block anomalies: Cr ($Z=24$) and Cu ($Z=29$); $f$‑block anomalies: Gd ($Z=64$) and Lu ($Z=71$).

Example (solved): Determine the ground‑state configuration of chromium (Z=24) and explain the anomaly.

StepExplanation
1Expected configuration from Aufbau: $\\text{[Ar]}3d^{4}4s^{2}$.
2Observed configuration: $\\text{[Ar]}3d^{5}4s^{1}$.
3Reason: a half‑filled $3d$ subshell ($3d^{5}$) provides maximum exchange stabilisation, outweighing the energy cost of promoting one $4s$ electron to $3d$.
4Resulting ionisation energy reflects this extra stability; $\text{IE}_{1}$ of Cr is lower than expected, confirming the anomaly.

This pattern recurs across the transition series and must be memorised for quick recall in CBSE examinations.

Oxidation states and their periodic trends

Oxidation states in d‑ and f‑block elements refer to the effective charge an atom acquires after loss or gain of electrons in a compound. It is a dimensionless quantity used to balance redox equations.

Key points and periodic trends

  • Across a period (from left to right) the maximum oxidation state generally increases due to the increasing nuclear charge and availability of d‑electrons for bonding.
  • Down a group the maximum oxidation state decreases because of the poor overlap of 4f/5f orbitals and the lanthanide/actinide contraction.
  • Early transition metals (Sc, Ti, V) exhibit high positive states (+3 to +5) whereas later ones (Ni, Cu, Zn) show lower or fixed states (+2, +1, 0).
  • In the f‑block, the common oxidation states are +3 (most stable) and +2/+4 for selected lanthanides and actinides.
  • Electronegativity (Pauling scale, unitless) and ionisation energy (kJ·mol−1) correlate positively with the ability to attain higher positive oxidation numbers.

General formula for oxidation‑state calculation

$OS = \text{charge of the compound} - \sum \text{(oxidation numbers of all other atoms)}$

Typical oxidation states

ElementCommon $OS$
Sc$+3$
Fe$+2$, $+3$
Cu$+1$, $+2$
Ce$+3$, $+4$
U$+3$, $+4$, $+5$, $+6$

Solved example

Find the oxidation state of Cr in $K_2Cr_2O_7$.

Solution:

  1. Assign known states: $OS_{K}=+1$, $OS_{O}=-2$.
  2. Let $OS_{Cr}=x$. The compound is neutral, so
    $2(+1)+2x+7(-2)=0$.
  3. Simplify: $2+2x-14=0 \Rightarrow 2x=12 \Rightarrow x=+6$.
  4. Thus each Cr atom is in the $+6$ oxidation state.

Understanding these trends helps predict the chemistry of transition and inner‑transition metals in redox reactions and coordination compounds.

Chemical properties of transition metals

Transition Metals – elements of the d‑block (groups 3–12) that possess an incomplete d‑subshell in at least one oxidation state.

  • Variable oxidation states: due to comparable energies of $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ orbitals. Example: $\text{Mn}$ shows $+2$ to $+7$.
  • Formation of coloured compounds: d‑d electronic transitions absorb visible light. Energy of transition $\Delta E = hc/\lambda$ (SI unit: J).
  • Catalytic activity: ability to change oxidation state facilitates redox cycles. Rate constant $k$ (unit: s$^{-1}$).
  • Complex ion formation: high charge density leads to large stability constants $K_f = \frac{[ML_n]}{[M][L]^n}$ (dimensionless).
PropertyTypical Observation
Oxidation states$+2$ to $+7$ (e.g., $\text{Mn}$)
ColourDue to $d\!\!\rightarrow\!d$ transitions; $\lambda$ in 400–700 nm
CatalysisRedox cycles; $\Delta H$ in kJ·mol$^{-1}$
Complex stability$K_f$ often $>10^{10}$

Solved Example

Find the oxidation state of Fe in the complex ion $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$.

  1. Let oxidation state of Fe be $x$.
  2. Each cyanide ion carries $-1$ charge, total charge $6(-1) = -6$.
  3. Overall charge of the ion is $-4$: $x + (-6) = -4$.
  4. Thus $x = +2$.

Hence Fe is in the $+2$ oxidation state, which explains the diamagnetic nature of the complex.

Complex formation and coordination chemistry of $d$-block elements

Complex formation (d‑block)

  • Coordination compound: central metal ion $M^{n+}$ bonded to $x$ ligands $L$ through coordinate covalent bonds, written as $[ML_x]^{n'}$.
  • Coordination number (CN): number of donor atoms around the metal. Common CN = 4 (tetrahedral, square planar) and 6 (octahedral).
  • Oxidation state of metal = charge on $M$ before ligand attachment.

Key formulas

ParameterExpression
Stability constant $K_f$$K_f = \frac{[ML_n]}{[M][L]^n}$ (dimensionless)
Crystal‑field splitting energy$\Delta_o$ (octahedral) or $\Delta_t$ (tetrahedral) in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$
Effective atomic number (EAN)$\text{EAN}=Z_{metal}+n$ where $n$ = total electrons donated by ligands

SI units: $\Delta$ in $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$; concentrations in $\text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

Factors influencing stability

  • Nature of metal (higher charge → larger $K_f$)
  • Nature of ligand (strong field → larger $\Delta$)
  • Chelate effect (multidentate ligands increase $K_f$)

Solved example

Calculate $K_f$ for $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$ if $[Co^{3+}] = 1.0\times10^{-4}\,\text{M}$, $[NH_3] = 0.02\,\text{M}$ and $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}=8.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{M}$.

Solution: $K_f = \frac{[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}}{[Co^{3+}][NH_3]^6}=\frac{8.0\times10^{-5}}{(1.0\times10^{-4})(0.02)^6}=\frac{8.0\times10^{-5}}{1.0\times10^{-4}\times6.4\times10^{-11}}\approx1.3\times10^{10}$. Thus the complex is very stable.

Lanthanide and actinide contraction

Definition: Lanthanide (actinide) contraction is the systematic decrease in atomic/ionic radii of the 4f (5f) series elements from the first to the last element, caused by the poor shielding ability of f‑electrons against the increasing nuclear charge.

Key points

  • Effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$ rises because $S$ (shielding constant) contributed by f‑electrons is almost constant.
  • Resulting radius reduction is approximately $0.2\,\text{Å}$ across the series.
  • Contraction influences oxidation states, complex stability, and separation of rare‑earth elements.

Formula (empirical)

The change in radius can be expressed as $\Delta r = k\,\Delta Z_{\text{eff}}$, where $k\approx 0.02\,\text{Å}$ per unit increase in $Z_{\text{eff}}$ for lanthanides.

SI units

  • Atomic/ionic radius: metre (m), usually expressed in ångströms (1 Å = $10^{-10}\,\text{m}$).
  • Effective nuclear charge: dimensionless.

Example

Given ionic radii $r_{\text{La}^{3+}} = 1.032\,\text{Å}$ and $r_{\text{Lu}^{3+}} = 0.861\,\text{Å}$, calculate the lanthanide contraction.

Solution:

$\Delta r = r_{\text{La}^{3+}} - r_{\text{Lu}^{3+}} = 1.032 - 0.861 = 0.171\,\text{Å}$.

Thus the contraction from La$^{3+}$ to Lu$^{3+}$ is $0.171\,\text{Å}$, close to the expected $0.2\,\text{Å}$.

Impact

  • Higher charge density of later lanthanides/actinides leads to stronger Lewis acidity.
  • Contraction causes overlap of 4f and 5d energy levels, affecting colour and magnetic properties.

Separation and purification of rare earth elements

Definition: Rare‑earth elements (REEs) are the 15 lanthanides ($\mathrm{La}$ to $\mathrm{Lu}$) together with scandium and yttrium. They occur together in ores and show very similar chemical behaviour, hence their separation is challenging.

Key Principles

  • Difference in solubility product ($K_{sp}$) of their salts.
  • Variation of stability constants ($K_f$) of complex ions.
  • Distribution ratio in solvent extraction: $D = \frac{[M]_{org}}{[M]_{aq}}$ (dimensionless).
  • Separation factor: $\alpha_{12} = \frac{D_1}{D_2}$.

Common Methods

MethodBasis
Fractional crystallisationSelective crystallisation of less soluble nitrate/oxalate.
Ion‑exchangeDifferent affinity of REE‑cations for resin (expressed by $K_{ex}$).
Solvent extractionComplex formation with organophosphorus extractants; governed by $D$ and $\alpha$.

Solvent‑Extraction Example

Given: $D_{\mathrm{Nd}} = 12$ and $D_{\mathrm{Pr}} = 3$ at 25 °C. Find the separation factor $\alpha_{\mathrm{Nd/Pr}}$ and state which ion is extracted more efficiently.

Solution:

$$\alpha_{\mathrm{Nd/Pr}} = \frac{D_{\mathrm{Nd}}}{D_{\mathrm{Pr}}} = \frac{12}{3} = 4$$

Since $\alpha > 1$, neodymium is extracted four \times more efficiently than praseodymium.

SI Units

  • Concentration: mol L$^{-1}$
  • Temperature: K or °C
  • $K_{sp}$, $K_f$, $D$, $\alpha$: dimensionless

Industrial applications and environmental aspects

Industrial Applications

  • Transition metals (d‑block): Catalysts (e.g., $Pt$, $Pd$ in hydrogenation), alloying agents ($Fe$, $Cr$, $Ni$) for stainless steel, $Cu$ in electrical wiring, $V$ in $Ti$–$V$ alloys for aerospace.
  • Lanthanides (f‑block): $Ce$ in catalytic cracking, $Nd$ in high‑strength permanent magnets ($Nd_2Fe_{14}B$), $Y$ in phosphors for LEDs, $Lu$ in PET scanners.
  • Actinides: $U$ and $Pu$ as nuclear fuel; $Th$ in thorium‑based reactors.

Environmental Aspects

  • Heavy‑metal toxicity: $Pb$, $Cd$, $Hg$ accumulate in food chain; permissible limit (WHO) $<0.01\ \text{mg\ L}^{-1}$.
  • Acid mine drainage: Oxidation of $Fe^{2+}$ to $Fe^{3+}$ releases $H^+$; overall reaction $4Fe^{2+}+O_2+4H^+\rightarrow4Fe^{3+}+2H_2O$.
  • Radioactive waste management: $U^{238}$ half‑life $=4.5\times10^9\ \text{yr}$; decay constant $\lambda=\frac{\ln2}{t_{1/2}}$.

Bioremediation using $Pseudomonas$ spp. can lower $Cd^{2+}$ concentration to $<0.005\ \text{mg\ L}^{-1}$. The Indian Factories Act (1987) mandates effluent $pH$ between 6.5 and 8.5 for metal‑bearing waste.

Key Formulae

ParameterExpression
Decay constant$\lambda=\frac{\ln2}{t_{1/2}}$ (s$^{-1}$)
Percent extraction$\%E=\frac{\text{mass\ of\ metal\ recovered}}{\text{mass\ \in\ ore}}\times100$

Solved Example

From 500 g of $Cu$ ore containing 2 % Cu, 85 % of the Cu is extracted. Calculate the mass of Cu obtained.

Solution: $\text{initial\ Cu}=0.02\times500\text{\ g}=10\text{\ g}$. $\text{recovered}=0.85\times10\text{\ g}=8.5\text{\ g}$. Hence $\%E=\frac{8.5}{10}\times100=85\%$.
