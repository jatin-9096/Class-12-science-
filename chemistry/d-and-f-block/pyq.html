Case Study 1 – Corrosion of Iron in Urban Water Supply

Passage: The municipal water supply of a mid‑size Indian city is sourced from a river that carries a moderate amount of dissolved oxygen and chloride ions (mainly from agricultural runoff). The water is pumped through a network of iron pipelines that have been in service for more than 15 years. Over the last two years, residents have reported a reddish‑brown deposit on the inner walls of the pipelines and a gradual decline in water pressure. Laboratory analysis of the deposit shows it to be mainly hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·nH₂O. The water chemistry at the point of delivery is as follows: pH = 7.2, [O₂] = 8 mg L⁻¹, [Cl⁻] = 0.5 mmol L⁻¹, temperature = 30 °C. The city authorities want to understand the electrochemical mechanism behind the corrosion, estimate the rate of metal loss, and suggest a feasible mitigation strategy that can be implemented without replacing the entire pipeline network.

Sub‑questions

  1. Write the half‑reactions for the anodic dissolution of iron and the cathodic reduction of dissolved oxygen in neutral water.
  2. Using the Nernst equation, calculate the equilibrium potential for the Fe²⁺/Fe couple at 30 °C, assuming [Fe²⁺] = 10⁻⁶ M.
  3. Explain how the presence of chloride ions accelerates the corrosion process, referring to the formation of soluble complexes.
  4. Given that the measured corrosion current density on the pipe surface is 2 µA cm⁻², estimate the average thickness of iron lost per year (in µm yr⁻¹). Assume the density of iron is 7.87 g cm⁻³ and the atomic weight of Fe is 55.85 g mol⁻¹.
  5. Propose a practical corrosion‑inhibition method suitable for the existing pipeline system and justify its selection based on the water chemistry.

Full Solutions

  1. Anodic reaction (oxidation of iron):

    $$\text{Fe (s)} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-$$

    Cathodic reaction (reduction of dissolved oxygen in neutral water):

    $$\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$$

    The overall corrosion reaction is obtained by balancing electrons (multiply the anodic reaction by 2):

    $$2\text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{Fe}^{2+} + 4\text{OH}^-$$

    Subsequent hydrolysis of Fe²⁺ leads to Fe(OH)₂, which is further oxidised to Fe(OH)₃ and finally to hydrated Fe₂O₃·nH₂O observed as rust.

  2. The standard electrode potential for the Fe²⁺/Fe couple is $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} = -0.44\;\text{V}$ (vs SHE). The Nernst equation at temperature $T$ (in Kelvin) is

    $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]}{a_{\text{Fe}}}$$

    For iron, $n = 2$ and the activity of solid iron $a_{\text{Fe}} = 1$. At 30 °C, $T = 303\;\text{K}$, the temperature‑dependent factor remains $0.0592\;\text{V}$ (valid within ±10 °C of 25 °C). Substituting $[\text{Fe}^{2+}] = 10^{-6}\;\text{M}$:

    $$E = -0.44\;\text{V} - \frac{0.0592}{2}\log(10^{-6})$$ $$E = -0.44\;\text{V} - 0.0296(-6)$$ $$E = -0.44\;\text{V} + 0.1776\;\text{V} = -0.2624\;\text{V}$$

    Thus, the equilibrium potential of the Fe²⁺/Fe couple under the given conditions is approximately $-0.26\;\text{V}$ vs SHE.

  3. Chloride ions act as aggressive agents in several ways:

    • Complex formation: Cl⁻ readily forms soluble complexes with Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, e.g., $\text{FeCl}_2$ and $\text{FeCl}_3$. This reduces the concentration of free Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ near the metal surface, shifting the equilibrium of the anodic reaction to the right and increasing the dissolution rate.
    • Pitting initiation: Adsorbed Cl⁻ ions penetrate the protective oxide/hydroxide film on iron, creating localized breakdown sites (pits). Inside a pit, the pH becomes highly acidic due to hydrolysis of Fe²⁺, further accelerating metal loss.
    • Electrical conductivity: Higher ionic strength improves the solution’s conductivity, facilitating the cathodic reduction of O₂ and thus increasing the overall corrosion current.

    Consequently, even a modest chloride concentration of 0.5 mmol L⁻¹ can significantly enhance the corrosion rate of iron in neutral water.

  4. The corrosion rate can be related to the corrosion current density $i_{\text{corr}}$ by Faraday’s law:

    $$\text{Mass loss per unit area} = \frac{i_{\text{corr}}\,M}{nF}$$

    where $M = 55.85\;\text{g mol}^{-1}$, $n = 2$, and $F = 96485\;\text{C mol}^{-1}$. First convert $i_{\text{corr}}$ to A cm⁻²:

    $$i_{\text{corr}} = 2\;\mu\text{A cm}^{-2} = 2 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{A cm}^{-2}$$

    Mass loss per cm² per second:

    $$\dot{m} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-6}\;\text{A cm}^{-2} \times 55.85\;\text{g mol}^{-1}}{2 \times 96485\;\text{C mol}^{-1}}$$ $$\dot{m} = \frac{1.117 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{g cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{192970} \approx 5.79 \times 10^{-10}\;\text{g cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$

    Convert to yearly loss:

    $$m_{\text{yr}} = 5.79 \times 10^{-10}\;\text{g cm}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1} \times 3.1536 \times 10^{7}\;\text{s yr}^{-1} \approx 0.0183\;\text{g cm}^{-2}\,\text{yr}^{-1}$$

    Now, using the density $\rho = 7.87\;\text{g cm}^{-3}$, the thickness loss $\Delta t$ per year is

    $$\Delta t = \frac{m_{\text{yr}}}{\rho} = \frac{0.0183\;\text{g cm}^{-2}}{7.87\;\text{g cm}^{-3}} \approx 2.33 \times 10^{-3}\;\text{cm yr}^{-1}$$

    Converting to micrometres:

    $$\Delta t \approx 23.3\;\mu\text{m yr}^{-1}$$

    Hence, the iron pipeline loses roughly $23\;\mu\text{m}$ of thickness each year under the observed corrosion current density.

  5. Recommended mitigation – Cathodic protection using a sacrificial anode (zinc or magnesium):**

    • **Principle:** Attach a more anodic metal (e.g., Zn, $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\;\text{V}$) to the iron pipe. The sacrificial metal preferentially oxidises, supplying electrons that suppress the anodic dissolution of iron.
    • **Compatibility with water chemistry:** The existing pH (7.2) and low chloride level do not hinder the operation of a zinc anode. Zinc corrosion products (Zn(OH)₂) are sparingly soluble and do not significantly affect water quality.
    • **Installation ease:** Zinc strips or rods can be clamped onto the interior of the existing pipelines during routine maintenance, avoiding the costly replacement of the entire network.
    • **Economic advantage:** The mass of zinc required is small compared to the volume of iron lost; periodic replacement (every 5–7 years) is far cheaper than pipe renewal.

    Alternative methods such as adding phosphate‑based corrosion inhibitors could also be considered, but they may lead to scaling in low‑pH zones and require careful dosage control. Hence, sacrificial anode cathodic protection is the most practical and reliable solution for the city’s water‑distribution system.
