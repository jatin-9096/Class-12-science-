1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Scope of Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. It encompasses two broad phenomena: (i) the generation of electrical energy from spontaneous redox reactions (galvanic or voltaic cells) and (ii) the driving of non‑spontaneous chemical changes by an external electric current (electrolytic cells). A redox reaction involves the transfer of electrons from a reducing agent (oxidation) to an oxidizing agent (reduction). In a galvanic cell, the spontaneous flow of electrons from the anode (site of oxidation) to the cathode (site of reduction) through an external circuit produces a measurable electromotive force (EMF). The EMF, denoted by $E_{\text{cell}}$, is the difference in electrode potentials of the two half‑cells under standard conditions (1 M concentrations, 1 atm pressure, 25 °C). The standard electrode potential, $E^\circ$, is a thermodynamic quantity tabulated for many half‑reactions and serves as a reference for predicting the direction of electron flow. In contrast, an electrolytic cell requires an external voltage greater than the cell’s EMF to force the reaction to proceed in the opposite direction, thereby enabling processes such as electro‑plating, electro‑refining, and the production of chemicals like chlorine and sodium hydroxide. The fundamental parameters governing electrochemical systems include the number of electrons transferred ($n$), the Faraday constant ($F = 96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$), and the reaction quotient ($Q$), which together determine the cell potential via the Nernst equation. Understanding these definitions provides the foundation for quantitative analysis of cell behaviour, energy efficiency, and the design of practical devices ranging from batteries to fuel cells.

Chemistry · Core Law
Key Electrochemical Laws: Faraday’s Laws and the Nernst Equation

Two cornerstone laws govern the quantitative aspects of electrochemical processes. Faraday’s First Law states that the mass ($m$) of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the total charge ($Q$) passed through the electrolyte: $$m = \frac{Q \, M}{nF}$$ where $M$ is the molar mass of the substance, $n$ the number of electrons involved per formula unit, and $F$ the Faraday constant. Faraday’s Second Law asserts that, for a given quantity of electricity, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($\frac{M}{n}$). These laws enable precise calculation of yields in industrial electro‑lysis and analytical techniques such as coulometry. The Nernst Equation provides a thermodynamic link between cell potential and the activities (or concentrations) of reacting species: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$ At 25 °C, this simplifies to $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log Q$$ where $Q$ is the reaction quotient. The equation predicts how $E$ varies with concentration, temperature, and pressure, allowing chemists to construct concentration cells, determine solubility products, and evaluate the feasibility of redox reactions under non‑standard conditions. When $Q = K_{\text{eq}}$, the cell potential becomes zero, indicating equilibrium. The Nernst equation also underpins the concept of overpotential, where kinetic barriers cause the observed potential to deviate from the thermodynamic value, a phenomenon critical in battery design and corrosion studies.

Chemistry · Core Law
Intuitive Understanding of Electrode Potentials and Cell Construction

Electrode potentials are not absolute values; they are measured relative to a reference electrode, conventionally the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) assigned a potential of $0.00\ \text{V}$. When a metal electrode is immersed in a solution containing its ions, a dynamic equilibrium establishes a potential difference between the metal and the solution. This potential, called the electrode potential, reflects the metal’s tendency to lose or gain electrons. A more positive $E^\circ$ indicates a stronger oxidizing ability (greater tendency to gain electrons), whereas a more negative $E^\circ$ signals a stronger reducing ability (greater tendency to lose electrons). Constructing a galvanic cell involves pairing two half‑cells such that electrons flow spontaneously from the electrode with lower (more negative) potential to the one with higher (more positive) potential. The cell diagram notation $ \text{Zn}| \text{Zn}^{2+} \,||\, \text{Cu}^{2+}|\text{Cu}$ succinctly captures the anode (Zn) on the left, the cathode (Cu) on the right, and the double vertical line denoting the salt bridge or porous membrane that maintains charge neutrality. The salt bridge contains an inert electrolyte (e.g., KCl) that permits ion migration without mixing the two solutions, thereby preventing rapid equilibration that would extinguish the cell voltage. Intuitively, the driving force for electron flow is the difference in free energy between the two half‑reactions. This free‑energy change, $\Delta G$, is related to the cell potential by $\Delta G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}$. A positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ yields a negative $\Delta G$, confirming spontaneity. Conversely, in an electrolytic cell, an external voltage $E_{\text{ext}}$ must exceed $E_{\text{cell}}$ (including any overpotential) to make $\Delta G$ positive, thereby forcing the non‑spontaneous reaction. This conceptual framework aids students in visualising the invisible electron flow and the role of the electrolyte as a medium for ion transport.

Chemistry · Core Law
Common Exceptions and Special Cases in Electrochemical Behaviour

While the Nernst equation and Faraday’s laws provide a robust foundation, several exceptions arise due to kinetic, structural, or thermodynamic nuances. One prominent exception is the phenomenon of overpotential, especially noticeable in reactions involving gas evolution (e.g., hydrogen or oxygen evolution) or metal deposition. Overpotential ($\eta$) represents the extra voltage required beyond the thermodynamic $E$ to overcome activation energy barriers, and it varies with electrode material, surface condition, and current density. For instance, the hydrogen evolution reaction on a platinum electrode exhibits a much lower overpotential than on a stainless‑steel electrode, making Pt the preferred catalyst in electrolyzers. Another special case involves mixed potentials in corrosion. In an aqueous environment, a metal surface may simultaneously undergo oxidation and reduction reactions, leading to a net corrosion potential that is a weighted average of individual half‑cell potentials. The mixed‑potential theory explains why certain alloys (e.g., stainless steel) resist corrosion better than pure metals: the presence of a passive oxide layer shifts the effective potential into a region where both anodic and cathodic rates are minimal. Complex ions and coordination compounds also deviate from simple predictions. The standard potentials for transition‑metal complexes depend on ligand field effects, which alter the metal’s electron configuration and thus its redox behaviour. For example, the $E^\circ$ for the Fe$^{3+}$/Fe$^{2+}$ couple changes markedly when ligands such as cyanide or ethylenediamine are present, reflecting changes in stabilization energy. Lastly, temperature dependence can lead to non‑linear behaviour. While the Nernst equation includes a temperature term, in practice, high temperatures can affect activity coefficients, solvent dielectric constant, and ion mobility, causing observed potentials to diverge from the ideal prediction. Recognising these exceptions equips learners to interpret experimental data critically and to design systems that mitigate undesirable effects.

Chemistry · Core Law
Worked Example: Calculating EMF of a Concentration Cell and Determining Mass Deposited

**Problem:** A concentration cell is constructed using the half‑reaction $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu(s)}$ at both electrodes. The left electrode is immersed in a $0.010\ \text{M}$ $\text{CuSO}_4$ solution, while the right electrode is in a $0.10\ \text{M}$ solution. (a) Calculate the cell EMF at $25^\circ\text{C}$. (b) If a current of $0.50\ \text{A}$ is passed for $2\ \text{h}$, determine the mass of copper deposited on the cathode.

Chemistry · Core Law
Solution to the Worked Example

**(a) EMF Calculation** For a concentration cell, $E^\circ = 0$ because both electrodes involve the same redox couple. The Nernst equation simplifies to $$E = -\frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]_{\text{cathode}}}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]_{\text{anode}}}$$ Here, the cathode is the side with higher ion concentration ($0.10\ \text{M}$) and the anode is the side with lower concentration ($0.010\ \text{M}$). With $n = 2$, $$E = -\frac{0.0592}{2}\log\frac{0.10}{0.010}= -0.0296\log 10 = -0.0296(1) = -0.0296\ \text{V}$$ Since the cell convention defines $E_{\text{cell}}$ as $E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$, the magnitude of the EMF is $|E| = 0.0296\ \text{V}$. The negative sign indicates that electrons flow from the dilute side (anode) to the concentrated side (cathode), as expected. **(b) Mass of Copper Deposited** Total charge passed: $$Q = I t = 0.50\ \text{A} \times (2\ \text{h} \times 3600\ \text{s h}^{-1}) = 0.50 \times 7200 = 3600\ \text{C}$$ Using Faraday’s First Law: $$m = \frac{Q M}{nF}$$ For copper, $M = 63.55\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $n = 2$. Substituting, $$m = \frac{3600\ \text{C} \times 63.55\ \text{g mol}^{-1}}{2 \times 96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}} \approx \frac{228\,780}{192\,970} \approx 1.185\ \

1. Derivation of the Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation relates the cell potential of an electrochemical cell to the activities (or concentrations) of the reactants and products. The derivation begins with the fundamental thermodynamic relation between the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) and the cell potential (E):

$$\Delta G = -nFE$$

where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred, F is Faraday’s constant (≈ 96 485 C mol⁻¹) and E is the cell potential under the given conditions.

For a reaction that is not at standard state, the Gibbs free energy change is expressed as:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Here, ΔG° is the standard Gibbs free energy change, R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and Q is the reaction quotient defined as the product of activities of products divided by that of reactants, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients.

Equating the two expressions for ΔG gives:

$$-nFE = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Recall that the standard cell potential E° is related to ΔG° by the same relationship:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$$

Substituting ΔG° in the previous equation yields:

$$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Dividing through by −nF and rearranging:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

Because chemists frequently use base‑10 logarithms, the natural logarithm can be converted using the identity ln x = 2.303 log₁₀ x. Thus:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} Q$$

At the standard temperature of 298 K (25 °C), the term \(\frac{2.303RT}{F}\) evaluates to 0.05916 V. Therefore, the widely used form of the Nernst equation becomes:

$$\boxed{E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916\ \text{V}}{n}\log_{10} Q}$$

For a half‑cell reaction involving a single ion, the reaction quotient simplifies to the ratio of ion activity to the standard activity (which is unity). In practice, concentrations are used as approximations of activities for dilute solutions, leading to the familiar expression:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916\ \text{V}}{n}\log_{10}\frac{[ \text{Red} ]}{[ \text{Ox} ]}$$

This equation is the cornerstone for calculating cell potentials under non‑standard conditions, and it also underpins the construction of concentration cells, pH meters, and ion‑selective electrodes.

2. Relationship Between Standard Electrode Potential and Gibbs Free Energy

From the definition of standard Gibbs free energy change for a redox reaction, we have:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$$

Rearranging gives the standard electrode potential in terms of thermodynamic quantities:

$$E^\circ = -\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{nF}$$

This relationship is essential when thermodynamic data (ΔG°) are available from tables, allowing the calculation of the corresponding standard electrode potential without experimental electrochemical measurements.

3. Derivation of the Overall Cell Potential for a Galvanic Cell

A galvanic cell consists of two half‑cells: an oxidation half‑cell (anode) and a reduction half‑cell (cathode). The overall cell reaction is obtained by adding the two half‑reactions, ensuring that electrons cancel. The cell potential is the difference between the cathode and anode potentials:

$$E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$$

If the half‑cell potentials are expressed under non‑standard conditions using the Nernst equation, the overall cell potential becomes:

$$E_{\text{cell}} = \left(E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - \frac{0.05916}{n_{\text{cath}}}\log_{10} Q_{\text{cath}}\right) - \left(E^\circ_{\text{anode}} - \frac{0.05916}{n_{\text{an}}}\log_{10} Q_{\text{an}}\right)$$

Since the number of electrons transferred is the same for both half‑reactions in a balanced cell, we set n = n₍cath₎ = n₍an₎. The overall reaction quotient Q for the cell is the product of the individual quotients, leading to the compact form:

$$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10} Q_{\text{cell}}$$

where E°₍cell₎ = E°₍cathode₎ – E°₍anode₎ and Q₍cell₎ is the reaction quotient for the overall cell reaction.

4. Derivation of Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

Faraday’s first law states that the mass (m) of a substance liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity (Q) passed through the electrolyte:

$$m \propto Q$$

Since electric charge is the product of current (I) and time (t), we write:

$$Q = It$$

Introducing a proportionality constant k, we have:

$$m = kIt$$

Faraday’s second law refines the constant k by relating it to the molar mass (M) and the number of electrons (n) involved in the electrode reaction. The amount of substance (in moles) liberated is given by the charge divided by the total charge required to deposit one mole of the substance:

$$\text{moles} = \frac{Q}{nF} = \frac{It}{nF}$$

Multiplying by the molar mass M yields the mass:

$$m = \frac{MIt}{nF}$$

This is the quantitative expression of Faraday’s laws, often written as:

$$\boxed{m = \frac{M}{nF}It}$$

It shows that for a given current and time, the mass deposited is inversely proportional to the number of electrons required per ion and directly proportional to the molar mass of the ion.

5. Derivation of the Relationship Between Cell EMF and Equilibrium Constant

At equilibrium, the cell potential becomes zero (E = 0) because no net current flows. Setting E = 0 in the Nernst equation gives:

$$0 = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10} K$$

where K is the equilibrium constant for the overall cell reaction. Solving for K:

$$\log_{10} K = \frac{nE^\circ}{0.05916}$$

Exponentiating both sides:

$$K = 10^{\frac{nE^\circ}{0.05916}}$$

Alternatively, using natural logarithms and the relation \(\ln K = \frac{nFE^\circ}{RT}\), we obtain:

$$\boxed{ \ln K = \frac{nF E^\circ}{RT} }$$

This equation demonstrates that a large positive standard cell potential corresponds to a very large equilibrium constant, indicating a strongly spontaneous reaction.
