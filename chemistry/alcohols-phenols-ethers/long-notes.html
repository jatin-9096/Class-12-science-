1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Concepts

Electrochemistry deals with the interrelationship between electrical energy and chemical change. A redox (reduction‑oxidation) reaction involves the transfer of electrons from a species that gets oxidised (loss of electrons) to a species that gets reduced (gain of electrons). The species that loses electrons is called the oxidising agent, while the species that gains electrons is called the reducing agent. In an electrochemical cell, two half‑cells are connected such that electrons flow spontaneously from the anode (site of oxidation) to the cathode (site of reduction) through an external circuit. The cell potential, denoted $E_{\text{cell}}$, is the driving force for the electron flow and is measured in volts (V). A positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ indicates a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions, whereas a negative value denotes non‑spontaneity.

The standard electrode potential, $E^\circ$, is measured under standard conditions: $1\ \text{M}$ concentration for all aqueous species, $1\ \text{atm}$ pressure for gases, and $25^\circ\text{C}$ temperature. The convention is that the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) is assigned a potential of $0.00\ \text{V}$. All other electrode potentials are reported relative to SHE. The cell notation for a galvanic cell is written as:

$$\text{Anode (oxidation) }| \text{ Anode solution }|| \text{ Cathode solution }| \text{ Cathode (reduction)}$$

For example, the Daniell cell is represented as $ \text{Zn}(s) | \text{Zn}^{2+}(1\ \text{M}) || \text{Cu}^{2+}(1\ \text{M}) | \text{Cu}(s) $. This notation succinctly conveys the composition of each half‑cell and the direction of electron flow.

Chemistry · Core Law
Electrode Potential and the Nernst Equation

The cell potential under non‑standard conditions is described by the Nernst equation, which relates $E_{\text{cell}}$ to the reaction quotient $Q$ and the number of electrons transferred $n$:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q \qquad \text{(at }25^\circ\text{C)}$$

Here, $Q$ is defined as the ratio of activities (or approximated concentrations) of products to reactants, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients. The Nernst equation shows that as the concentration of reactants decreases or the concentration of products increases, the cell potential diminishes, reflecting a reduced driving force for the reaction.

For a half‑cell reaction, the Nernst equation can be written as:

$$E_{\text{half‑cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{half‑cell}} - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Reduced form}]}{[\text{Oxidised form}]}$$

This form is particularly useful when constructing concentration cells, where the same electrode material is present in both half‑cells but at different concentrations. The potential difference arises solely from the concentration gradient, and the Nernst equation quantifies this effect precisely.

Key points to remember:

  • When $Q = 1$, the Nernst equation reduces to $E = E^\circ$.
  • The sign of $E$ determines spontaneity: $E > 0$ implies a spontaneous process.
  • The temperature dependence can be introduced by replacing $0.0592$ with $\frac{RT}{nF}$, where $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, and $F$ Faraday’s constant.

Chemistry · Core Law
Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

Michael Faraday formulated two fundamental laws that quantify the relationship between the amount of electric charge passed through an electrolyte and the amount of chemical change that occurs.

First Law: The mass ($m$) of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the electrolyte.

$$m = Z \times Q$$

Here, $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance, defined as the mass deposited per coulomb of charge. $Z$ can be expressed in terms of the molar mass ($M$) and the valency ($n$) of the ion:

$$Z = \frac{M}{nF}$$

where $F = 96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$ is Faraday’s constant.

Second Law: For a given quantity of electricity, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights (molar mass divided by valency).

These laws enable precise calculations in electroplating, electrorefining, and industrial electrolysis processes. They also provide a direct method to determine the valency of an unknown ion by measuring the mass deposited for a known charge.

Example of application: In the electrolysis of molten $\text{NaCl}$, passing $1\ \text{F}$ of charge (i.e., $96\,485\ \text{C}$) will deposit $23.0\ \text{g}$ of sodium, because $M_{\text{Na}} = 23.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $n = 1$, giving $Z = \frac{23.0}{1 \times 96\,485} = 2.38 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{g C}^{-1}$. Multiplying by $96\,485\ \text{C}$ yields $23.0\ \text{g}$, confirming the law.

Chemistry · Core Law
Intuition, Exceptions, and Real‑World Considerations

While the theoretical framework of electrochemistry is elegant, real‑world systems often deviate from ideal behaviour. Several factors influence the observed cell potential and the efficiency of electrochemical processes:

  • Activity vs. Concentration: The Nernst equation assumes ideal behaviour where activity coefficients are unity. In concentrated solutions, ion‑ion interactions reduce the activity, leading to lower effective potentials. The corrected form uses activities $a_i = \gamma_i [i]$, where $\gamma_i$ is the activity coefficient.
  • Overpotential (η): At the electrode surface, additional potential is required to overcome kinetic barriers associated with electron transfer. This overpotential is especially significant for gas evolution reactions (e.g., hydrogen or oxygen evolution) and can be expressed as $E_{\text{observed}} = E_{\text{thermodynamic}} + \eta$.
  • Temperature Effects: The Nernst slope $\frac{0.0592}{n}$ varies with temperature ($\frac{RT}{nF}$). At higher temperatures, the slope increases, making the cell potential more sensitive to concentration changes.
  • Electrode Surface Condition: Surface roughness, adsorption of species, and formation of passivation layers can alter the effective area and thus the current density, influencing the rate of electrolysis.
  • Side Reactions: In complex electrolytes, competing redox processes may occur, consuming charge without contributing to the intended deposition. For example, in the electrolysis of aqueous $\text{NaCl}$, chlorine gas evolution at the anode competes with water oxidation, affecting the overall efficiency.
Understanding these deviations is crucial for designing industrial electrolytic cells, batteries, and fuel cells. Engineers often employ catalysts, temperature control, and membrane separators to mitigate unwanted effects and optimise performance.

Exception to Faraday’s Laws: In cases where the deposited species undergoes a chemical transformation immediately after deposition (e.g., formation of an insoluble compound), the measured mass may not correspond directly to the charge passed. Corrections must be applied based on the stoichiometry of the subsequent reaction.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

1. Nernst Equation (Electrochemistry)

The cell potential $E$ of a galvanic cell at any temperature $T$ is related to the standard cell potential $E^\circ$ and the reaction quotient $Q$ by the Nernst equation. Starting from the fundamental relation between Gibbs free energy and cell potential:

$$\Delta G = -nFE$$

where $n$ is the number of moles of electrons transferred and $F$ is the Faraday constant ($96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$). At equilibrium, $\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$, and the standard Gibbs free energy change is $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$. Substituting these expressions gives:

$$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Rearranging for $E$ yields:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

Since chemists prefer base‑10 logarithms, we use $\ln Q = 2.303\log_{10} Q$:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} Q$$

At $25^\circ\text{C}$ ($T = 298\ \text{K}$), the term $\frac{2.303RT}{F}$ evaluates to $0.05916\ \text{V}$. Thus the practical form becomes:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10} Q$$

For a half‑cell reaction $a\text{A} + ne^- \rightleftharpoons b\text{B}$, the reaction quotient $Q$ is expressed as $\frac{[\text{B}]^b}{[\text{A}]^a}$. Substituting this into the equation provides the final Nernst expression used in CBSE examinations.

2. Rate Law from the Method of Initial Rates (Chemical Kinetics)

Consider a generic elementary reaction:

$$\text{aA} + \text{bB} \rightarrow \text{Products}$$

The rate law is assumed to be of the form:

$$\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}]^x[\text{B}]^y$$

where $k$ is the rate constant, and $x$, $y$ are the orders with respect to A and B respectively. To determine $x$ and $y$, we perform a series of experiments keeping one concentration constant while varying the other. Suppose the following data are obtained (all at constant temperature):

  • Experiment 1: $[\text{A}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$, $[\text{B}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$, Rate $= 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M s}^{-1}$
  • Experiment 2: $[\text{A}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$, $[\text{B}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$, Rate $= 8.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M s}^{-1}$
  • Experiment 3: $[\text{A}] = 0.10\ \text{M}$, $[\text{B}] = 0.20\ \text{M}$, Rate $= 4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M s}^{-1}$

Dividing the rate of Experiment 2 by Experiment 1 eliminates $[\text{B}]$:

$$\frac{8.0\times10^{-3}}{2.0\times10^{-3}} = \frac{k(0.20)^x(0.10)^y}{k(0.10)^x(0.10)^y} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^x = 2^x$$

Thus $4 = 2^x$ giving $x = 2$. Similarly, dividing Experiment 3 by Experiment 1 eliminates $[\text{A}]$:

$$\frac{4.0\times10^{-3}}{2.0\times10^{-3}} = \frac{k(0.10)^x(0.20)^y}{k(0.10)^x(0.10)^y} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^y = 2^y$$

Hence $2 = 2^y$ and $y = 1$. The overall rate law becomes:

$$\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}]^2[\text{B}]$$

To find $k$, substitute any experimental set, e.g., Experiment 1:

$$2.0\times10^{-3} = k(0.10)^2(0.10) \Rightarrow k = \frac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{1.0\times10^{-3}} = 2.0\ \text{s}^{-1}\ \text{M}^{-2}$$

3. Arrhenius Equation (Temperature Dependence of Rate Constant)

The empirical Arrhenius equation relates the rate constant $k$ to absolute temperature $T$:

$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$

where $A$ is the frequency factor, $E_a$ is the activation energy (J mol$^{-1}$), $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$). Taking natural logarithms on both sides gives a linear form:

$$\ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{RT}$$

Plotting $\ln k$ versus $1/T$ yields a straight line with slope $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$. To derive $E_a$ from two temperature points $T_1$ and $T_2$ with corresponding rate constants $k_1$ and $k_2$, subtract the two equations:

$$\ln k_2 - \ln k_1 = -\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

Rearranging for $E_a$:

$$E_a = R\frac{\ln(k_1/k_2)}{(1/T_2)-(1/T_1)}$$

This expression is frequently used in CBSE practical questions where $k$ values are obtained at two temperatures.

4. Derivation of the Integrated Rate Law for a First‑Order Reaction

For a first‑order reaction $A \rightarrow \text{Products}$, the differential rate law is:

$$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt} = k[\text{A}]$$

Separating variables and integrating from $t = 0$ (where $[\text{A}] = [\text{A}]_0$) to a generic time $t$ (where $[\text{A}] = [\text{A}]_t$) gives:

$$\int_{[\text{A}]_0}^{[\text{A}]_t}\frac{d[\text{A}]}{[\text{A}]} = -k\int_{0}^{t}dt$$ $$\ln[\text{A}]_t - \ln[\text{A}]_0 = -kt$$

Or, using logarithm properties:

$$\ln\frac{[\text{A}]_t}{[\text{A}]_0} = -kt$$

Converting to base‑10 logarithm (common in textbooks):

$$\log_{10}\frac{[\text{A}]_t}{[\text{A}]_0} = -\frac{k t}{2.303}$$

Rearranging for $[\text{A}]_t$ yields the familiar exponential decay form:

$$[\text{A}]_t = [\text{A}]_0 e^{-kt}$$

The half‑life $t_{1/2}$ is obtained by setting $[\text{A}]_t = \frac{1}{2}[\text{A}]_0$:

$$\frac{1}{2} = e^{-kt_{1/2}} \Rightarrow \ln 2 = k t_{1/2} \Rightarrow t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k}$$

5. Derivation of the Integrated Rate Law for a Second‑Order Reaction (A + A)

For a second‑order reaction where two molecules of the same species react, the rate law is:

$$-\frac{d[\text{A}]}{dt} = k[\text{A}]^2$$

Separating variables:

$$\int_{[\text{A}]_0}^{[\text{A}]_t}\frac{d[\text{A}]}{[\text{A}]^2} = -k\int_{0}^{t}dt$$ $$\left[-\frac{1}{[\text{A}]}\right]_{[\text{A}]_0}^{[\text{A}]_t} = -kt$$ $$\frac{1}{[\text{A}]_t} - \frac{1}{[\text{A}]_0} = kt$$

Thus the integrated form is:

$$\frac{1}{[\text{A}]_t} = kt + \frac{1}{[\text{A}]_0}$$

The half‑life for a second‑order reaction depends on the initial concentration:

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[\text{A}]_0}$$

6. Derivation of the Equilibrium Constant Expression from Thermodynamics

For a general reversible reaction:

$$\nu_1\text{A}_1 + \nu_2\text{A}_2 \rightleftharpoons \nu_3\text{A}_3 + \nu_4\text{A}_4$$

The standard Gibbs free energy change is linked to the equilibrium constant $K$ by:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$$

From the definition of $\Delta G$ for any state:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

At equilibrium, $\Delta G = 0$ and $Q = K$, thus:

$$0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln K \Rightarrow \Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$$
