Case Study 1 – Removal of Hardness from Water Using Ion‑Exchange Resins

Passage: Hard water, which contains high concentrations of calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) ions, is a common problem in many Indian towns. The presence of these divalent cations leads to scale formation in boilers, reduces the efficiency of soaps, and causes wear in household appliances. A municipal water treatment plant has adopted a cation‑exchange process where hard water is passed through a column packed with a strong‑acidic resin in the sodium form, $\text{R–SO}_3\text{Na}$. The exchange reaction can be represented as: $$\text{R–SO}_3\text{Na} + \text{Ca}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{R–SO}_3\text{Ca} + \text{Na}^+$$ and similarly for magnesium ions. The resin is regenerated by flushing with a concentrated NaCl solution, restoring it to the sodium form. The plant processes 500 m³ of water per hour, and the inlet water has a hardness of 250 mg L\(^{-1}\) expressed as CaCO\(_3\). The target is to reduce hardness to below 60 mg L\(^{-1}\). The resin capacity is 2.0 eq kg\(^{-1}\) (equivalents of cation per kilogram of dry resin).

Sub‑questions

  1. Calculate the total amount (in equivalents) of hardness ions that must be removed from 1 hour of water flow.
  2. Determine the minimum mass of dry ion‑exchange resin required to achieve the desired hardness reduction, assuming 90 % utilization of the resin capacity.
  3. Write the balanced overall ion‑exchange reaction for simultaneous removal of Ca\(^{2+}\) and Mg\(^{2+}\) ions, assuming the inlet water contains 60 % calcium hardness and 40 % magnesium hardness (by mass of CaCO\(_3\)).
  4. If the regeneration solution contains 10 % (w/v) NaCl, calculate the volume of regeneration solution needed to fully regenerate 1 kg of exhausted resin.
  5. Discuss two environmental advantages of using ion‑exchange technology over chemical precipitation for hardness removal.

Full Solutions

  1. Hardness expressed as CaCO\(_3\) can be converted to equivalents using the equivalent weight of CaCO\(_3\) (50 g eq\(^{-1}\)). The inlet hardness is 250 mg L\(^{-1}\) = 0.250 g L\(^{-1}\). Equivalents per litre: $$\text{eq L}^{-1}= \frac{0.250\ \text{g L}^{-1}}{50\ \text{g eq}^{-1}} = 5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{eq L}^{-1}$$ For 1 hour, the volume treated is 500 m³ = 5.0 × 10⁵ L. Total equivalents to be removed: $$\text{eq}_{\text{total}} = 5.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{eq L}^{-1}\times5.0\times10^{5}\ \text{L}=2.5\times10^{3}\ \text{eq}$$ The target hardness is 60 mg L\(^{-1}\) (0.060 g L\(^{-1}\)), which corresponds to $$\text{eq}_{\text{target}} = \frac{0.060}{50}=1.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{eq L}^{-1}$$ Equivalents that remain after treatment: $$\text{eq}_{\text{remain}} = 1.2\times10^{-3}\times5.0\times10^{5}=6.0\times10^{2}\ \text{eq}$$ Hence, equivalents that must be removed: $$\boxed{2.5\times10^{3}-6.0\times10^{2}=1.9\times10^{3}\ \text{eq}}$$

  2. The resin capacity is 2.0 eq kg\(^{-1}\). With 90 % utilization, the effective capacity becomes $$C_{\text{eff}} = 0.90\times2.0 = 1.8\ \text{eq kg}^{-1}$$ Required resin mass: $$m_{\text{resin}} = \frac{1.9\times10^{3}\ \text{eq}}{1.8\ \text{eq kg}^{-1}} \approx 1.06\times10^{3}\ \text{kg}$$ Therefore, a minimum of **≈ 1 060 kg** of dry resin is needed.

  3. Let the fraction of calcium hardness be 0.60 and magnesium hardness be 0.40 (by CaCO\(_3\) mass). Convert these fractions to equivalents: calcium (Ca\(^{2+}\)) contributes 2 eq per mole of CaCO\(_3\), magnesium (Mg\(^{2+}\)) also 2 eq per mole. The overall ion‑exchange reaction can be written as: $$\begin{aligned} &0.60\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Na} + \text{Ca}^{2+} \rightarrow 0.60\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Ca} + 2\,\text{Na}^+ \\ &0.40\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Na} + \text{Mg}^{2+} \rightarrow 0.40\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Mg} + 2\,\text{Na}^+ \end{aligned}$$ Combining both, the net balanced equation (per equivalent of hardness removed) is: $$\text{R–SO}_3\text{Na} + \frac{0.60}{1}\,\text{Ca}^{2+} + \frac{0.40}{1}\,\text{Mg}^{2+} \rightarrow 0.60\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Ca} + 0.40\,\text{R–SO}_3\text{Mg} + 2\,\text{Na}^+$$ This representation emphasizes that each divalent cation replaces two Na\(^+\) ions on the resin.

  4. Regeneration replaces the bound Ca\(^{2+}\) and Mg\(^{2+}\) with Na\(^+\) from NaCl. The stoichiometry per equivalent of exchanged cation is 1 mol NaCl → 1 eq Na\(^+\). For 1 kg of exhausted resin (capacity 2.0 eq kg\(^{-1}\)), the total equivalents to be regenerated are 2.0 eq. Moles of NaCl required: $$n_{\text{NaCl}} = 2.0\ \text{eq}=2.0\ \text{mol}$$ Mass of NaCl needed: $$m_{\text{NaCl}} = 2.0\ \text{mol}\times58.44\ \text{g mol}^{-1}=116.9\ \text{g}$$ A 10 % (w/v) NaCl solution contains 10 g NaCl per 100 mL, i.e., 0.10 g mL\(^{-1}\). Volume required: $$V = \frac{116.9\ \text{g}}{0.10\ \text{g mL}^{-1}} = 1.169\times10^{3}\ \text{mL}=1.17\ \text{L}$$ Hence, **≈ 1.2 L** of 10 % NaCl solution regenerates 1 kg of resin.

  5. Environmental advantage 1 – Minimal sludge generation: Ion‑exchange replaces hardness ions with sodium ions in solution, producing a clear effluent. In contrast, chemical precipitation (e.g., using lime) generates large volumes of insoluble calcium carbonate or magnesium hydroxide sludge that requires disposal, often leading to land‑filling or further treatment.

    Environmental advantage 2 – Lower chemical consumption: The regeneration step uses only a dilute NaCl solution, which is inexpensive and environmentally benign. Lime‑softening consumes large quantities of calcium hydroxide and acid for pH adjustment, increasing the plant’s carbon footprint and the risk of acid‑base imbalance in receiving water bodies.

Case Study 2 – Green Synthesis of Silver Nanoparticles Using Plant Extracts

Passage: Nanotechnology has found applications in antimicrobial coatings, water disinfection, and medical diagnostics. Conventional chemical routes for synthesizing silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) involve toxic reducing agents such as sodium borohydride, which pose environmental and health hazards. A research group at an Indian university explored a green synthesis route using aqueous extract of Azadirachta indica (neem) leaves. The phytochemicals (flavonoids, terpenoids) act as both reducing and stabilizing agents. In a typical experiment, 50 mL of 1 mM AgNO\(_3\) solution was mixed with 5 mL of leaf extract and heated at 80 °C for 30 minutes. The colour changed from colourless to brown, indicating formation of AgNPs. UV‑Vis spectroscopy showed a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at 420 nm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed spherical particles with an average diameter of 15 nm. The synthesized AgNPs exhibited a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 8 µg mL\(^{-1}\) against Escherichia coli. The cost of the plant extract is negligible, and the process generates no hazardous by‑products. The study suggests that such green routes can replace conventional methods for large‑scale production of nanomaterials.
