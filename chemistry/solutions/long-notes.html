Concentration terms and units

Solution: homogeneous mixture of solute & solvent.

  • Molarity (M): $M = \\frac{n_{solute}}{V_{solution}}$ (mol L$^{-1}$)
  • Molality (m): $m = \\frac{n_{solute}}{mass_{solvent\,(kg)}}$ (mol kg$^{-1}$)
  • Normality (N): $N = \\frac{equivalents}{V_{solution}}$ (eq L$^{-1}$)
  • Mass %: $\% w/w = \\frac{mass_{solute}}{mass_{solution}}\\times100$
  • Volume %: $\% v/v = \\frac{V_{solute}}{V_{solution}}\\times100$
  • ppm: $ppm = \\frac{mass_{solute}}{mass_{solution}}\\times10^{6}$
  • ppb: $ppb = \\frac{mass_{solute}}{mass_{solution}}\\times10^{9}$
  • Mole fraction (χ): $\\chi_{solute}= \\frac{n_{solute}}{n_{solute}+n_{solvent}}$

Key relation: For solutes where one mole provides $z$ equivalents, $N = M\\times z$.

TermFormulaSI Unit
Molarity$M = \\frac{n}{V}$mol L$^{-1}$
Molality$m = \\frac{n}{kg}$mol kg$^{-1}$
Normality$N = \\frac{eq}{V}$eq L$^{-1}$
Mass %$\% w/w = \\frac{m_{sol}}{m_{sol}+m_{solv}}\\times100$%
ppm$ppm = \\frac{m_{sol}}{m_{solution}}\\times10^{6}$mg L$^{-1}$ (approx)

Example: Find molarity of 58.5 g NaCl (M.W. = 58.5 g mol$^{-1}$) in 0.500 L solution.

Steps:

  1. $n = \\frac{58.5\,g}{58.5\,g\,mol^{-1}} = 1\,mol$
  2. $M = \\frac{n}{V} = \\frac{1\,mol}{0.500\,L} = 2.00\,M$

Thus the solution is $2.00\,M$ NaCl.

Molarity, molarity calculations and solution stoichiometry

Definition: Molarity ($M$) is the amount of solute (in moles) present per litre of solution.

SI unit: $\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$ (often written as M).

  • Formula: $M = \frac{n\text{ (mol)}}{V\text{ (L)}}$ where $n$ is moles of solute and $V$ is volume of solution.
  • From mass: $n = \frac{m}{M_{r}}$ so $M = \frac{m}{M_{r}V}$ ($m$ = mass of solute, $M_{r}$ = molar mass).
  • Solution stoichiometry (dilution): $M_{1}V_{1}=M_{2}V_{2}$.
QuantitySymbolUnitExpression
Molarity$M$$\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$$M = \frac{n}{V}$
Moles$n$$\text{mol}$$n = \frac{m}{M_{r}}$
Mass$m$$\text{g}$$m = nM_{r}$

Example (Molarity calculation):

Find the molarity of a solution prepared by dissolving $58.5\,\text{g}$ of NaCl ($M_{r}=58.44\,\text{g\,mol}^{-1}$) in $0.500\,\text{L}$ of water.

Step 1: $n = \frac{58.5}{58.44}=1.001\,\text{mol}$

Step 2: $M = \frac{1.001}{0.500}=2.00\,\text{M}$

The solution is $2.00\,\text{M}$ NaCl.

Molality, normality and related concentration concepts

Molality (m) – amount of solute (in moles) per kilogram of solvent.

Formula: $\\frac{n_{solute}}{kg_{solvent}}$ → $m = \\frac{\\text{moles of solute}}{\\text{mass of solvent (kg)}}$.

SI unit: $\\text{mol·kg}^{-1}$.

Normality (N) – equivalents of solute per litre of solution.

One equivalent is the amount of substance that furnishes or consumes one mole of H⁺ or e⁻ in a reaction.

Formula: $N = \\frac{\\text{equivalents}}{\\text{volume of solution (L)}}$.

SI unit: $\\text{eq·L}^{-1}$.

Key relations

QuantityExpression
Molarity (M)$M = \\frac{n_{solute}}{V_{solution}}$ (mol·L$^{-1}$)
Normality (N)$N = M \\times n_{eq}$
Molality (m)$m = \\frac{M}{\\rho - M \\times M_{solvent}}$

Example

Calculate the molality of 58.5 g NaCl (M = 58.44 g·mol$^{-1}$) dissolved in 500 g water.

  • moles of NaCl = $\\frac{58.5}{58.44}=1.001\\text{ mol}$
  • mass of solvent = $0.500\\text{ kg}$
  • molality $m = \\frac{1.001}{0.500}=2.00\\text{ m}$

Mole fraction, mole percent and related expressions

Definitions

  • Mole fraction (XA): $X_A = \\frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B + \dots}$ (dimensionless).
  • Mole percent (%A): $\\%_A = X_A \times 100$.
  • Mass fraction (wA): $w_A = \\frac{m_A}{m_{\\text{mix}}}$.

Key Relations

QuantityExpression
Mole fraction$X_A = \\frac{n_A}{\\sum n_i}$
Mole percent$\\%_A = X_A \times 100$
Mass fraction$w_A = \\frac{X_A M_A}{\\sum X_i M_i}$
Relation with molarity (M)$X_A = \\frac{M_A V}{\\sum M_i V_i}$ (for same volume)

SI Units

  • Mole fraction, mole percent, mass fraction – dimensionless (expressed in % for mole percent).
  • Molar mass $M$ – $\\text{kg mol}^{-1}$ (or $\\text{g mol}^{-1}$).

Example

In a binary mixture, $n_A = 2\\ \text{mol}$ and $n_B = 3\\ \text{mol}$. Find $X_A$, $\\%_A$ and $w_A$ if $M_A = 40\\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $M_B = 60\\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

Solution:

  • $X_A = \\frac{2}{2+3} = \\frac{2}{5} = 0.40$.
  • $\\%_A = 0.40 \times 100 = 40\\%$.
  • Mass of A = $2 \times 40 = 80\\ \text{g}$, mass of B = $3 \times 60 = 180\\ \text{g}$, total mass = $260\\ \text{g}$.
  • $w_A = \\frac{80}{260} = 0.3077 \approx 30.8\\%$.

These expressions are frequently used in solution stoichiometry and colligative‑property calculations.

Colligative properties – boiling point elevation and freezing point depression

Definition: A colligative property depends only on the number of solute particles in a given amount of solvent, not on their nature.

Key formulas:

  • Boiling‑point elevation: $\Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m$
  • Freezing‑point depression: $\Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m$
  • Molality: $m = \\frac{n_{solute}}{W_{solvent\,(kg)}}$

Symbols & SI units

SymbolMeaningUnit
$\Delta T_b,\ \Delta T_f$Change in boiling / freezing point$^\circ\mathrm{C}$
$i$Van’t Hoff factor (number of particles formed)dimensionless
$K_b,\ K_f$Boiling‑point / freezing‑point constant of the solvent$^\circ\mathrm{C\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$
$m$Molality of the solution$\mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$

Brief derivation (boiling‑point elevation)

At the boiling point the vapor pressure of the solution equals the external pressure. Adding a non‑volatile solute lowers the solvent’s vapor pressure by a factor $x_{solvent}$ (Raoult’s law). To restore the original vapor pressure the temperature must be raised, giving $\Delta T_b = iK_b m$.

Solved example

Calculate the boiling‑point elevation when $1\ \mathrm{mol}$ of glucose ($i=1$) is dissolved in $500\ \mathrm{g}$ of water. $K_b$(water) $=0.512\ ^\circ\mathrm{C\,kg\,mol^{-1}}$.

Solution:

  1. Convert mass of water to kilograms: $W = 0.500\ \mathrm{kg}$.
  2. Molality $m = \\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol}}{0.500\ \mathrm{kg}} = 2\ \mathrm{mol\,kg^{-1}}$.
  3. $\Delta T_b = i K_b m = 1 \times 0.512 \times 2 = 1.024\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
  4. New boiling point $= 100.00 + 1.024 = 101.024\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Vapor pressure lowering and Raoult’s law

Vapor pressure lowering is the decrease in the equilibrium vapor pressure of a solvent when a non‑volatile solute is dissolved.

Raoult’s law (ideal solution) : $P_{\text{solution}} = X_{\text{solvent}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$

where $P_{\text{solution}}$ = vapor pressure of the solution, $X_{\text{solvent}}$ = mole fraction of solvent, $P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$ = vapor pressure of pure solvent (at same $T$).

Vapor‑pressure lowering, $\Delta P$, is obtained by subtracting the two pressures:

$$\Delta P = P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}} - P_{\text{solution}} = X_{\text{solute}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$$

Since $X_{\text{solute}} = \dfrac{n_{\text{solute}}}{n_{\text{solute}}+n_{\text{solvent}}}$, for dilute solutions $X_{\text{solute}}\approx\dfrac{n_{\text{solute}}}{n_{\text{solvent}}}$ and $\Delta P$ is directly proportional \to the amount of solute (a colligative property).

SymbolMeaningUnit
$P_{\text{solution}}$Vapor pressure of solutionPa or atm
$P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$Vapor pressure of pure solventPa or atm
$X_{\text{solvent}}$, $X_{\text{solute}}$Mole \frac{t}ions–
$\Delta P$Vapor‑pressure loweringPa or atm

Example : Calculate $\Delta P$ when 1 mol of a non‑volatile solute is dissolved \in 1 kg of water at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ ($P^{\circ}_{\text{water}}=23.8\ \text{mmHg}$).

  • $n_{\text{solvent}} = \dfrac{1000\ \text{g}}{18\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 55.56\ \text{mol}$
  • $X_{\text{solute}} = \dfrac{1}{1+55.56}=0.0177$
  • $\Delta P = 0.0177 \times 23.8 = 0.421\ \text{mmHg}$
  • $P_{\text{solution}} = 23.8 - 0.421 = 23.38\ \text{mmHg}$

Thus, the solution’s vapor pressure is lowered by $0.42\ \text{mmHg}$, illustrating Raoult’s law.

Osmotic pressure and its applications

Definition: Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) is the minimum pressure that must be applied on the solution side of a semipermeable membrane to stop the net flow of solvent from pure solvent to the solution.

Derivation (van’t Hoff): At equilibrium the chemical potentials of the solvent are equal: $\mu_1^{\text{pure}} = \mu_1^{\text{solution}} + V_1\pi$. Using $\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln a$ and for a dilute solution $a \approx 1-\frac{n_2}{n_1}$, we obtain $\pi = iMRT$, where $i$ is the van’t Hoff factor.

SymbolMeaningUnit
$\pi$Osmotic pressurePa
$i$van’t Hoff factor–
$M$Molaritymol·L$^{-1}$
$R$Universal gas constant8.314 J·mol$^{-1}$·K$^{-1}$
$T$Absolute temperatureK

Key formula: $\pi = iMRT$ (SI: $\pi$ in Pa, $M$ in mol·m$^{-3}$, $T$ in K).

Units: 1 Pa = 1 N·m$^{-2}$; often expressed in atm (1 atm = $1.013\times10^5$ Pa).

Example: Calculate $\pi$ for a 0.10 mol·L$^{-1}$ glucose solution at $25^{\circ}$C (298 K). Glucose does not dissociate, so $i=1$.

Solution: $M = 0.10\,\text{mol·L}^{-1}=0.10\times10^{3}\,\text{mol·m}^{-3}=100\,\text{mol·m}^{-3}$. $\pi = iMRT = 1\times100\times8.314\times298 \approx 2.48\times10^{5}\,\text{Pa}=2.45\,\text{atm}$.

Preparation of solutions and solution stoichiometry

Solution: A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in larger amount is the solvent and the other(s) are solutes.

Concentration terms (SI units)

TermFormulaUnit
Molarity (M)$M = \frac{n}{V}$mol L$^{-1}$
Molality (m)$m = \frac{n}{W_{solvent}}$mol kg$^{-1}$
Normality (N)$N = \frac{equivalents}{V}$eq L$^{-1}$
Mass % (w/w)$\% w/w = \frac{mass_{solute}}{mass_{solution}}\times100$%
Mole fraction (X)$X_{solute}=\frac{n_{solute}}{n_{total}}$dimensionless

Preparation of a solution

  • Weigh the required amount of solute.
  • Dissolve in a portion of solvent (usually water) in a beaker.
  • Transfer to a volumetric flask and make up to the mark with solvent.

Solution stoichiometry

  • For dilution: $C_1V_1 = C_2V_2$ where $C$ is concentration and $V$ is volume.
  • For reacting solutions: use $n = M\times V$ to find moles, then apply stoichiometric coefficients.

Example

Prepare $250\,\text{mL}$ of $0.2\,\text{M}$ NaCl solution.

  1. Calculate moles needed: $n = M\times V = 0.2\times0.250 = 0.050\,\text{mol}$.
  2. Mass of NaCl: $m = n\times M_{w}=0.050\times58.44 = 2.92\,\text{g}$.
  3. Weigh $2.92\,\text{g}$ NaCl, dissolve in about $150\,\text{mL}$ water, transfer to a $250\,\text{mL}$ volumetric flask and make up to the mark.
