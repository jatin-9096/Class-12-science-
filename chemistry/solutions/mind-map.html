Concentration terms and calculations

Concentration Terms – Quick Reference

  • Molarity (M): $\\text{M}=\\frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}}$, SI unit $\\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$.
  • Molality (m): $\\text{m}=\\frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solvent}}}$, SI unit $\\text{mol\,kg}^{-1}$.
  • Normality (N): $\\text{N}=\\frac{\text{equivalents}}{V_{\text{solution}}}$, $\\text{eq\,L}^{-1}$.
  • Mole fraction (X): $\\displaystyle X_{A}=\\frac{n_{A}}{\sum n_{i}}$ (dimensionless).
  • Mass percent (% w/w): $\\displaystyle \% w/w=\\frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solution}}}\times100$.
  • Parts per million (ppm): $\\displaystyle \text{ppm}=\\frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solution}}}\times10^{6}$.

Key Derivations

TermFormulaDerivation
Molarity$\\text{M}=\\frac{n}{V}$Number of moles $n=\frac{m}{M_{r}}$, $V$ in litres.
Molality$\\text{m}=\\frac{n}{m_{\text{solvent}}}$Mass of solvent $m_{\text{solvent}}$ in kg.
Normality$\\text{N}=\\frac{n\times\text{valency}}{V}$Equivalents = $n\times\text{valency}$.

Solved Example

Calculate the molarity of a solution prepared by dissolving $58.5\,\text{g}$ of NaCl (M$_{r}=58.5\,\text{g\,mol}^{-1}$) in $500\,\text{mL}$ of water.

  1. Number of moles $n=\\frac{58.5}{58.5}=1\,\text{mol}$.
  2. Volume of solution $V=500\,\text{mL}=0.500\,\text{L}$.
  3. Molarity $M=\\frac{1}{0.500}=2.00\,\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$.

Thus, the solution is $2.00\,\text{M}$ NaCl.

Molarity, Molality and Normality

Molarity (M) – number of moles of solute per litre of solution. $M = \frac{n}{V_{sol}}$ where $n$ is in mol and $V_{sol}$ in L. SI unit: mol·L$^{-1}$.

Molality (m) – moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. $m = \frac{n}{m_{solvent}}$. SI unit: mol·kg$^{-1}$.

Normality (N) – equivalents of solute per litre of solution. $N = \frac{eq}{V_{sol}} = \frac{n\times f}{V_{sol}}$, $f$ = equivalents per mole. SI unit: eq·L$^{-1}$.

Key relations

  • $M = \frac{N}{f}$
  • $m = \frac{M}{\rho - M\times M_{solute}}$ (approx., $\rho$ = density of solution in kg·L$^{-1}$)

Conversion table

QuantitySymbolFormula
Molarity$M$$M = \frac{n}{V_{sol}}$
Molality$m$$m = \frac{n}{m_{solvent}}$
Normality$N$$N = \frac{n\times f}{V_{sol}}$

Example: Prepare $250\,\text{mL}$ of $0.2\,\text{M}$ NaCl solution.

Required moles $n = M\times V = 0.2\times 0.250 = 0.050\,\text{mol}$. Mass $= n\times M_{NaCl}=0.050\times 58.44 = 2.92\,\text{g}$. We weigh $2.92\,\text{g}$ NaCl and dissolve in water and make up the volume to $250\,\text{mL}$.

Mole fraction and mole percent

Definition: The mole fraction of component $i$ in a mixture is the ratio of the amount of substance of that component to the total amount of substance.

$$X_i = \\frac{n_i}{\\sum_{j} n_j}$$

It is a dimension‑less quantity (SI unit: –).

Mole percent (mole %) is simply the mole \frac{t}ion expressed \in per cent:

$$\\text{Mole \%}_i = X_i \\times 100$$

Key relations

  • Mass \frac{t}ion $w_i = \\frac{m_i}{\\sum m_j}$.
  • Connecting $X_i$ and $w_i$: $$X_i = \\frac{w_i / M_i}{\\sum_{j} w_j / M_j}$$ where $M_i$ is molar mass (kg·mol$^{-1}$).

Typical values

Component$n$ (mol)$X_i$
Water2$\\frac{2}{5}=0.40$
Ethanol3$\\frac{3}{5}=0.60$

Example: A solution contains 2 mol of $\\mathrm{H_2O}$ and 3 mol of $\\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}$. Find the mole \frac{t}ion and mole percent of water.

Solution: Total moles $n_{\\text{tot}} = 2 + 3 = 5$ mol.
$$X_{\\mathrm{H_2O}} = \\frac{2}{5} = 0.40$$
$$\\text{Mole \%}_{\\mathrm{H_2O}} = 0.40 \\times 100 = 40\%$$

Thus water constitutes 0.40 mole fraction or 40 % mole percent of the mixture.

Colligative properties – boiling point elevation and freezing point depression

Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles, not on their nature. The two most important for Class‑12 are boiling‑point elevation ($\Delta T_b$) and freezing‑point depression ($\Delta T_f$).

Key definitions

  • Molality, $m = \frac{n_{solute}}{mass_{solvent}\;(kg)}$ (unit: mol·kg⁻¹).
  • Van’t Hoff factor, $i$, number of particles formed per formula unit (e.g., $i=2$ for NaCl).

Boiling‑point elevation

The rise in boiling point is given by

$\Delta T_b = K_b\,m$

where the ebullioscopic constant

$K_b = i\,\frac{R\,T_b^2}{\Delta H_{vap}}$

($R=8.314\,J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}$, $T_b$ = normal boiling point in K, $\Delta H_{vap}$ = molar enthalpy of vaporisation in J·mol⁻¹). SI unit of $\Delta T_b$ and $K_b$ is kelvin (K).

Freezing‑point depression

The depression in freezing point is

$\Delta T_f = K_f\,m$

with the cryoscopic constant

$K_f = i\,\frac{R\,T_f^2}{\Delta H_{fus}}$

($T_f$ = normal freezing point, $\Delta H_{fus}$ = molar enthalpy of fusion). Units are identical to those of $K_b$.

Derivation (outline)

  • Start from Raoult’s law: $P_{solution}=x_{solvent}\,P^\circ_{solvent}$.
  • Equate chemical potentials of liquid and vapour at the new boiling/freezing point.
  • Replace mole fraction $x_{solvent}=\frac{1}{1+im}$ and linearise for dilute solutions ($im\ll1$).
  • Obtain $\Delta T = \frac{RT^2}{\Delta H}\,i m$, which defines $K_b$ or $K_f$.

Table of symbols

SymbolMeaningUnit
$\Delta T_b$Boiling‑point elevationK
$\Delta T_f$Freezing‑point depressionK
$K_b$Ebullioscopic constantK·kg·mol⁻¹
$K_f$Cryoscopic constantK·kg·mol⁻¹
$m$Molalitymol·kg⁻¹

Solved example

Calculate the boiling‑point elevation when 0.500 mol of a non‑volatile solute ($i=1$) is dissolved in 250 g of water. $K_b$(water) = 0.512 K·kg·mol⁻¹.

Molality: $m = \frac{0.500}{0.250}=2.00\;\text{mol·kg}^{-1}$.

Elevation: $\Delta T_b = K_b\,m = 0.512\times2.00 = 1.024\;\text{K}$.

New boiling point = $100.0\;\degree C + 1.024\;\text{K} \approx 101.0\;\degree C$.

Vapor pressure lowering – Raoult’s law

Vapor pressure lowering (Raoult’s law) – When a non‑volatile solute is dissolved in a volatile solvent, the vapour pressure of the solution ($P$) becomes lower than that of the pure solvent ($P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$). The decrease, $\Delta P$, is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the solute.

Key formula

  • $\Delta P = P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}-P = X_{\text{solute}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$
  • $P = X_{\text{solvent}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$ where $X_{\text{solvent}}=1-X_{\text{solute}}$

Derivation (outline)

  1. For an ideal solution, the escaping tendency of each component is proportional to its mole fraction.
  2. Hence $P_{\text{solvent}} = X_{\text{solvent}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$.
  3. Since $X_{\text{solvent}} = 1- X_{\text{solute}}$, substitute to obtain $\Delta P = X_{\text{solute}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$.

Symbols & SI units

SymbolMeaningUnit
$\Delta P$Vapour‑pressure loweringPa (or atm, mmHg)
$P^{\circ}_{\text{solvent}}$Vapour pressure of pure solventPa
$X_{\text{solute}}$Mole fraction of solutedimensionless

Solved example

Calculate the vapour‑pressure lowering when 1.00 mol of a non‑volatile solute is dissolved in 1.00 kg of water. $P^{\circ}_{\text{water}} = 23.8\,\text{mmHg}$ at 25 °C.

Number of moles of water $=\frac{1000\,\text{g}}{18.0\,\text{g mol}^{-1}}=55.5\,\text{mol}$.

Mole fraction of solute $X_{\text{solute}} = \frac{1.00}{1.00+55.5}=0.0177$.

Vapour‑pressure lowering $\Delta P = X_{\text{solute}}\,P^{\circ}_{\text{water}} = 0.0177\times23.8=0.42\,\text{mmHg}$.

New vapour pressure $P = 23.8-0.42 = 23.38\,\text{mmHg}$.

This illustrates the linear dependence of $\Delta P$ on the solute mole fraction as stated by Raoult’s law.

Ideal and non‑ideal solutions

Ideal Solution – a binary mixture that obeys Raoult’s law for the entire composition range. The mole fraction of component $i$ in the vapour phase $y_i$ equals its mole fraction in the liquid $x_i$ when the vapour behaves ideally.

Raoult’s law : $P=\sum_{i}x_iP_i^{*}$ where $P$ is the total vapour pressure (Pa) and $P_i^{*}$ is the vapour pressure of pure $i$ (Pa).

Non‑ideal Solution – shows deviation from Raoult’s law. Positive deviation ($P>\sum x_iP_i^{*}$) indicates weaker intermolecular forces; negative deviation indicates stronger forces.

  • Activity $a_i=\gamma_i x_i$, $\gamma_i$ being the activity coefficient (dimensionless).
  • Modified Raoult’s law: $P_i=\gamma_i x_i P_i^{*}$.
  • Relative volatility $\alpha_{AB}=\dfrac{K_A}{K_B}=\dfrac{\gamma_A P_A^{*}}{\gamma_B P_B^{*}}$.
  • Henry’s law for a dilute component: $P_i=k_H x_i$ (Pa, $k_H$ in Pa).
  • Excess Gibbs energy $G^{E}=RT\sum_{i}x_i\ln\gamma_i$ (J·mol$^{-1}$).
TermExpressionUnits
Partial pressure (ideal)$P_i=x_iP_i^{*}$Pa
Partial pressure (non‑ideal)$P_i=\gamma_i x_i P_i^{*}$Pa
Activity coefficient$\gamma_i=\dfrac{P_i}{x_iP_i^{*}}$–

Example: Calculate the total vapour pressure of an ideal binary solution containing 0.40 mol fraction ethanol ($P_{EtOH}^{*}=7.9\ \text{kPa}$) and 0.60 mol fraction water ($P_{H_2O}^{*}=3.2\ \text{kPa}$).

Solution: $P=0.40\times7.9+0.60\times3.2=3.16+1.92=5.08\ \text{kPa}$.

Solubility product (Ksp) and common ion effect

Solubility product (Ksp) – For a sparingly soluble salt AB that dissociates as AB(s) ⇒ A+ + B-, the equilibrium constant is

ExpressionUnits
$K_{sp}= [A^{+}][B^{-}]$$\text{mol}^2\ \text{L}^{-2}$ (often quoted as dimensionless)

Derivation: The general equilibrium constant is $K = \dfrac{[A^{+}][B^{-}]}{[AB]}$. The activity of a pure solid is taken as 1, so $K_{sp}= [A^{+}][B^{-}]$.

Common‑ion effect – Adding an ion common to the salt shifts the equilibrium to the left, decreasing solubility. For a 1:1 salt, if the concentration of the common ion is $c$, the new solubility $s$ is

$s = \dfrac{K_{sp}}{c}$

Key points

  • Only concentrations of aqueous ions appear in $K_{sp}$.
  • $K_{sp}$ is temperature dependent; larger $K_{sp}$ means higher solubility.
  • Units are often omitted because $K_{sp}$ is expressed in terms of activities.

Example

Calculate the solubility of AgCl in a 0.10 M NaCl solution. $K_{sp}(\text{AgCl}) = 1.8\times10^{-10}$.

Using the common‑ion expression:

$s = \dfrac{1.8\times10^{-10}}{0.10} = 1.8\times10^{-9}\ \text{M}$

Thus the solubility of AgCl is $1.8\times10^{-9}$ mol L-1, much lower than in pure water.

Preparation of solutions and dilution

Solution: A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in larger amount is the solvent and the lesser amount is the solute.

Concentration terms (SI units)

TermExpressionSI unit
Mass percent (w/w)$\frac{m_{solute}}{m_{solution}}\times100$g 100 g-1
Molarity (M)$M=\frac{n_{solute}}{V_{solution}}$mol L-1
Molality (b)$b=\frac{n_{solute}}{m_{solvent}}$mol kg-1
Normality (N)$N=\frac{eq_{solute}}{V_{solution}}$eq L-1
Mole fraction (X)$X_{A}=\frac{n_{A}}{\sum n_{i}}$dimensionless

Preparation of a solution

  • Weigh the required mass of solute (using analytical balance).
  • Dissolve in a portion of solvent in a beaker.
  • Transfer to a volumetric flask and make up to the calibrated mark with solvent.

Dilution

The amount of solute remains constant during dilution, therefore $n=C\times V$ is invariant. From this, the dilution relation is derived:

$C_{1}V_{1}=C_{2}V_{2}$

where $C_{1}$ and $V_{1}$ are concentration and volume of the stock solution, and $C_{2}$ and $V_{2}$ are those of the diluted solution.

Solved example

Prepare $250\,\text{mL}$ of $0.1\,\text{M}$ HCl using a $1.0\,\text{M}$ stock solution.

Using $C_{1}V_{1}=C_{2}V_{2}$:

$V_{1}=\frac{C_{2}V_{2}}{C_{1}}=\frac{0.1\times250}{1.0}=25\,\text{mL}$

Take $25\,\text{mL}$ of the $1.0\,\text{M}$ HCl and dilute to $250\,\text{mL}$ with distilled water in a volumetric flask. The final solution is $0.1\,\text{M}$ HCl.
