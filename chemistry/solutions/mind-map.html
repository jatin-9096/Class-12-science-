1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Basic Concepts

Electrochemistry deals with the inter‑conversion of chemical and electrical energy. The central idea is a redox (reduction‑oxidation) process, where electrons are transferred from a reducing agent (oxidised) to an oxidising agent (reduced). An oxidation is the loss of electrons, denoted by an increase in oxidation number, while a reduction is the gain of electrons, signified by a decrease in oxidation number. The species that loses electrons is called the anode and the one that gains electrons is the cathode in an electrochemical cell.

An electrochemical cell consists of two half‑cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. The two half‑cells are linked by a salt bridge or porous membrane that maintains electrical neutrality. The potential difference between the two electrodes under open‑circuit conditions is called the electromotive force (EMF) or cell potential, symbolised by $E_{\text{cell}}$. When the cell operates spontaneously as a galvanic (voltaic) cell, $E_{\text{cell}}$ is positive; when external energy is supplied to drive a non‑spontaneous reaction, the cell functions as an electrolytic cell and $E_{\text{cell}}$ is negative.

Standard conditions (denoted by a superscript $^\circ$) refer to $1\ \text{M}$ concentration for all solutes, $1\ \text{atm}$ pressure for gases, and $25^\circ\text{C}$ (298 K) temperature. The standard electrode potential $E^\circ$ of a half‑reaction is measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which is arbitrarily assigned $E^\circ_{\text{SHE}} = 0.00\ \text{V}$. The sign and magnitude of $E^\circ$ indicate the tendency of a species to gain electrons (positive $E^\circ$) or lose electrons (negative $E^\circ$). Understanding these definitions is essential before proceeding to quantitative aspects of electrochemistry.

Chemistry · Core Law
Key Laws Governing Electrochemical Processes

The quantitative description of electrochemical phenomena rests on two fundamental laws formulated by Michael Faraday. Faraday’s First Law states that the mass ($m$) of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the cell: $$m = Z\,Q$$ where $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance. Since $Q = I t$ (current $I$ multiplied by time $t$), the law can be expressed as $$m = Z I t.$$

Faraday’s Second Law asserts that when the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E$). Mathematically, $$\frac{m_1}{E_1} = \frac{m_2}{E_2} = \frac{Q}{F}$$ where $F$ is the Faraday constant ($F = 96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$). The equivalent weight $E$ is related to molar mass $M$ and the number of electrons $n$ transferred in the half‑reaction: $$E = \frac{M}{n}.$$

The relationship between the Gibbs free energy change ($\Delta G$) and cell potential is given by $$\Delta G = -n F E_{\text{cell}}.$$ At standard conditions, $$\Delta G^\circ = -n F E^\circ_{\text{cell}}.$$ This equation links thermodynamics with electrochemistry and explains why a positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ corresponds to a negative $\Delta G^\circ$, indicating a spontaneous reaction.

The Nernst Equation extends the concept of cell potential to non‑standard conditions: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q,$$ where $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$), $T$ is temperature in kelvin, $n$ is the number of electrons transferred, and $Q$ is the reaction quotient. At $25^\circ\text{C}$, the equation simplifies to $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q.$$ Mastery of these laws enables precise calculation of electrode potentials, deposited masses, and the feasibility of redox processes.

Chemistry · Core Law
Intuitive Understanding and Conceptual Tools

To develop intuition about electrochemical cells, it is useful to visualise the cell diagram, which succinctly represents the composition and order of the half‑reactions. A typical galvanic cell is written as: $$\text{Anode (oxidation)}\ |\ \text{Anode solution}\ ||\ \text{Cathode solution}\ |\ \text{Cathode (reduction)}.$$ The double vertical line denotes the salt bridge. For example, a $\text{Zn|Zn}^{2+}\ ||\ \text{Cu}^{2+}| \text{Cu}$ cell indicates zinc oxidation at the left electrode and copper reduction at the right electrode.

The sign convention for cell potential follows the rule: $E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$, where each $E$ is the standard reduction potential of the respective half‑reaction. This convention ensures that a positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ corresponds to a spontaneous galvanic process. The table of standard reduction potentials, often memorised by students, provides a quick way to predict the direction of electron flow: the half‑reaction with higher $E^\circ$ will occur as reduction (cathode), while the one with lower $E^\circ$ will be reversed (oxidation at the anode).

Spontaneity can also be judged by the cell’s overall reaction quotient $Q$. If $E > 0$, the reaction proceeds forward; if $E < 0$, the reverse reaction is favoured. The Nernst equation shows how changes in concentration, pressure, or temperature shift $E$, thereby influencing the direction and extent of the reaction. For instance, increasing the concentration of reactants in the cathodic half‑reaction raises $E$, making the cell more powerful.

Another intuitive tool is the concept of electrochemical series, which orders metals and ions according to their $E^\circ$ values. Metals higher in the series (more negative $E^\circ$) are stronger reducing agents and are more readily oxidised. Conversely, ions higher in the series (more positive $E^\circ$) are stronger oxidising agents. This series explains everyday phenomena such as corrosion of iron (Fe) in the presence of oxygen and water, and the ability of copper to plate onto a zinc surface in a galvanic cell.

Chemistry · Core Law
Exceptions, Special Cases and Practical Considerations

While the ideal equations presented earlier assume perfect conditions, real‑world electrochemical systems often deviate due to several factors. Overpotential is a kinetic barrier that requires an additional potential beyond the thermodynamic $E_{\text{cell}}$ to drive an electrochemical reaction at a measurable rate. Overpotential is especially significant for gas evolution reactions such as hydrogen or oxygen evolution, where the observed cell voltage is higher than predicted by the Nernst equation.

Concentration cells are a special class where both electrodes involve the same redox couple but at different concentrations. The cell potential arises solely from the concentration gradient and is given by $$E = \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[{\text{Ox}}]_1}{[{\text{Ox}}]_2}.$$ These cells are useful for determining diffusion potentials and for calibrating concentration measurements.

In many practical electrolytes, complex ion formation alters the effective concentration of free ions. For example, in a copper(II) sulfate solution containing ammonia, the formation of $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$ reduces the activity of free $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, thereby affecting the measured $E_{\text{cell}}$. The Nernst equation must then incorporate the activity of the complex species, or the equilibrium constant for complex formation must be considered.

Temperature dependence is another source of deviation. The term $\frac{RT}{nF}$ in the Nernst equation indicates that $E$ varies linearly with temperature. At higher temperatures, the magnitude of the logarithmic term increases, which can either raise or lower $E$ depending on whether $Q$ is greater or less than 1. This principle underlies the operation of thermally activated batteries and fuel cells.

Finally, the presence of a salt bridge or porous membrane is crucial for maintaining charge neutrality. If the bridge fails, charge buildup can halt the flow of electrons, causing the cell voltage to drop to zero even though the redox reaction is still thermodynamically favourable. Understanding these practical nuances helps students bridge the gap between textbook calculations and laboratory observations.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this segment we present a comprehensive, CBSE‑aligned derivation of the most frequently used electrochemical equations. The derivations are laid out in a step‑by‑step fashion, each step justified with fundamental thermodynamic or kinetic principles. All mathematical expressions are rendered in LaTeX for clarity.

1. Relation between Gibbs Free Energy Change and Cell Potential

The fundamental thermodynamic link between the change in Gibbs free energy ($\Delta G$) of an electrochemical cell and its electromotive force (EMF, $E$) is derived from the definition of electrical work. For a reversible cell, the maximum non‑expansion work obtainable is the electrical work $W_{\text{elec}}$, given by

$$W_{\text{elec}} = -nFE$$

where $n$ is the number of moles of electrons transferred, $F$ is the Faraday constant ($96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$), and $E$ is the cell potential in volts (V). Since at constant temperature and pressure the maximum work equals $-\Delta G$, we write

$$\Delta G = -nFE$$

Rearranging, the cell potential can be expressed as

$$E = -\frac{\Delta G}{nF}$$

This equation is the cornerstone for all subsequent electrochemical derivations.

2. Standard Cell Potential and Standard Gibbs Free Energy

When all reactants and products are in their standard states (1 bar for gases, 1 M for solutes, pure solids/liquids), the Gibbs free energy change is denoted $\Delta G^\circ$ and the cell potential $E^\circ$. Substituting these standard quantities into the previous relation yields

$$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$$

Thus, the standard EMF of a cell can be directly calculated from tabulated standard Gibbs free energies of formation, and vice‑versa.

3. Derivation of the Nernst Equation

The Nernst equation provides the cell potential under non‑standard conditions. Starting from the general expression for Gibbs free energy at any state:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

where $R$ is the universal gas constant ($8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$), $T$ is the absolute temperature (K), and $Q$ is the reaction quotient. Substituting $\Delta G = -nFE$ and $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ$ gives

$$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q$$

Dividing through by $-nF$:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

For convenience, the natural logarithm is often converted to base‑10 logarithm using $\ln x = 2.303\log_{10}x$:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} Q$$

At $25^\circ\text{C}$ ($298\ \text{K}$), the factor $\frac{2.303RT}{F}$ simplifies to $0.05916\ \text{V}$, leading to the widely used form:

$$\boxed{E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10} Q}\qquad (T = 298\ \text{K})$$

This equation is indispensable for calculating cell potentials when concentrations differ from unity.

4. Derivation of the Relation between $K_{\text{sp}}$ and $E^\circ$ for Solubility‑Controlled Electrodes

Consider a sparingly soluble salt $MX_{(s)} \rightleftharpoons M^{m+} + X^{n-}$ with solubility product $K_{\text{sp}} = [M^{m+}][X^{n-}]$. When this salt forms one electrode of a galvanic cell, the electrode reaction can be written as

$$M^{m+} + ne^- \rightleftharpoons M_{(s)}$$

The Nernst equation for this half‑cell is

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10}[M^{m+}]$$

Since $[M^{m+}] = \frac{K_{\text{sp}}}{[X^{n-}]}$, substituting gives

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{K_{\text{sp}}}{[X^{n-}]}\right)$$

Rearranging to isolate $K_{\text{sp}}$ yields

$$\log_{10}K_{\text{sp}} = n\frac{E^\circ - E}{0.05916} + \log_{10}[X^{n-}]$$

This relationship is useful for determining solubility products from measured electrode potentials.

5. Derivation of the Temperature‑Dependent Form of the Nernst Equation

When temperature deviates significantly from $298\ \text{K}$, the temperature term must be retained explicitly. Starting again from

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$

and converting to base‑10 logarithm:

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10} Q$$

Thus the temperature‑dependent coefficient is $\frac{2.303RT}{nF}$. At $T = 310\ \text{K}$ (physiological temperature), the coefficient becomes

$$\frac{2.303 \times 8.314 \times 310}{n \times 96\,485} \approx \frac{0.0615}{n}\ \text{V}$$

Hence the Nernst equation at $310\ \text{K}$ reads

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0615}{n}\log_{10} Q$$

Students should memorize the $0.05916$ factor for $298\ \text{K}$ and understand how to adjust it for other temperatures.

6. Derivation of the Relationship between $E_{\text{cell}}$ and $K_{\text{eq}}$

For any redox reaction at equilibrium, the reaction quotient $Q$ equals the equilibrium constant $K_{\text{eq}}$. Setting $E = 0$ in the Nernst equation (since no net EMF exists at equilibrium) gives

$$0 = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln K_{\text{eq}}$$

Rearranging:

$$E^\circ = \frac{RT}{nF}\ln K_{\text{eq}}$$

Or, in base‑10 form:

$$\boxed{E^\circ = \frac{0.05916}{n}\log_{10} K_{\text{eq}}}\qquad (T = 298\ \text{K})$$

This equation illustrates why reactions with large $K_{\text{eq}}$ possess high positive standard potentials.

7. Derivation of the Randles–Sevcik Equation (for CV peak current)

Although primarily a physical‑chemistry concept, the Randles–Sevcik equation is part of the CBSE syllabus under electrochemical techniques. For a reversible redox couple, the peak current $i_p$ observed in cyclic voltammetry is given by

$$i_p = (2.69\times10^5)\,n^{3/2} A D^{1/2} C^* \nu^{1/2}$$

where:

  • $n$ = number of electrons transferred per molecule,
  • $A$ = electrode area (cm²),
  • $D$ = diffusion coefficient (cm² s⁻¹),
  • $C^*$ = bulk concentration (mol cm⁻³),
  • $\nu$ = scan rate (V s⁻¹).

The derivation starts from Fick’s first law and the Nernstian boundary condition at the electrode surface. By solving the diffusion equation under a linear potential sweep, one obtains the above proportionality. The constant $2.69\times10^5$ arises from the combination of universal constants ($R$, $F$, $T$) evaluated at $298\ \text{K}$.

8. Derivation of the Butler‑Volmer Equation

The Butler‑Volmer equation describes the current density ($j$) as a function of overpotential ($\eta$) for an electrode reaction:

$$j = j_0\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha nF\eta}{RT}\right) - \exp\!\left(-\frac{(1-\alpha)nF\eta}{RT}\right)\right]$$

where $j_0$ is the exchange current density, $\alpha$ is the charge‑transfer coefficient (0 < $\alpha$ < 1). The derivation proceeds by considering the forward ($j_f$) and reverse ($j_r$) reaction rates, each obeying an Arrhenius‑type dependence on the activation energy modified by the electrical work term $nF\eta$. The net current is the difference $j = j_f - j_r$, leading directly to the expression above.

9. Derivation of the Relationship between $E_{\text{cell}}$ and $pH$ (for hydrogen electrode)

For the half‑reaction involving hydrogen ions:

$$2\text{H}^+ + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2(g)$$

The Nernst equation becomes

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.05916}{2}\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{H}_2}}{[ \text{H}^+]^2}\right)$$

Assuming $P_{\text{H}_2}=1\ \text{atm}$, the expression simplifies to

$$E = 0 - 0.05916\,\text{pH}$$

since $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[ \text{H}^+]$. This linear dependence of electrode potential on pH is the basis of the pH‑meter calibration.
