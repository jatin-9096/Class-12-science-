1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Definition and Scope of Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. It studies redox reactions that either produce an electric current (galvanic or voltaic cells) or are driven by an external current (electrolytic cells). The fundamental premise is that oxidation and reduction always occur simultaneously; electrons released in oxidation travel through an external circuit to the site of reduction. In CBSE Class 12, the chapter “Electrochemistry” consolidates concepts such as electrode potentials, the Nernst equation, Faraday’s laws of electrolysis, and applications like batteries, fuel cells, and corrosion. Understanding electrochemistry requires a clear grasp of several key terms: anode (electrode where oxidation occurs), cathode (electrode where reduction occurs), electrolyte (medium that conducts ions), and cell potential ($E_{\text{cell}}$), which is the difference between the reduction potentials of the cathode and anode. The scope extends beyond laboratory cells to industrial processes such as metal plating, purification of metals, and the operation of electrochemical sensors. By linking thermodynamics with kinetics, electrochemistry provides a quantitative framework to predict the spontaneity of redox processes, calculate equilibrium constants, and design efficient energy storage devices. The chapter also introduces the concept of standard electrode potential ($E^\circ$) measured under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 25 °C), which serves as a reference for constructing cell diagrams and evaluating reaction feasibility.

Chemistry · Thermodynamics
Electrode Potentials and the Nernst Equation

The electrode potential of a half‑cell is a measure of its tendency to gain electrons (be reduced). Standard electrode potentials ($E^\circ$) are tabulated against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which is arbitrarily assigned a potential of 0 V. For a generic half‑reaction $$\text{Ox} + ne^- \rightarrow \text{Red}$$ the Nernst equation relates the actual electrode potential ($E$) to the activities (or concentrations) of the reacting species: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Red}]}{[\text{Ox}]}$$ (at 25 °C, where 0.0592 V = $\frac{RT}{F}\ln10$). This equation reveals that $E$ becomes less positive (or more negative) when the concentration of the oxidised form increases relative to the reduced form, reflecting Le Chatelier’s principle at the electrochemical level. In a galvanic cell, the overall cell potential is given by $$E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$$ where each electrode potential is evaluated using the Nernst equation if non‑standard conditions prevail. The sign of $E_{\text{cell}}$ determines spontaneity: a positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ indicates a spontaneous redox process, while a negative value denotes a non‑spontaneous reaction that must be driven electro‑lytically. The Nernst equation also explains why concentration cells, where both electrodes involve the same redox couple but at different concentrations, generate a potential purely from the concentration gradient. For example, a Cu$^{2+}$/Cu concentration cell with $[{\rm Cu^{2+}}]_{\text{left}} = 0.10\,$M and $[{\rm Cu^{2+}}]_{\text{right}} = 1.0\,$M yields $$E = -\frac{0.0592}{2}\log\frac{0.10}{1.0}=0.0296\ \text{V}.$$ Such quantitative tools are indispensable for predicting cell behaviour, designing batteries, and interpreting electrochemical data.

Chemistry · Kinetics
Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis and Their Applications

Michael Faraday formulated two fundamental laws that link the amount of electricity passed through an electrolyte to the mass of substance deposited or liberated at the electrodes. First law: The mass ($m$) of a substance produced at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the cell, $$m = ZQ,$$ where $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance (g C$^{-1}$). Second law: When the same quantity of electricity is passed, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E = \frac{M}{n}$, where $M$ is molar mass and $n$ the number of electrons exchanged). Combining both laws yields the practical expression $$m = \frac{M}{nF}Q,$$ with $F = 96\,485\,$C mol$^{-1}$ (Faraday constant). These relationships enable precise calculations in industrial electro‑plating, purification of metals, and analytical techniques such as coulometry. **Worked Example:** In an electrolytic cell, 0.500 g of copper is deposited on the cathode when a current of 2.00 A flows for 30 min. Verify Faraday’s law. First compute the total charge: $Q = I t = 2.00\ \text{A} \times 30\ \text{min} \times 60\ \text{s min}^{-1}=7200\ \text{C}$. For copper, $M = 63.55\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$ and $n = 2$ (Cu$^{2+}$ + 2e$^-$ → Cu). The theoretical mass from Faraday’s law: $$m = \frac{M}{nF}Q = \frac{63.55}{2 \times 96\,485}\times 7200 \approx 0.237\ \text{g}.$$ The experimental mass (0.500 g) exceeds the theoretical value, indicating that side reactions (e.g., water reduction) or measurement errors contributed extra mass. This example illustrates how Faraday’s laws provide a baseline for evaluating efficiency and purity in electro‑chemical processes.

Chemistry · Electrochemical Cells
Construction, Operation, and Exceptions in Galvanic Cells

A galvanic (voltaic) cell consists of two half‑cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. The half‑cells are linked by a salt bridge or porous membrane that permits ion flow while preventing mixing of the solutions. The anode (oxidation) releases electrons that travel through the external circuit to the cathode (reduction). The cell diagram succinctly represents the arrangement, for example: $$\text{Zn(s)}| \text{Zn}^{2+}(1\ \text{M}) || \text{Cu}^{2+}(1\ \text{M})| \text{Cu(s)}.$$ The cell potential for this Zn–Cu cell under standard conditions is $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} - E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = 0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) = 1.10\ \text{V}$. **Exceptions:** Not all redox couples follow the simple additive rule for $E_{\text{cell}}$. In cells involving gases at pressures different from 1 atm, the Nernst equation must incorporate partial pressures. Moreover, when a reaction involves a solid or pure liquid, its activity is unity and does not appear in the Nernst expression. Another notable exception is the presence of complex ions; the formation constant ($K_f$) of a complex can shift the effective concentration of the free ion, thereby altering the measured $E_{\text{cell}}$. For instance, in a silver–silver chloride electrode, the potential depends on the solubility product ($K_{sp}$) of AgCl: $$E = E^\circ_{\text{Ag}^+/\text{Ag}} - \frac{0.0592}{1}\log[\text{Ag}^+] = E^\circ_{\text{AgCl}/\text{Ag}} - \frac{0.0592}{1}\log\frac{K_{sp}}{[\text{Cl}^-]}.$$ Understanding these nuances is essential for accurate cell design, especially in analytical electrochemistry where reference electrodes (e.g., saturated calomel electrode) rely on such equilibria. The practical operation of galvanic cells also demands attention to polarization (deviation from ideal behaviour) caused by gas bubble formation, concentration gradients, and electrode surface passivation. Mitigation strategies include stirring the electrolyte, using porous electrodes, or adding depolarizers such as $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ in Daniell cells.

Electrochemical Technologies: Batteries, Fuel Cells, and Corrosion Prevention

The theoretical principles of electrochemistry translate directly into technologies that power modern life. **Primary batteries** (e.g., Zn–MnO$_2$ dry cell) are designed for one‑time use; their cell potential is fixed by the intrinsic redox couple and cannot be regenerated. **Secondary (rechargeable) batteries** such as lead‑acid, Ni‑Cd, and Li‑ion rely on reversible redox reactions. 2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this comprehensive section, we present step‑by‑step derivations of the most frequently used equations in CBSE Class 12 Chemistry. Each derivation follows the standard textbook approach, beginning with fundamental definitions, applying thermodynamic identities, and arriving at the final algebraic expression. The derivations are written in a clear, pedagogical style suitable for revision and exam preparation.

We start with the derivation of the Gibbs free‑energy change for a process at constant temperature and pressure. The differential form of the first law of thermodynamics for a closed system is

$$dU = \delta Q - \delta W = \delta Q - p\,dV.$$

For a reversible process, the heat exchanged is related to the change in entropy by $\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$. Substituting this into the first law gives

$$dU = T\,dS - p\,dV.$$

Now, the enthalpy $H$ is defined as $H = U + pV$. Differentiating, we obtain

$$dH = dU + p\,dV + V\,dp = T\,dS + V\,dp.$$

At constant pressure ($dp = 0$), the differential of enthalpy reduces to $dH = T\,dS$. Rearranging, we find the fundamental relation

$$dS = \frac{dH}{T}.$$

For a finite change from state 1 to state 2, integration yields

$$\Delta S = \int_{1}^{2}\frac{dH}{T}.$$

Similarly, the Gibbs free energy is defined as $G = H - TS$. Differentiating, we obtain

$$dG = dH - T\,dS - S\,dT = -S\,dT + V\,dp.$$

At constant temperature and pressure ($dT = dp = 0$), the differential simplifies to $dG = 0$, implying that the Gibbs free energy is a state function whose change between two states is

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S.$$

This is the cornerstone equation for predicting spontaneity of processes under standard laboratory conditions.

Next, we derive the expression for the molar heat capacity at constant pressure, $C_p$. By definition, the heat added at constant pressure is

$$\delta Q_p = C_p\,dT.$$

From the first law, $\delta Q_p = dH$ at constant pressure. Equating the two expressions gives

$$C_p = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_p.$$

Integrating $C_p$ over a temperature interval provides the enthalpy change:

$$\Delta H = \int_{T_1}^{T_2} C_p\,dT.$$

In many practical situations, $C_p$ is assumed to be constant over the temperature range, leading to the simplified relation $\Delta H = C_p\,(T_2 - T_1)$.

The entropy change for a reversible process can be derived from the definition of entropy. For a system undergoing a temperature change at constant pressure, the differential of entropy is

$$dS = \frac{C_p}{T}\,dT.$$

Integrating from $T_1$ to $T_2$ yields

$$\Delta S = \int_{T_1}^{T_2}\frac{C_p}{T}\,dT.$$

If $C_p$ is constant, the integral evaluates to $\Delta S = C_p\,\ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)$.

We now derive the ideal gas law from kinetic theory. Consider a gas of $N$ molecules in a volume $V$ at temperature $T$. The average translational kinetic energy per molecule is $\frac{3}{2}k_B T$, where $k_B$ is Boltzmann’s constant. The pressure exerted by the gas is related to the momentum transfer upon collision with the walls. Summing over all molecules and using the definition of Avogadro’s number $N_A$, we obtain the macroscopic relation

$$PV = nRT,$$

where $n = N/N_A$ is the number of moles and $R = N_A k_B$ is the universal gas constant.

For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, the work done is $W = -nRT \ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)$. The heat exchanged equals the work done, $\delta Q = -W$, leading to the isothermal work expression used in derivations of the Van’t Hoff equation.

The Van’t Hoff equation relates the temperature dependence of the equilibrium constant $K$ to the standard enthalpy change $\Delta H^\circ$. Starting from the fundamental relation $\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K$ and using $\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ$, we write

$$-RT \ln K = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ.$$

Rearranging gives

$$\ln K = -\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT} + \frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R}.$$
