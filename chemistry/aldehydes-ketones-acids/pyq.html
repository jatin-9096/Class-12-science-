Case Study 1: Rate of Decomposition of Hydrogen Peroxide in a Household Setting

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is widely used as a mild antiseptic and oxidizing agent in household cleaning. Its decomposition into water and oxygen is a classic example of a homogeneous reaction that can be catalysed by various substances. In this case study, you will analyse the kinetics of the decomposition reaction in a real‑life scenario where a small amount of manganese dioxide (MnO₂) is added to a 3 % solution of H₂O₂. The reaction proceeds as follows:

$$\ce{2 H2O2 (aq) -> 2 H2O (l) + O2 (g)}$$

A 100 mL sample of the 3 % H₂O₂ solution is placed in a sealed glass container equipped with a gas burette. After adding 0.1 g of MnO₂, the volume of oxygen evolved is recorded every 30 s for 10 min. The recorded data are shown in the table below:

The reaction is known to follow a second‑order rate law with respect to H₂O₂. Using the data above, answer the following questions:

  1. Plot the concentration of H₂O₂ versus time and determine the initial concentration of H₂O₂ in the solution.
  2. Using the second‑order integrated rate law, calculate the rate constant k for the decomposition reaction.
  3. Discuss how the presence of MnO₂ influences the rate constant compared to the uncatalysed decomposition of H₂O₂.
  4. Predict the time required for the reaction to reach 90 % completion and explain the assumptions made in your calculation.

Solution

1. Concentration vs. Time Plot and Initial Concentration

The volume of O₂ evolved is directly proportional to the amount of H₂O₂ decomposed because each mole of H₂O₂ produces 0.5 mol of O₂. Using the ideal gas law at room temperature (25 °C) and 1 atm, the molar volume of a gas is approximately 24.45 L mol⁻¹. Therefore, the number of moles of O₂ evolved at time t is:

$$n_{\ce{O2}}(t) = \frac{V_{\ce{O2}}(t)}{24.45}$$

The initial concentration of H₂O₂, [H₂O₂]₀, is given by the concentration of the 3 % solution. A 3 % (w/w) solution contains 3 g of H₂O₂ per 100 g of solution. The density of the solution is approximately 1.01 g mL⁻¹, so 100 mL of solution has a mass of 101 g. Hence:

$$\text{Mass of H₂O₂} = 0.03 \times 101\,\text{g} = 3.03\,\text{g}$$

Molar mass of H₂O₂ = 34.01 g mol⁻¹, thus the initial number of moles of H₂O₂ in 100 mL is:

$$n_{\ce{H2O2}}^0 = \frac{3.03}{34.01} = 0.0891\,\text{mol}$$

The initial concentration is:

$$[\ce{H2O2}]_0 = \frac{0.0891}{0.1\,\text{L}} = 0.891\,\text{M}$$

To plot concentration vs. time, we calculate the remaining concentration at each time point:

$$[\ce{H2O2}](t) = [\ce{H2O2}]_0 - \frac{n_{\ce{O2}}(t)}{0.5}$$

For example, at t = 60 s, V_O₂ = 24.3 mL, so n_O₂ = 24.3/24.45 = 0.994 mol. The amount of H₂O₂ decomposed is 0.994 mol / 0.5 = 1.988 mol, which is impossible because the initial moles are only 0.0891. This indicates that the assumption of 24.45 L mol⁻¹ is not valid for the small volumes measured; instead, we use the proportionality that 1 mL of O₂ corresponds to 1 mL / 24.45 L = 0.0000409 mol. Thus the moles of O₂ at 60 s is:

$$n_{\ce{O2}}(60) = \frac{24.3\,\text{mL}}{24.45\,\text{L}} = \frac{24.3}{24450} = 9.94\times10^{-4}\,\text{mol}$$

The amount of H₂O₂ decomposed is 9.94×10⁻⁴ mol / 0.5 = 1.99×10⁻³ mol. Thus the remaining concentration is:

$$[\ce{H2O2}](60) = \frac{0.0891 - 0.00199}{0.1} = 0.873\,\text{M}$$

Repeating this calculation for all time points yields the concentration vs. time data. A plot of [H₂O₂] vs. t will show a gradual decrease, confirming the second‑order nature of the reaction.

2. Rate Constant Calculation

For a second‑order reaction with respect to H₂O₂, the integrated rate law is:

$$\frac{1}{[\ce{H2O2}](t)} = \frac{1}{[\ce{H2O2}]_0} + k\,t$$

Using the concentration values at t = 0 s and t = 300 s, we have:

$$\frac{1}{0.891} = 1.122 + k \times 0$$

$$\frac{1}{0.873} = 1.146 + k \times 300$$

Solving for k:

$$k = \frac{\frac{1}{0.873} - \frac{1}{0.891}}{300} = \frac{1.146 - 1.122}{300} = \frac{0.024}{300} = 8.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$$

A more accurate value can be obtained by linear regression of 1/[H₂O₂] vs. t for all data points, yielding k ≈ 8.2×10⁻⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹.

3. Effect of MnO₂

In the absence of a catalyst, the decomposition of H₂O₂ is extremely slow (k ≈ 10⁻⁸ M⁻¹ s⁻¹). The addition of MnO₂ provides an alternate pathway with a lower activation energy, thereby increasing the rate constant by several orders of magnitude. The measured k of 8.2×10⁻⁵ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ reflects the catalytic efficiency of MnO₂ under the experimental conditions.

4. Time to 90 % Completion

90 % completion means 10 % of the initial concentration remains:

$$[\ce{H2O2}]_{90\%} = 0.1 \times [\ce{H2O2}]_0 = 0.0891 \times 0.1 = 0.00891\,\text{M}$$

Using the integrated rate law:

$$\frac{1}{0.00891} = \frac{1}{0.891} + k\,t_{90}$$

$$112.3 = 1.122 + 8.2\times10^{-5}\,t_{90}$$

$$t_{90} = \frac{112.3 - 1.122}{8.2\times10^{-5}} \approx 1.37\times10^{6}\,\text{s} \approx 16\,\text{days}$$

This long time indicates that even with catalysis, complete decomposition is slow at the given concentration. The calculation assumes a constant rate constant and no change in catalyst activity over time, which may not hold in practice due to catalyst deactivation or changes in temperature.
