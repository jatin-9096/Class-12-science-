1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Chemical Bonding: Definitions, Laws, Intuition, Exceptions, Worked Example

Chemical bonding is the fundamental interaction that holds atoms together to form molecules and solids. The classical Lewis model introduces the concept of valence electrons and octet rule, explaining covalent, ionic, and metallic bonds in terms of electron sharing or transfer. In covalent bonding, two atoms share a pair of electrons, forming a sigma bond; additional pi bonds arise from the overlap of p orbitals in double and triple bonds. Ionic bonding is described by the electrostatic attraction between cations and anions, often represented by the formula $AB \rightarrow A^{+} + B^{-}$, where the lattice energy stabilises the crystal structure. Metallic bonding involves a delocalised “sea of electrons” that provides high electrical conductivity and malleability. The quantum mechanical view refines these concepts by introducing molecular orbitals (MOs) formed from linear combinations of atomic orbitals (LCAO). Bonding and antibonding MOs determine bond order, with a higher bond order indicating a stronger, shorter bond. The electronegativity difference between atoms, quantified by Pauling’s scale, predicts bond polarity: a difference greater than 1.7 indicates ionic character, while a smaller difference indicates covalent character. Exceptions to the octet rule are abundant: boron trifluoride ($BF_3$) exhibits a 6‑electron configuration, while sulfur hexafluoride ($SF_6$) exceeds the octet with 12 valence electrons. Hypervalency is explained by d‑orbital participation or expanded valence shells. A worked example: consider the formation of water. Oxygen has six valence electrons and requires two more to complete its octet. Hydrogen has one valence electron and needs one more. Two O–H covalent bonds form, each sharing a pair of electrons, resulting in the bent geometry of $H_2O$ with a bond angle of approximately 104.5°. The bond dissociation energy for O–H is about 460 kJ mol⁻¹, illustrating the strength of covalent bonds in small molecules. This example demonstrates how electron counting, electronegativity, and molecular geometry converge to explain the stability and properties of a ubiquitous compound.

Chemistry · Core Law
Thermodynamics: Laws, Intuition, Exceptions, Worked Example

Class 12 thermodynamics revolves around two fundamental laws. The first law, the principle of conservation of energy, states that the change in internal energy of a system equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system: $$\Delta U = Q - W.$$ The second law introduces entropy, asserting that the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases; it can only remain constant or increase. This law explains the directionality of spontaneous processes. The Gibbs free energy, defined as $$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S,$$ combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure: a negative $\Delta G$ indicates a spontaneous reaction. Enthalpy changes are measured in calorimetry, while entropy changes are inferred from the disorder of the system. Intuitively, chemical reactions tend to move toward states with higher probability, reflected in increased entropy. Exceptions arise in systems with strong ordering forces, such as crystallisation, where entropy decreases but the enthalpy change is sufficiently negative to drive the process. A worked example: consider the dissolution of ammonium nitrate in water. The reaction is endothermic ($\Delta H > 0$) but highly exothermic in terms of entropy ($\Delta S > 0$) due to the increased randomness of ions in solution. At room temperature, the positive entropy term outweighs the enthalpy term, resulting in a negative $\Delta G$ and a spontaneous dissolution. The temperature dependence of $\Delta G$ can be illustrated by plotting $\Delta G$ versus $T$, showing the point where the curve crosses zero, indicating the equilibrium temperature. This example underscores how thermodynamic parameters govern real-world processes such as refrigeration, battery operation, and biological metabolism.

Chemistry · Core Law
Electrochemistry: Galvanic Cells and Electrochemical Laws

Electrochemistry bridges chemical reactions and electrical energy. A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts spontaneous redox reactions into electrical work. The cell potential, $E_{\text{cell}}$, is the difference between the cathode and anode potentials: $$E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}.$$ Standard electrode potentials, tabulated for half‑reactions, allow prediction of cell behaviour. The Nernst equation refines these potentials under non‑standard conditions: $$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q,$$ where $Q$ is the reaction quotient. The direction of electron flow is from the anode (oxidation) to the cathode (reduction). The cell’s ability to do work is quantified by the Gibbs free energy: $$\Delta G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}.$$ A key law is the Faraday’s law of electrolysis, relating the mass of substance deposited or liberated to the charge passed: $$m = \frac{QzM}{F},$$ where $z$ is the valency, $M$ the molar mass, and $F$ the Faraday constant. Exceptions to ideal behaviour occur due to overpotentials, concentration gradients, and temperature variations, which reduce the observed cell voltage. A worked example: a Daniell cell comprising $\ce{Zn|Zn^{2+}}$ and $\ce{Cu^{2+}|Cu}$ has standard potentials $E^{\circ}_{\text{Zn}} = -0.76$ V and $E^{\circ}_{\text{Cu}} = +0.34$ V, giving $E^{\circ}_{\text{cell}} = 1.10$ V. If the $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ concentration is $0.01$ M and $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ is $1$ M at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$, the Nernst equation yields $E_{\text{cell}} \approx 1.10 - \frac{0.0592}{2}\log\frac{0.01}{1} \approx 1.20$ V. This demonstrates how concentration changes influence cell potential and highlights the practical importance of electrode design in batteries and electroplating.

Chemistry · Core Law
Organic Chemistry: Functional Groups and Their Properties

Functional groups are the structural motifs that determine the chemical behaviour of organic molecules. The simplest groups include alkanes ($\ce{C–C}$ single bonds), alkenes ($\ce{C=C}$ double bonds), alkynes ($\ce{C≡C}$ triple bonds), alcohols ($\ce{–OH}$), ethers ($\ce{–O–}$), ketones ($\ce{C=O}$), aldehydes ($\ce{R–CHO}$), carboxylic acids ($\ce{–COOH}$), amides ($\ce{–CONH_2}$), and amines ($\ce{–NH_2}$). Each group imparts distinct reactivity: for instance, the carbonyl group in ketones and aldehydes is electrophilic, undergoing nucleophilic addition, whereas alkenes undergo electrophilic addition via a carbocation intermediate. The acidity of carboxylic acids ($pK_a \approx 4.8$) contrasts sharply with the basicity of amines ($pK_b \approx 5.5$). Resonance stabilization in aromatic systems, described by Hückel’s rule ($4n+2$ π electrons), confers exceptional stability and unique reactivity patterns such as electrophilic aromatic substitution. Exceptions to these general rules arise in strained rings (cyclopropane), heteroaromatic systems (pyridine), and in the presence of electron-withdrawing or donating substituents that alter electronic density. A worked example: the synthesis of acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) from salicylic acid involves esterification with acetic anhydride. The mechanism proceeds via protonation of the carbonyl oxygen, nucleophilic attack by the phenolic oxygen, and elimination of acetic acid. The reaction is facilitated by an acid catalyst (pyridine) and proceeds with a yield of about 80 %. This example illustrates how functional group transformations, acid–base equilibria, and reaction mechanisms interrelate to produce pharmaceutically relevant compounds.

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In this section we present a systematic derivation of the most frequently used equations in CBSE Class‑12 Chemistry. Each derivation is broken into clear, logical steps, followed by a concise formula list that specifies the variables, their SI units, and the conditions under which the equation holds. At the end of the article, a set of practice questions is provided to reinforce understanding.

1. Rate Law for Elementary Reactions

For an elementary reaction of the form

$$A + B \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products,$$

the rate law is obtained directly from the stoichiometry of the elementary step. The collision theory tells us that the frequency of successful collisions is proportional to the probability of finding an A molecule and a B molecule simultaneously in the reactive volume. This probability is proportional to the product of their concentrations.

Step‑by‑step:

  1. Define the reaction rate as the change in concentration of a reactant per unit time: $$r = -\frac{1}{\nu_A}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\nu_B}\frac{d[B]}{dt},$$ where $\nu_A$ and $\nu_B$ are stoichiometric coefficients.
  2. For an elementary step, the probability of collision between A and B is proportional to $[A][B]$. Thus, $$r = k[A][B].$$
  3. Generalize to an elementary reaction with arbitrary stoichiometry: $$A_m + B_n \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products,$$ giving $$r = k[A]^m[B]^n.$$

Hence, the rate law for an elementary reaction is simply the product of concentrations raised to the powers equal to their stoichiometric coefficients.

2. Equilibrium Constant from Gibbs Free Energy

At equilibrium, the Gibbs free energy change of the reaction is zero: $$\Delta G = 0.$$ The relationship between $\Delta G$ and the reaction quotient $Q$ is

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q.$$ Setting $\Delta G = 0$ gives

$$0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln K,$$

where $K$ is the equilibrium constant. Solving for $K$:

  1. Rearrange: $$RT\ln K = -\Delta G^\circ.$$
  2. Divide by $RT$: $$\ln K = -\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT}.$$
  3. Exponentiate both sides: $$K = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT}\right).$$

Thus, the equilibrium constant is directly related to the standard Gibbs free energy change.

3. Nernst Equation for Electrochemical Cells

The cell potential $E$ under non‑standard conditions is related to the standard potential $E^\circ$ by the Nernst equation. Starting from the definition of Gibbs free energy change for a cell:

$$\Delta G = -nFE,$$

and the relation between $\Delta G$ and the reaction quotient $Q$:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q,$$

we combine them:

  1. Substitute $\Delta G$ from the first equation into the second: $$-nFE = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q.$$
  2. Express $\Delta G^\circ$ in terms of $E^\circ$: $$\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ.$$
  3. Insert this into the equation: $$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q.$$
  4. Divide by $-nF$: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q.$$
  5. For a reaction $\nu_1A_1 + \nu_2A_2 \rightarrow \nu_3A_3 + \nu_4A_4$, the reaction quotient is $$Q = \frac{[A_3]^{\nu_3}[A_4]^{\nu_4}}{[A_1]^{\nu_1}[A_2]^{\nu_2}}.$$

At 25 °C, $RT/F \approx 0.0257$ V, so the Nernst equation simplifies to

$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log_{10}Q.$$

4. Beer–Lambert Law

The absorbance $A$ of a solution is proportional to the concentration $c$ of absorbing species and the path length $l$:

$$A = \varepsilon\,c\,l,$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the molar absorptivity. Derivation:

  1. Consider a thin slice of thickness $dx$ in a cuvette of total length $l$. The differential change in intensity $I$ is given by Beer's differential equation: $$\frac{dI}{I} = -\alpha\,dx,$$ where $\alpha$ is the absorption coefficient.
  2. Integrate from $x=0$ (incident intensity $I_0$) to $x=l$ (transmitted intensity $I$): $$\int_{I_0}^{I}\frac{dI}{I} = -\alpha\int_{0}^{l}dx.$$ This yields $$\ln\frac{I}{I_0} = -\alpha l.$$
  3. Define absorbance as $$A = -\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0} = \frac{\ln(I_0/I)}{\ln 10}.$$ Substituting the integrated result: $$A = \frac{\alpha l}{\ln 10}.$$
  4. Relate $\alpha$ to concentration: $$\alpha = \varepsilon\,c\,\ln 10.$$ Substituting gives the Beer–Lambert law.

5. Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation

For a weak acid HA dissociating as $$\mathrm{HA}\;\rightleftharpoons\;\mathrm{H^+}+\mathrm{A^-},$$ the acid dissociation constant is

$$K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}. $$

Taking the negative logarithm of both sides:

  1. $$-\log K_a = -\log[\mathrm{H^+}] - \log\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}. $$
  2. Recognize that $-\log[\mathrm{H^+}] = \mathrm{pH}$ and $-\log K_a = \mathrm{p}K_a$:
  3. Thus, $$\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}. $$

This is the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, widely used in buffer calculations.

6. van’t Hoff Equation

The van’t Hoff equation relates the temperature dependence of the equilibrium constant $K$ to the standard enthalpy change $\Delta H^\circ$:

$$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT^2}.$$

Derivation:

  1. Start from the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation: $$\Delta G^\circ = \Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ.$$
  2. Express $\Delta G^\circ$ in terms of $K$: $$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K.$$
  3. Differentiate both sides with respect to $T$:
  4. Left side: $$\frac{d}{dT}(-RT\ln K) = -R\ln K - RT\frac{1}{K}\frac{dK}{dT}.$$
  5. Right side: $$\frac{d}{dT}(\Delta H^\circ - T\Delta S^\circ) = -\Delta S^\circ.$$
  6. Rearrange to isolate $\frac{d\ln K}{dT}$:
  7. Using $\Delta S^\circ = (\Delta H^\circ - \Delta G^\circ)/T$, substitute and simplify to obtain the van’t Hoff equation.
