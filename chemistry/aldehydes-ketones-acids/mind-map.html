1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Covalent Bonding and Molecular Orbital Theory

Covalent bonding arises when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons to achieve a stable electronic configuration. The simplest representation is the Lewis dot structure, where each dot corresponds to an electron and shared pairs are depicted as lines. The octet rule, a useful guideline for main‑group elements, states that atoms tend to form bonds until they have eight electrons in their valence shell. However, exceptions such as the 18‑electron rule for transition metals and the 4‑electron rule for boranes must be remembered. VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) theory predicts the geometry of a molecule by minimizing repulsions between electron pairs; for example, $\ce{H2O}$ adopts a bent shape with an H–O–H angle of 104.5°, while $\ce{CO2}$ is linear due to the arrangement of two double bonds. Hybridization explains the mixing of atomic orbitals to form hybrid orbitals that accommodate bonding. In $\ce{CH4}$, sp³ hybridization of carbon yields four equivalent σ bonds, whereas in $\ce{CCl4}$, sp³ hybridization of chlorine leads to tetrahedral geometry. The distinction between σ and π bonds is crucial: σ bonds involve head‑to‑head overlap and are stronger, while π bonds arise from side‑to‑side overlap of p orbitals and are weaker, influencing reactivity. Molecular Orbital (MO) theory provides a more accurate description by combining atomic orbitals to form bonding ($\sigma$, $\pi$) and antibonding ($\sigma^*$, $\pi^*$) molecular orbitals. The bond order, calculated as $BO = \frac{N_b - N_a}{2}$, where $N_b$ and $N_a$ are the numbers of electrons in bonding and antibonding orbitals respectively, predicts bond strength and length. For instance, $\ce{O2}$ has a bond order of 2, corresponding to a double bond, whereas $\ce{N2}$ has a bond order of 3, explaining its high bond dissociation energy. Exceptions such as $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$, and $\ce{F2}$ illustrate the limits of the octet rule and the importance of MO theory. A worked example: in $\ce{H2O}$, the oxygen atom uses sp³ hybridization to form two σ bonds with hydrogen and two lone pairs; the resulting electron pair geometry is tetrahedral, but the molecular geometry is bent due to lone pair repulsion. This comprehensive understanding of covalent bonding, from Lewis structures to MO diagrams, equips students to predict molecular shapes, bond strengths, and reactivity patterns in organic and inorganic chemistry.

Chemistry · Core Law
Ionic Bonding, Crystal Lattice Energy and Solubility Rules

Ionic bonding occurs when a metal donates one or more electrons to a non‑metal, resulting in the formation of cations and anions that are held together by electrostatic attraction. The strength of this interaction is quantified by the lattice energy, $U$, the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form a crystalline solid. A simplified expression for lattice energy is $U = \frac{N_A z^+ z^- e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r_0} \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)$, where $z^+$ and $z^-$ are the charges of the ions, $r_0$ is the interionic distance, $n$ is the Born exponent, and $N_A$ is Avogadro’s number. Factors that increase lattice energy include higher ionic charges and smaller ionic radii, which lead to stronger Coulombic attraction. The Born–Haber cycle uses lattice energy along with other thermodynamic quantities—ionization energy, electron affinity, and lattice enthalpy—to calculate the overall lattice enthalpy of formation. Solubility rules, derived from empirical observations, predict whether an ionic compound will dissolve in water. For example, most nitrates, acetates, and alkali metal salts are soluble, whereas most sulfates, chlorides, and hydroxides are insoluble, with notable exceptions such as $\ce{AgCl}$ and $\ce{PbSO4}$. A worked example: the dissolution of $\ce{NaCl}$ in water is driven by the hydration energy of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$, which outweighs the lattice energy, making the process exothermic and favorable. Conversely, $\ce{CaCO3}$ has a high lattice energy and low hydration energy, rendering it insoluble. Understanding ionic bonding, lattice energy, and solubility rules is essential for predicting the behavior of salts in aqueous solutions, designing precipitation reactions, and interpreting the outcomes of analytical procedures such as qualitative inorganic analysis.

Introduction
In CBSE Class 12 Chemistry the master equation bank is a collection of fundamental relationships that connect the kinetic, thermodynamic, and equilibrium aspects of chemical reactions. The derivations below follow the standard textbook approach used in the CBSE curriculum, with each step justified by either collision theory, transition state theory, or basic algebraic manipulation. All mathematical expressions are rendered in LaTeX for clarity. The content is organised into major thematic blocks: rate laws, integrated rate equations, Arrhenius and transition state equations, thermodynamic relations, equilibrium constants, and special reaction mechanisms. At the end a concise formula list summarises each equation with its variables, SI units, and applicable conditions. The entire discussion is written in a single paragraph block, as required, with line breaks inserted for readability. 1. Rate Law and Reaction Order
The instantaneous rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. For a general reaction $$ aA + bB \rightarrow cC + dD, $$ the rate can be expressed as $$ \text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{a}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\frac{1}{b}\frac{d[B]}{dt} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{d[C]}{dt} = \frac{1}{d}\frac{d[D]}{dt}. $$ Collision theory postulates that the rate is proportional to the frequency of effective collisions between reacting species. Thus the rate law takes the form $$ \text{Rate} = k [A]^m [B]^n, $$ where $k$ is the rate constant, $m$ and $n$ are the reaction orders with respect to $A$ and $B$ respectively. The overall order is $m+n$. The values of $m$ and $n$ are determined experimentally by measuring the rate at different concentrations and applying the method of initial rates. For a unimolecular reaction the order is typically one, whereas for a bimolecular reaction it can be zero, one, or two depending on the mechanism. The rate constant $k$ has units that depend on the overall order: for a first‑order reaction $k$ has units $s^{-1}$, for a second‑order reaction $k$ has units $M^{-1}s^{-1}$, and for a zero‑order reaction $k$ has units $M s^{-1}$.

2. Integrated Rate Equations
The integrated rate equations relate the concentration of a reactant to time for reactions of a given order. They are obtained by separating variables and integrating. For a zero‑order reaction: $$ \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k \quad \Rightarrow \quad [A] = [A]_0 - kt. $$ For a first‑order reaction: $$ \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k[A] \quad \Rightarrow \quad \ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt \quad \Rightarrow \quad [A] = [A]_0 e^{-kt}. $$ For a second‑order reaction: $$ \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k[A]^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{1}{[A]} = \frac{1}{[A]_0} + kt. $$ These equations are used to determine the rate constant from experimental concentration–time data. The linear forms of the equations are often plotted: $[A]$ vs $t$ for zero order, $\ln[A]$ vs $t$ for first order, and $1/[A]$ vs $t$ for second order. A straight line indicates that the assumed order is correct.

3. Half‑Life Expressions
The half‑life $t_{1/2}$ is the time required for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half its initial value. For a first‑order reaction: $$ t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}. $$ For a second‑order reaction: $$ t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[A]_0}. $$ For a zero‑order reaction: $$ t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}. $$ These expressions are useful for comparing the relative speeds of reactions of different orders.

4. Arrhenius Equation
The temperature dependence of the rate constant is described by the Arrhenius equation. Starting from collision theory, the rate constant is proportional to the number of collisions per unit time and the fraction of collisions that have sufficient energy to overcome the activation barrier $E_a$. This leads to: $$ k = A e^{-E_a/(RT)}, $$ where $A$ is the pre‑exponential factor (frequency factor), $R$ is the universal gas constant, and $T$ is the absolute temperature. Taking natural logarithms yields the linear form: $$ \ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\frac{1}{T}. $$ A plot of $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ gives a straight line with slope $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$. The activation energy $E_a$ can thus be determined experimentally. The pre‑exponential factor $A$ is related to the collision frequency and the steric factor, which accounts for the orientation of molecules during collision.

5. Transition State Theory (Eyring Equation)
While collision theory provides a qualitative picture, transition state theory offers a more quantitative description of the rate constant in terms of thermodynamic parameters. The key idea is that reactants must form a transient transition state (activated complex) before proceeding to products. The rate constant is given by: $$ k = \frac{k_B T}{h} e^{-\Delta G^\ddagger/(RT)}, $$ where $k_B$ is Boltzmann’s constant, $h$ is Planck’s constant, and $\Delta G^\ddagger$ is the Gibbs free energy of activation. Expanding $\Delta G^\ddagger$ using $\Delta G^\ddagger = \Delta H^\ddagger - T\Delta S^\ddagger$ gives: $$ k = \frac{k_B T}{h} e^{\Delta S^\ddagger/R} e^{-\Delta H^\ddagger/(RT)}. $$ Thus the rate constant depends exponentially on the enthalpy of activation and also on the entropy of activation. The pre‑exponential factor in the Arrhenius equation can be related to the entropy term: $A = (k_B T/h) e^{\Delta S^\ddagger/R}$.

6. Thermodynamic Relations
The Gibbs free energy change of a reaction is related to the equilibrium constant $K$ by: $$ \Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K. $$
