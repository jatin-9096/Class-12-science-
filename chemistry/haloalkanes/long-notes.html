Nomenclature and classification of haloalkanes and haloarenes

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are organic compounds containing a carbon‑halogen bond of the type $R\!\! -\!X$ where $R$ is an alkyl group and $X$ = $F,\,Cl,\,Br,\,I$. Haloarenes (aryl halides) have the halogen directly attached to an aromatic ring ($Ar\!\! -\!X$).

Classification:

  • Based on the hybridisation of the carbon bearing $X$: $sp^3$ (saturated), $sp^2$ (allylic/vinylic), $sp$ (acetylenic).
  • According to the number of halogen atoms: monohalo, dihalo, polyhalo.
  • According to the nature of $X$: primary, secondary, tertiary (for haloalkanes).

Key Nomenclature Rules (IUPAC):

  1. Identify the longest carbon chain containing the carbon attached to $X$; this becomes the parent hydrocarbon.
  2. Number the chain to give the carbon bearing $X$ the lowest possible locant.
  3. Replace the terminal “‑e” of the parent name with “‑halo” (e.g., $\text{propane} \rightarrow \text{chloropropane}$).
  4. If more than one halogen is present, list them alphabetically with their locants (e.g., $1\!\! -\!bromo\!\! -\!2\!\! -\!chloro\!\! -\!propane$).
  5. For haloarenes, the aromatic ring is the parent; the halogen is indicated as a prefix (e.g., $\text{bromobenzene}$).

Physical Data (Typical):

TypeGeneral FormulaExampleBoiling Point ($^\circ$C)
Alkyl halide$R\!\! -\!X$CH$_3$CH$_2$Cl12.3
Aryl halide$Ar\!\! -\!X$C$_6$H$_5$Br156

Solved Example:

Find the IUPAC name of $\text{CH}_3\!\! -\!\text{CH}_2\!\! -\!\text{CH}_2\!\! -\!\text{Cl}$.

  1. Longest chain = 3 carbon atoms → parent = $\text{propane}$.
  2. Number from the end nearest the halogen → chlorine on carbon‑1.
  3. Replace “‑e” with “‑chloro” and add locant: $1\!\! -\!\text{chloropropane}$.

Methods of preparation of haloalkanes

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are organic compounds in which one hydrogen atom of an alkane is replaced by a halogen ($\\mathrm{X = F, Cl, Br, I}$).

Methods of preparation

MethodReagents / Conditions
Addition to alkenesAlkene + $\\mathrm{HX}$ (room temp) → haloalkane; $\\mathrm{X_2}$ (UV) → vicinal dihalide; $\\mathrm{X_2/H_2O}$ → halohydrin then $\\mathrm{NaOH}$ → haloalkane
From alcohols$\\mathrm{R-OH + HX}$ (conc., $\\ge 60^{\circ}C$) or $\\mathrm{SOCl_2}$, $\\mathrm{PCl_5}$, $\\mathrm{PCl_3}$ (0–25°C) → $\\mathrm{R-X}$ + $\\mathrm{HCl}$
From alkyl sulfonates$\\mathrm{R-OSO_2R'} + NaX$ (dry $\\mathrm{Acetone}$, reflux) → $\\mathrm{R-X}$ + $\\mathrm{NaOSO_2R'}$ (Finkelstein reaction)
From carboxylic acids$\\mathrm{R-COOH}$ + $\\mathrm{PBr_3}$ → $\\mathrm{R-Br}$ + $\\mathrm{CO_2}$ (decarboxylative bromination)

Key points

  • Primary alkyl halides are best obtained via $\\mathrm{S_N2}$ pathways (e.g., $\\mathrm{SOCl_2}$).
  • Secondary/tertiary halides often arise from $\\mathrm{S_N1}$ conditions (e.g., $\\mathrm{PCl_5}$).
  • Reaction rate for $\\mathrm{S_N2}$: $\\mathrm{Rate = k[RX][Y^-]}$ (second order).
  • Temperature is expressed in $\\mathrm{^{\circ}C}$; pressure in $\\mathrm{atm}$.

Solved example

Prepare $\\mathrm{1\text{-}chloropropane}$ from $\\mathrm{1\text{-}propanol}$ using thionyl chloride.

  1. Write the reaction:
    $\\mathrm{CH_3CH_2CH_2OH + SOCl_2 \xrightarrow{0-25^{\circ}C} CH_3CH_2CH_2Cl + SO_2 \uparrow + HCl \uparrow}$
  2. Mechanism: $\\mathrm{S_N2}$ – $\\mathrm{Cl^-}$ attacks the carbon bearing the –OH while $\\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ converts –OH to a good leaving group ($\\mathrm{OSOCl}$).
  3. Conditions: Add $\\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ dropwise to chilled $\\mathrm{1\text{-}propanol}$, stir 30 min, then distill the product (bp $\\approx 46^{\circ}C$).
  4. Yield calculation (if 10 g $\\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ used, excess): theoretical $m_{product}=\frac{10\,g}{119.0\,g\,mol^{-1}}\times 78.5\,g\,mol^{-1}=6.6\,g$; actual 5.5 g → % yield $=\frac{5.5}{6.6}\times100=83\%$.

Physical properties of haloalkanes and haloarenes

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are organic compounds containing a halogen atom (F, Cl, Br, I) covalently bonded to a saturated carbon atom. Haloarenes (aryl halides) have the halogen attached to an aromatic carbon.

Physical properties and trends:

  • Boiling point ($T_b$) and melting point ($T_m$) increase with molecular weight and with the size of the halogen (F<Cl<Br<I).
  • Polarity of C–X bond ($\mu$) follows $\\text{C–F}>\\text{C–Cl}>\\text{C–Br}>\\text{C–I}$, influencing dipole moment (unit: Debye, D) and intermolecular forces.
  • Density ($\rho$) of haloalkanes is higher than corresponding alkanes; typical units $kg\\,m^{-3}$.
  • Solubility in water decreases from fluorides to iodides; hydrogen bonding possible only for C–F.
  • Refractive index ($n_D$) increases with atomic mass; dimensionless.

Representative boiling points (°C):

Compound$T_b$ (°C)
Methyl fluoride$-78$
Methyl chloride$-24$
Methyl bromide$3.6$
Methyl iodide$42$

Key colligative formula (boiling‑point elevation): $$\\Delta T_b = i\\,K_b\\,m$$ where $i$ = van’t Hoff factor, $K_b$ = ebullioscopic constant (°C·kg·mol$^{-1}$), $m$ = molality (mol·kg$^{-1}$).

Solved example: A $1\\,\\text{m}$ aqueous solution of chloroethane ($\\text{C}_2\\text{H}_5\\text{Cl}$) dissociates into $\\text{C}_2\\text{H}_5^+$ and $\\text{Cl}^-$, so $i=2$. For water $K_b=0.512\\,^{\\circ}\\text{C\\,kg\\,mol}^{-1}$. Calculate $\\Delta T_b$.

Solution: $$\\Delta T_b = i K_b m = 2 \times 0.512 \times 1 = 1.024\\,^{\\circ}\\text{C}$$. Hence the solution boils at $100 + 1.024 = 101.024\\,^{\\circ}\\text{C}$.

Nucleophilic substitution reactions (SN1 and SN2)

Definitions

  • SN1 (Unimolecular) substitution: A two‑step mechanism where the rate‑determining step is the formation of a carbocation; overall rate depends only on the substrate concentration.
  • SN2 (Bimolecular) substitution: A single concerted step where the nucleophile attacks the substrate from the backside, leading to inversion of configuration; rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile.

Rate Laws & Kinetic Order

  • SN1: $\text{Rate}=k[RX]$  (first order, $\text{s}^{-1}$).
  • SN2: $\text{Rate}=k[RX][Nu^-]$  (second order, $\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$).

Key Equations

  • Arrhenius: $k=A e^{-E_a/(RT)}$ where $A$ is the pre‑exponential factor, $E_a$ (kJ·mol$^{-1}$) is activation energy, $R=8.314\,\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $T$ (K).
  • Half‑life for SN1 (first order): $t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k}$.

Mechanistic Features

FeatureSN1SN2
Rate‑determining stepCarbocation formationBack‑side attack
OrderFirstSecond
Solvent effectPolar protic stabilises carbocationPolar aprotic favours nucleophile
StereochemistryRacemisation (planar carbocation)Inversion (Walden inversion)

Factors Influencing Rate

  • Substrate: tertiary > secondary > primary for SN1; primary > secondary > tertiary for SN2 (steric hindrance).
  • Nucleophile strength: strong bases (e.g., $\text{OH}^-$, $\text{CN}^-$) accelerate SN2.
  • Leaving group ability: $\text{I}^- > \text{Br}^- > \text{Cl}^- > \text{F}^-$.

Solved Example (SN2)

Given $k=2.5\times10^{-3}\,\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ at 298 K for the reaction $\text{CH}_3\text{Br}+\text{OH}^-\rightarrow\text{CH}_3\text{OH}+\text{Br}^-$. If $[\text{CH}_3\text{Br}]=0.010\,\text{M}$ and $[\text{OH}^-]=0.020\,\text{M}$, calculate the rate.

\[\text{Rate}=k[\text{CH}_3\text{Br}][\text{OH}^-]=2.5\times10^{-3}\times0.010\times0.020=5.0\times10^{-7}\,\text{M s}^{-1}\]

The reaction proceeds at $5.0\times10^{-7}\,\text{M s}^{-1}$ under the stated conditions.

Elimination reactions (E1 and E2)

Elimination Reactions (E1 & E2)

Elimination removes a leaving group (LG) and a β-hydrogen from adjacent carbon atoms, forming a C=C double bond.

E1 (Unimolecular Elimination)

  • Two-step mechanism: (i) ionisation – $R\text{–}LG \rightarrow R^{+} + LG^{-}$, (ii) deprotonation – $R^{+} + B^{-} \rightarrow Alkene + BH$.
  • Rate law: $rate = k[substrate]$ (first order).
  • Rate constant unit: $s^{-1}$.
  • Favoured by weak bases, polar protic solvents, and tertiary substrates.

E2 (Bimolecular Elimination)

  • Concerted one-step: $R\text{–}CH_{2}\text{–}CH_{2}\text{–}LG + B^{-} \rightarrow Alkene + LG^{-} + BH$.
  • Rate law: $rate = k[substrate][base]$ (second order).
  • Rate constant unit: $M^{-1}s^{-1}$.
  • Requires a strong base, polar aprotic solvent, and anti-periplanar geometry of H and LG.

Key Rules

  • Zaitsev’s rule: the more substituted alkene is preferred unless a bulky base is used.
  • Anti-periplanar arrangement is mandatory for E2; E1 proceeds via planar carbocation.
ReactionRate LawOrder
E1$rate = k[substrate]$1st
E2$rate = k[substrate][base]$2nd

Solved Example

Dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane with aqueous NaOH (25 °C).

  1. Substrate is tertiary → favours E2 with strong base NaOH.
  2. Anti-periplanar H is on a methyl group; removal gives isobutene:
  3. Overall equation: $$(CH_{3})_{3}C\text{–}Br + NaOH \rightarrow (CH_{3})_{2}C=CH_{2} + NaBr + H_{2}O$$

Rate expression: $rate = k[(CH_{3})_{3}C\text{–}Br][OH^{-}]$.

Reactions of haloarenes (electrophilic aromatic substitution and nucleophilic substitution)

Reactions of Haloarenes

Haloarenes undergo two characteristic aromatic substitutions:

  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS): substitution of a hydrogen atom by an electrophile. The aromatic ring retains its conjugated system.
  • Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution (SNAr): replacement of a halogen by a nucleophile via addition‑elimination (Meisenheimer complex) when the ring is activated by strong –NO₂, –CN, –COOR groups.

Key formulas

  • Arrhenius rate expression: $k = A\,e^{-E_a/(R\,T)}$ ( $k$ in $s^{-1}$ or $M^{-1}s^{-1}$, $E_a$ in $kJ\,mol^{-1}$, $R = 8.314\,J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}$ ).
  • Hammett equation for EAS: $\log\frac{k_X}{k_H}= \rho\,\sigma$ where $k_X$ is rate constant for substituted haloarene, $\sigma$ is substituent constant, $\rho$ is reaction constant.
  • SNAr rate (second order): $k = k_1[ArX][Nu^-]$ (units $M^{-1}s^{-1}$).
Substituent$\sigma$ (para)Effect on EAS
–NO₂$+0.78$deactivates
–CH₃$-0.17$activates

Typical mechanism (SNAr)

  1. Attack of $Nu^-$ on $C$ bearing $X$, forming a Meisenheimer σ‑complex.
  2. Departure of $X^-$ restoring aromaticity.

Solved Example

Calculate the relative rate of nitration of chlorobenzene compared to benzene using $\rho = -0.5$ and $\sigma_{p\text{-Cl}} = +0.23$.

Using Hammett: $\log\frac{k_{Cl}}{k_H}= \rho\,\sigma = (-0.5)(+0.23)= -0.115$ → $\frac{k_{Cl}}{k_H}=10^{-0.115}=0.77$. Thus chlorobenzene nitrates ~23 % slower than benzene.

Mechanistic aspects and factors affecting reaction rates

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes are organic compounds containing a carbon‑halogen bond. Their reaction rates depend on the mechanism (SN1, SN2, E1, E2) and several kinetic factors.

Rate Laws & Key Equations

MechanismRate LawRate Constant Expression
SN1$rate = k[RX]$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$
SN2$rate = k[RX][Nu^-]$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$
E1$rate = k[RX]$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$
E2$rate = k[RX][Base]$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$

Factors affecting rate

  • Substrate structure: Primary > secondary > tertiary for SN2; opposite for SN1/E1.
  • Nucleophile/Base strength: Strong, less‑sterically hindered nucleophiles accelerate SN2; strong bases favor E2.
  • Solvent: Polar protic stabilises ions → faster SN1/E1; polar aprotic stabilises nucleophile → faster SN2/E2.
  • Leaving group ability: $I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-$ (higher ability → lower $E_a$).
  • Temperature: $rate \propto e^{-E_a/RT}$; higher $T$ increases both $k$ and the proportion of elimination.

Solved Example

For the SN2 reaction $CH_3CH_2CH_2Br + I^- \rightarrow CH_3CH_2CH_2I + Br^-$ in acetone, $k = 2.5 \\times 10^3\;M^{-1}s^{-1}$ at $298\;K$. If $[RX]=0.010\;M$ and $[I^-]=0.020\;M$, calculate the initial rate.

Solution: $rate = k[RX][I^-] = (2.5 \\times 10^3)(0.010)(0.020) = 5.0 \\times 10^2\;M\,s^{-1}$.

Thus, a strong, unhindered nucleophile in a polar aprotic solvent gives a rapid SN2 substitution.

Industrial applications and environmental impact

Industrial Applications

  • Production of Vinyl chloride (precursor to PVC): $C_2H_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_3Cl + HCl$.
  • Synthesis of Chloroform and Carbon tetrachloride by chlorination of methane: $CH_4 + 3Cl_2 \rightarrow CCl_4 + 3HCl$.
  • Manufacture of Pharmaceutical intermediates such as chlorobenzene \rightarrow phenylalanine derivatives.
  • Preparation of Solvents (e.g., dichloromethane) via controlled chlorination of methanol.

Key Formula

ProcessBalanced Equation
Vinyl chloride$C_2H_4 + Cl_2 \rightarrow C_2H_3Cl + HCl$
Chloroform$CHCl_3 + H_2O \rightarrow CO_2 + 3HCl$ (hydrolysis)

Environmental Impact

  • Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) – bioaccumulate in food chain.
  • Release of HCl leads to acid rain; SI unit for concentration $[HCl]$ in $mol\,L^{-1}$.
  • Some haloarenes act as ozone‑depleting substances; ozone depletion potential (ODP) expressed relative to $CCl_4$.

Solved Example

Calculate mass of vinyl chloride produced when $5.0\ \text{mol}$ of ethylene reacts completely with excess $Cl_2$.

Given$n_{C_2H_4}=5.0\ \text{mol}$
Reaction stoichiometry$1\ mol\ C_2H_4 \rightarrow 1\ mol\ C_2H_3Cl$
Molar mass $M_{C_2H_3Cl}=62.5\ \text{g\ mol}^{-1}$
Mass $= n \times M$$m = 5.0 \times 62.5 = 312.5\ \text{g}$

Thus $312.5\ \text{g}$ of vinyl chloride is obtained.
