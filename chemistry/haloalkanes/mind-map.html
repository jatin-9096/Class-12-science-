Nomenclature and classification of haloalkanes and haloarenes

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are organic compounds containing at least one halogen atom (F, Cl, Br, I) covalently bonded to a saturated carbon atom. Haloarenes are aromatic analogues where the halogen is attached to an sp$^2$ carbon of the benzene ring.

  • Classification of haloalkanes (based on carbon bearing the halogen):
    TypeGeneral FormulaExample
    Primary$\text{R-CH}_2\text{-X}$1‑bromoethane
    Secondary$\text{R-CHX- R'}$2‑chloropropane
    Tertiary$\text{R-CX(R')_2}$2‑bromo‑2‑methylpropane
  • Classification of haloarenes: Monohaloarenes, di‑haloarenes, poly‑haloarenes; ortho, meta, para positional isomers.

Nomenclature Rules (IUPAC):

  • Identify the longest carbon chain containing the carbon bearing the halogen; number it to give the lowest possible locant to the halogen.
  • Use the prefix “halo‑” (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo) followed by the parent alkane name.
  • If more than one identical halogen is present, use di‑, tri‑, tetra‑ as prefixes and give separate locants (e.g., 1,2‑dichloroethane).
  • For haloarenes, the aromatic ring is the parent; the halogen is indicated by the same prefixes with the locant (e.g., 4‑bromophenol).

Key Formula (Moles of NaOH required for nucleophilic substitution): $n_{\text{NaOH}} = n_{\text{haloalkane}} \times \nu$, where $\nu$ is the stoichiometric coefficient (usually 1 for $\text{SN}2$).

Solved Example:

Calculate the mass of NaOH needed to completely react with $0.50\,\text{mol}$ of 2‑bromo‑2‑methylpropane in an $\text{SN}1$ reaction.

  1. Reaction: $\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{CBr} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{COH} + \text{NaBr}$
  2. From the equation, $\nu = 1$, so $n_{\text{NaOH}} = 0.50\,\text{mol}$.
  3. Molar mass of NaOH = $22.99 + 15.999 + 1.008 = 39.997\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$.
  4. Mass $= n \times M = 0.50\,\text{mol} \times 39.997\,\text{g mol}^{-1} = 20.0\,\text{g}$.

Thus, $20.0\,\text{g}$ of NaOH will completely convert $0.50\,\text{mol}$ of the haloalkane.

Methods of preparation of haloalkanes and haloarenes

Definitions

  • Haloalkane: An alkane in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by halogen atoms (Cl, Br, I, F).
  • Haloarene: An aromatic compound containing one or more halogen substituents on the benzene ring.

Key Methods of Preparation

MethodReagents / ConditionsMechanism
From alkenes (addition)$\text{HX}$ (or $\text{X}_2$) in presence of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$Electrophilic addition (Markovnikov)
From alcohols$\text{HX}$ (conc.) or $\text{PCl}_5$, $\text{SOCl}_2$; $\text{ZnCl}_2$ (Lucas reagent)SN1 (tertiary) / SN2 (primary)
From alkyl halides (halogen exchange)Na$\text{X}$ in acetone (Finkelstein); Cu$\text{X}$, NaCN (nucleophilic substitution)SN2
From carboxylic acidsHBr, $\text{PBr}_3$ (decarboxylation)Radical decarboxylation
From Grignard reagentsR$\text{MgX}$ + $\text{RX}$ → R–R' + MgX$_2$Coupling
Haloarenes – electrophilic aromatic substitution$\text{X}_2$ + FeCl$_3$ (Cl), AlCl$_3$ (Br)σ‑complex formation
Haloarenes – Sandmeyer reactionAr–N$_2^+$Cl$^-$ + CuX (X = Cl, Br, CN)Radical substitution via diazonium salt

Rate Laws (SI Units)

  • Halogenation of alkanes: $Rate = k[RH][X_2]$; $k$ in $M^{-1}s^{-1}$.
  • SN1: $Rate = k[RX]$; $k$ in $s^{-1}$.
  • SN2: $Rate = k[RX][Nu^-]$; $k$ in $M^{-2}s^{-1}$.

Solved Example

Prepare 1‑chloropropane from 1‑propanol using Lucas reagent.

  1. Reaction: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + HCl \xrightarrow{\text{ZnCl}_2} \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Cl} + H_2O$.
  2. Mechanism: Primary alcohol undergoes SN2 attack by $Cl^-$ generated in situ.
  3. Calculation (if 10.0 g 1‑propanol, $M_{\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{O}}=60.1\,g\,mol^{-1}$):
    $n = \frac{10.0}{60.1}=0.166\,mol$; theoretical $m_{\text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{Cl}} = 0.166\times 78.5=13.0\,g$.
    If 11.5 g product obtained, % yield $=\frac{11.5}{13.0}\times100=88.5\%$.

This concise table‑driven format aligns with the CBSE Class 12 syllabus and aids quick revision for exams.

Physical properties and trends in boiling point, solubility and polarity

Definition: Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) and haloarenes (aryl halides) are organic compounds containing a carbon‑halogen bond.

Key factors influencing physical properties

  • Intermolecular forces – dipole‑dipole, London dispersion and, for H‑bond donors, hydrogen bonding.
  • Polarity – depends on C–X bond dipole moment $\mu = \delta \times d$.
  • Molecular mass – heavier halogen → stronger dispersion forces.

Boiling‑point trend

SeriesTrend
Haloalkanes (same carbon skeleton)$\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} < \text{CH}_3\text{Br} < \text{CH}_3\text{I}$
Haloarenes (same ring)$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{F} < \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Cl} < \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{Br}$

Explanation: As atomic size and mass increase, London dispersion forces ($F_{\text{L}} \propto \alpha$) increase, raising $T_{\text{b}}$.

Solubility in water

  • Small, highly polar haloalkanes (e.g., CH$_2$Cl$_2$) are moderately soluble.
  • Heavier halides are sparingly soluble because dispersion forces dominate.
  • Haloarenes are essentially insoluble due to aromatic π‑system and lack of H‑bonding.

Polarity order (C–X bond)

$\mu_{\text{C–F}} > \mu_{\text{C–Cl}} > \mu_{\text{C–Br}} > \mu_{\text{C–I}}$ because electronegativity difference $\Delta \chi$ decreases down the group.

Solved Example

Calculate the dipole moment of the C–Cl bond in chloromethane if $\Delta \chi = 0.5$ and bond length $d = 1.78\ \text{Å}$. Using $\mu = 0.208\ \Delta \chi \times d$ (Debye, $d$ in Å), $\mu = 0.208 \times 0.5 \times 1.78 = 0.185\ \text{D}$. Hence chloromethane is polar ($\mu \approx 0.19\ \text{D}$).

These trends help predict boiling points, solubility and reactivity of haloalkanes and haloarenes in the CBSE exam.

Mechanism and kinetics of nucleophilic substitution reactions (SN1 and SN2)

Definitions

  • SN1: unimolecular nucleophilic substitution; rate depends only on substrate.
  • SN2: bimolecular nucleophilic substitution; rate depends on substrate and nucleophile.

Kinetic Expressions

ReactionRate lawOrderUnit of $k$
SN1$rate = k[RX]$First order$s^{-1}$
SN2$rate = k[RX][Nu^-]$Second order$M^{-1}s^{-1}$

Derivation (SN1)

For the slow step $RX \rightarrow R^+ + X^-$, $$\\frac{d[RX]}{dt} = -k[RX]$$ integrating gives $$[RX]_t = [RX]_0 e^{-kt}$$.

Key Factors

  • Stability of carbocation (SN1) – tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • Strength and steric hindrance of nucleophile (SN2) – strong, unhindered nucleophile favours SN2.
  • Solvent polarity – polar protic stabilises ions (SN1); polar aprotic enhances nucleophilicity (SN2).

Solvent Effect on Rate Constant

Arrhenius equation: $$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$ where $A$ (s$^{-1}$ or M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$) is frequency factor, $E_a$ (kJ·mol$^{-1}$) activation energy.

Solved Example

At 298 K, the SN2 reaction between $CH_3Br$ and $OH^-$ follows $rate = k[CH_3Br][OH^-]$. Initial concentrations: $[CH_3Br]_0 = 0.010\;M$, $[OH^-]_0 = 0.020\;M$. After 500 s, $[CH_3Br] = 0.006\;M$. Find $k$.

For second‑order reaction, $$\\frac{1}{[CH_3Br]_t} - \\frac{1}{[CH_3Br]_0} = k[OH^-]t$$ (since $[OH^-]$ ≈ constant). Plugging values: $$\\frac{1}{0.006} - \\frac{1}{0.010} = k(0.020)(500)$$ $$166.67 - 100 = 5000k$$ $$66.67 = 5000k$$ $$k = 1.33\\times10^{-2}\;M^{-1}s^{-1}$$.

This $k$ value confirms a typical SN2 rate constant in polar aprotic solvent.

Mechanism and kinetics of elimination reactions (E1 and E2)

Elimination Reactions (E1 & E2)

Definition: An elimination reaction removes two atoms or groups from adjacent carbon atoms, forming a double bond. E1 proceeds via a unimolecular, two‑step pathway; E2 is a concerted bimolecular process.

  • E1: Rate‑determining step is loss of leaving group → carbocation. Rate law: $rate = k[RX]$ (first order, $s^{-1}$).
  • E2: Simultaneous removal of proton by base and departure of leaving group. Rate law: $rate = k[RX][Base]$ (second order, $M^{-1}s^{-1}$).

Kinetic Expressions

StepRate LawUnits of $k$
E1$rate = k[RX]$$s^{-1}$
E2$rate = k[RX][Base]$$M^{-1}s^{-1}$

Key Formulae

  • Arrhenius: $$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$ where $A$ (s$^{-1}$ or M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$), $E_a$ (kJ·mol$^{-1}$), $R = 8.314$ J·mol$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$, $T$ (K).
  • Degree of elimination: $\alpha = \frac{\text{moles of alkene formed}}{\text{initial moles of substrate}}$.

Solved Example

At $298\,K$, $k_{E2}=2.5\times10^{-3}\,M^{-1}s^{-1}$. For $[CH_3CH_2Br]=0.010\,M$ and $[OH^-]=0.020\,M$, calculate the rate.

Using $rate = k[RX][Base]$:

$rate = 2.5\times10^{-3}\times0.010\times0.020 = 5.0\times10^{-7}\,M\,s^{-1}$.

The reaction follows second‑order kinetics, confirming an E2 mechanism.

Reactions of haloarenes: electrophilic aromatic substitution and nucleophilic substitution

Reactions of Haloarenes

Haloarenes (Ar–X, X = Cl, Br, I) undergo two major aromatic substitution pathways:

  • Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS): The aromatic ring acts as a nucleophile and attacks an electrophile, displacing the halogen only when it is a good leaving group (e.g., –I in iodonium ion formation). The overall rate follows first‑order kinetics: $\\frac{d[ArX]}{dt} = -k[ArX]$ where $k = A\\,e^{-E_a/RT}$.
  • Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution (SNAr): Requires a strong electron‑withdrawing group (–NO₂, –CN, –COOR) ortho/para to the halogen. The mechanism proceeds via a Meisenheimer complex (σ‑complex). Rate law: $\\frac{d[ArX]}{dt} = -k[ArX][Nu^-]$ (second order).

Key Factors

FactorEASSNAr
Leaving group abilityGood (I > Br > Cl)Good (I > Br > Cl)
Activating groupElectron‑donating (–OMe, –NH₂) ↑ rateElectron‑withdrawing (–NO₂) ↑ rate
SolventPolar protic favours carbocation formationPolar aprotic stabilises $\\text{Nu}^-$

Typical SNAr Example

Reaction: $\\text{p‑Nitrochlorobenzene} + NaOH \rightarrow p\text{-Nitrophenol} + NaCl$

Solution:

  1. Formation of Meisenheimer complex: $\\text{C}_6H_4(NO_2)Cl + OH^- \rightarrow [C_6H_4(NO_2)Cl(OH)]^{\!-}$
  2. Elimination of Cl⁻ to restore aromaticity: $[C_6H_4(NO_2)Cl(OH)]^{\!-} \rightarrow C_6H_4(NO_2)OH + Cl^-$

Overall rate: $k = 1.2\times10^{-3}\,\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ at $298\,\text{K}$ (experimental). The reaction exemplifies the necessity of a –NO₂ group ortho/para to the halogen for a feasible SNAr pathway.

Take‑away: EAS dominates when the ring is electron‑rich; SNAr requires strong –EWG activation and a good leaving halide, proceeding via a σ‑complex with second‑order kinetics.

Factors affecting reactivity and selectivity in haloalkanes and haloarenes

Factors affecting reactivity & selectivity in haloalkanes

  • Leaving group ability: Better leaving groups stabilize the carbanion formed after bond cleavage. Order: $I^- > Br^- > Cl^- > F^-$ (in polar protic solvents).
  • Nucleophilicity: Depends on charge, basicity and solvent. In polar protic media $Nu^-$ > $NuH$; trend $RS^- > RO^- > H^-$. Rate law for $S_N2$: $rate = k[RX][Nu^-]$ with $k$ in $M^{-1}s^{-1}$.
  • Solvent effect: Polar protic solvents stabilise ions, favour $S_N1$; polar aprotic solvents enhance $S_N2$ by not solvating $Nu^-$. Dielectric constant ($\epsilon$) is the SI unit‑less measure of polarity.
  • Steric hindrance: $S_N2$ rate decreases sharply from primary $< $secondary $< $tertiary. $S_N1$ rate increases with carbocation stability: $3^circ > 2^circ > 1^circ$.
  • Bond dissociation energy (BDE): Lower $C–X$ BDE accelerates both $S_N1$ and $S_N2$. Typical values: $C–Iapprox 240;kJ,mol^{-1}$, $C–Clapprox 340;kJ,mol^{-1}$.

Haloarenes

  • Reactivity governed by aromatic resonance and leaving‑group ability. $S_NAr$ proceeds via Meisenheimer complex; electron‑withdrawing groups (–NO$_2$, –CN) ortho/para accelerate the reaction.
  • Activation energy ($E_a$) relation: $k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$ (Arrhenius). $A$ (frequency factor) in $s^{-1}$.
  • Solvent polarity influences $S_NAr$ less than aliphatic $S_N$ because the rate‑determining step is intramolecular.

Solved Example

Calculate the relative rate of $S_N1$ for $tert$‑butyl bromide ($C_4H_9Br$) vs $1$‑bromopropane at $25^circ C$, given $k_{tert}=1.2\times10^{-3}\;s^{-1}$ and $k_{1^circ}=2.5\times10^{-6}\;s^{-1}$.

Solution: $\frac{rate_{tert}}{rate_{1^circ}} = \frac{k_{tert}}{k_{1^circ}} = \frac{1.2\times10^{-3}}{2.5\times10^{-6}} \approx 4.8\times10^{2}$. Hence $tert$‑butyl bromide reacts ~480 times faster than $1$‑bromopropane in $S_N1$, illustrating the dominant role of carbocation stability.

Industrial applications and environmental impact

Industrial Applications

  • Manufacture of chloroform and carbon tetrachloride by chlorination of methane: $\text{CH}_4 + 3\,\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4 + 4\,\text{HCl}$.
  • Synthesis of vinyl chloride monomer (VCM) from 1,2‑dichloroethane, a key step in PVC production.
  • Preparation of pharmaceutical intermediates such as chloramphenicol and organophosphorus insecticides.
  • Use as solvents (e.g., dichloromethane) and refrigerants (CFC‑12).

Environmental Impact

  • Haloalkanes are persistent, bio‑accumulative and ozone‑depleting; many are listed under the Montreal Protocol.
  • Photolysis in the stratosphere releases chlorine radicals that catalyse $\text{O}_3$ destruction: $\text{Cl} + \text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{ClO} + \text{O}_2$; $\text{ClO} + \text{O} \rightarrow \text{Cl} + \text{O}_2$.
  • Ground‑level release leads to groundwater contamination; toxicity expressed as $\text{LD}_{50}$ (mg kg$^{-1}$).

Solved Example

Calculate the mass of $\text{CCl}_4$ obtained when $5.0\,\text{mol}$ of $\text{CH}_4$ is completely chlorinated (theoretical yield only).

Balanced equation: $\text{CH}_4 + 3\,\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4 + 4\,\text{HCl}$. Moles of $\text{CCl}_4$ = moles of $\text{CH}_4$ = $5.0\,\text{mol}$. Molar mass of $\text{CCl}_4$ = $12.01 + 4\times35.45 = 153.81\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$. Mass = $5.0\times153.81 = 7.69\times10^2\,\text{g}$.
