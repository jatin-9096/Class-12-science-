1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Scope of Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. It studies redox processes that either generate an electric current (galvanic or voltaic cells) or are driven by an external current (electrolytic cells). The central concepts are oxidation (loss of electrons) and reduction (gain of electrons). In a redox reaction, the species that loses electrons is called the *anode* and the species that gains electrons is the *cathode*. The direction of spontaneous electron flow is from anode to cathode through the external circuit, while ions migrate through the internal salt bridge or porous membrane to maintain charge neutrality. The *cell potential* ($E_{\text{cell}}$) quantifies the driving force of the redox process and is measured in volts (V). A positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ indicates a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions, whereas a negative value denotes a non‑spontaneous process that requires external energy. Standard electrode potentials ($E^\circ$) are tabulated at 25 °C, 1 atm pressure, and unit activity for solutes, providing a convenient reference for calculating cell potentials using the equation $$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}.$$ The Nernst equation extends this relationship to non‑standard conditions, linking the cell potential to the reaction quotient ($Q$) and temperature ($T$): $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q,$$ where $n$ is the number of moles of electrons transferred, $R$ is the universal gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), and $F$ is Faraday’s constant (96 485 C mol⁻¹). Understanding these definitions forms the foundation for analysing galvanic cells, electrolytic processes, corrosion, batteries, and many industrial applications.

Chemistry · Core Law
Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis and Their Applications

Michael Faraday formulated two quantitative laws that describe the relationship between the amount of electricity passed through an electrolyte and the mass of substances deposited or liberated at the electrodes. First Law: The mass ($m$) of a substance produced at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed, i.e., $$m = ZQ,$$ where $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance (g C⁻¹). Since $Q = It$ (current $I$ multiplied by time $t$), the law can also be expressed as $$m = ZIt.$$ Second Law: When the same quantity of electricity is passed through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E$). Mathematically, $$\frac{m_1}{E_1} = \frac{m_2}{E_2} = \dots = \frac{Q}{F},$$ where $E = \frac{M}{n}$ (molar mass $M$ divided by the number of electrons $n$ involved in the electrode reaction). These laws enable calculation of the amount of metal plated in electroplating, the yield of gases in electrolysis of water, and the capacity of batteries. For instance, in the electrolysis of molten $\ce{NaCl}$, passing 9650 C of charge (equivalent to 0.1 F) will deposit 2.30 g of sodium at the cathode because the electrochemical equivalent of Na is $Z_{\ce{Na}} = \frac{M_{\ce{Na}}}{F} = \frac{22.99}{96\,485} \approx 2.38\times10^{-4}\,\text{g C}^{-1}$. Multiplying by the charge gives the deposited mass. Faraday’s laws also underpin the concept of *Faraday’s constant* ($F$), the charge carried by one mole of electrons, which is pivotal in linking chemical stoichiometry with electrical quantities.

Chemistry · Core Law
The Nernst Equation: Intuition, Derivation, and Practical Use

The Nernst equation provides a quantitative bridge between thermodynamics and electrochemistry, allowing us to predict cell potentials under any set of conditions. Starting from the Gibbs free energy relationship $$\Delta G = -nFE,$$ and the fundamental thermodynamic identity $$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q,$$ we combine them to obtain $$-nFE = -nFE^\circ + RT\ln Q,$$ which simplifies to the Nernst equation $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q.$$ At 25 °C (298 K), the term $\frac{RT}{F}$ equals 0.025693 V, and converting the natural logarithm to base‑10 yields the convenient form $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q.$$ The reaction quotient $Q$ reflects the instantaneous ratio of product activities to reactant activities. For a generic redox reaction $$\ce{aA + bB -> cC + dD},$$ the Nernst equation becomes $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}.$$ This expression explains why cell potentials decrease as reactant concentrations fall or product concentrations rise. It also rationalises the effect of temperature: higher $T$ amplifies the influence of $Q$, making the cell more sensitive to concentration changes. In practical terms, the Nernst equation is indispensable for designing batteries (optimising electrolyte concentration), for analytical techniques such as potentiometry (e.g., pH measurement using the hydrogen electrode), and for understanding corrosion processes where local changes in ion concentration shift the corrosion potential.

Chemistry · Core Law
Exceptions, Limitations, and Special Cases in Electrochemical Theory

While the standard equations of electrochemistry are robust, several scenarios deviate from ideal behaviour. Non‑ideal activity coefficients: The Nernst equation assumes unit activity for solids and pure liquids, and uses concentrations for solutes. In highly concentrated solutions, ion–ion interactions alter activities, requiring activity coefficients ($\gamma$) to correct the expression: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{a_{\text{products}}}{a_{\text{reactants}}},\quad a_i = \gamma_i [i].$$ Ignoring $\gamma$ can lead to significant errors, especially in battery electrolytes with high ionic strength. Mixed potentials: Real electrodes often host multiple redox couples simultaneously (e.g., $\ce{Fe^{2+}/Fe}$ and $\ce{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}$). The observed potential is the *mixed potential* where the net anodic and cathodic currents balance. This phenomenon explains corrosion of metals in aerated solutions, where oxygen reduction and metal oxidation occur concurrently. Kinetic overpotential: The Nernst equation predicts the thermodynamic equilibrium potential, but actual cell operation involves kinetic barriers. Activation overpotential ($\eta_{\text{act}}$) arises from the finite rate of electron transfer, described by the Butler‑Volmer equation $$i = i_0\left[e^{\alpha nF\eta/RT} - e^{-(1-\alpha)nF\eta/RT}\right],$$ where $i_0$ is the exchange current density and $\alpha$ the charge‑transfer coefficient. Large $\eta_{\text{act}}$ reduces the effective cell voltage. Concentration overpotential: When mass transport of reactants to the electrode surface is limited, a concentration gradient develops, leading to an additional voltage drop ($\eta_{\text{conc}}$). This is critical in high‑current batteries where diffusion of $\ce{Li+}$ ions in the electrolyte becomes rate‑limiting. Electrolytic cells with non‑spontaneous reactions: In electrolytic processes, an external voltage greater than the absolute value of $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ must be applied to overcome both thermodynamic and kinetic barriers. For example, water electrolysis requires a minimum of 1.23 V (thermodynamic) plus additional overpotential to drive $\ce{O2}$ evolution. Recognising these exceptions ensures accurate predictions and efficient design of real‑world electrochemical systems.

Derivation Details

1. Derivation of Rate Law for Elementary Reactions
For an elementary reaction of the form
$$A + B \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products$$
the rate of disappearance of reactants is proportional to the probability of collision between molecules of A and B. The collision theory gives the rate expression as
$$r = k [A][B]$$
where $k$ is the rate constant and $[A]$, $[B]$ are molar concentrations. The derivation starts from the definition of rate as the change in concentration with respect to time:
$$r = -\frac{1}{\nu_A}\frac{d[A]}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\nu_B}\frac{d[B]}{dt}$$
with $\nu_A$ and $\nu_B$ being stoichiometric coefficients (here both 1). The collision frequency $Z_{AB}$ for bimolecular collisions is given by kinetic theory as
$$Z_{AB} = N_A N_B \sigma_{AB} \bar{v}_{AB}$$
where $N_A$, $N_B$ are number densities, $\sigma_{AB}$ is the collision cross‑section, and $\bar{v}_{AB}$ is the average relative speed. Converting number densities to molar concentrations using Avogadro’s number $N_A$ (not to be confused with the species), we obtain
$$Z_{AB} = N_A N_B \sigma_{AB} \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi \mu_{AB}}}$$
with $\mu_{AB}$ the reduced mass. The rate constant $k$ is then expressed as
$$k = \frac{Z_{AB}}{N_A N_B} = \sigma_{AB} \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi \mu_{AB}}}$$
which shows the temperature dependence of $k$ for an elementary step. For a unimolecular reaction
$$A \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products$$
the rate law becomes
$$r = k [A]$$
because only one species is involved in the rate‑determining step. The general form for an elementary reaction with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_i$ is
$$r = k \prod_i [A_i]^{\nu_i}$$
where the exponents are the stoichiometric coefficients of the reactants in the elementary step. This derivation is the foundation for constructing rate laws for complex mechanisms by adding elementary steps and applying the steady‑state or pre‑equilibrium approximations. 2. Integrated Rate Laws for First and Second Order Reactions
For a first‑order reaction
$$A \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products$$
the differential rate law is
$$-\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]$$
Separating variables and integrating from $[A]_0$ at $t=0$ to $[A]$ at time $t$ gives
$$\int_{[A]_0}^{[A]} \frac{d[A]}{[A]} = -k \int_0^t dt$$
which yields the integrated form
$$\ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0} = -kt$$
or equivalently
$$[A] = [A]_0 e^{-kt}$$
The half‑life $t_{1/2}$ is obtained by setting $[A] = [A]_0/2$ and solving for $t$:
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}$$
For a second‑order reaction of the type
$$A + B \;\xrightarrow{k}\; Products$$
with initial concentrations $[A]_0$ and $[B]_0$, the rate law is
$$-\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A][B]$$
If $[A]_0 = [B]_0$, the integrated rate law simplifies to
$$\frac{1}{[A]} - \frac{1}{[A]_0} = kt$$
or
$$[A] = \frac{[A]_0}{1 + k[A]_0 t}$$
For the general case where $[A]_0 \neq [B]_0$, the integrated form is
$$\frac{[B]_0}{[A]_0 - [B]_0} \ln\frac{[A][B]_0}{[B][A]_0} = kt$$
