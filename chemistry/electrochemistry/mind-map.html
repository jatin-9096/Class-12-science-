Electrochemical cells and cell notation

Electrochemical Cell – a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy (galvanic) or vice‑versa (electrolytic).

Cell notation represents the cell in a compact form:

ComponentSymbol
Anode (oxidation)$\text{Zn(s)}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(a)$
Cathode (reduction)$\text{Cu}^{2+}(a)|\text{Cu(s)}$
Salt bridge$||$

The general format is $\text{anode}|\text{anode\ ion\ (aq)}||\text{cathode\ ion\ (aq)}|\text{cathode}$.

  • Standard EMF: $E^\circ_{cell}=E^\circ_{cathode}-E^\circ_{anode}$ (V).
  • Nernst equation: $$E=E^\circ-\frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$ where $n$ = electrons transferred, $F=96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, $R=8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $T$ \in K.
  • At $298\ \text{K}$, $\frac{RT}{F}=0.0257\ \text{V}$, so $$E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0257}{n}\ln Q$$.

Solved Example

Find $E_{cell}$ for the Daniell cell $\text{Zn(s)}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(0.010\ \text{M})||\text{Cu}^{2+}(0.10\ \text{M})|\text{Cu(s)}$ at $298\ \text{K}$. $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}}=-0.76\ \text{V}$, $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}}=+0.34\ \text{V}$.

Solution: $E^\circ_{cell}=0.34-(-0.76)=1.10\ \text{V}$. $Q=\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]}=\frac{0.010}{0.10}=0.10$. $n=2$. $$E=1.10-\frac{0.0257}{2}\ln(0.10)=1.10-0.01285\times(-2.302)=1.10+0.0296\approx1.13\ \text{V}$$.

Key points: EMF is positive for spontaneous galvanic cells; cell notation reads left to right (anode to cathode); the salt bridge maintains charge neutrality.

Galvanic (voltaic) cells and EMF

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell – Definition
A galvanic cell is a spontaneous electrochemical system that converts chemical energy into electrical energy. It consists of two half‑cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte, connected by a salt bridge or porous membrane.

Cell Notation
General form: $\text{Zn(s)}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(aq)\,||\,\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)|\text{Cu(s)}$ where “|” separates different phases and “||” denotes the salt bridge.

Key Formulas

  • Cell EMF (standard): $E^{\circ}_{cell}=E^{\circ}_{cathode}-E^{\circ}_{anode}$ (unit: V).
  • Nernst Equation (at $T$ K): $$E_{cell}=E^{\circ}_{cell}-\frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$ where $R=8.314\,\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $F=96485\,\text{C mol}^{-1}$, $n$ = electrons transferred, $Q$ = reaction quotient.
  • Relation with Gibbs free energy: $\Delta G=-nF E_{cell}$ (unit: J).

Units

  • EMF – volt (V)
  • Standard electrode potential – V
  • Gibbs free energy – joule (J)

Worked Example

Calculate the EMF of the cell $\text{Zn(s)}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(1.0\,\text{M})||\text{Cu}^{2+}(0.10\,\text{M})|\text{Cu(s)}$ at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$.

  1. Standard potentials: $E^{\circ}_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}}=+0.34\,\text{V}$, $E^{\circ}_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}}=-0.76\,\text{V}$.
  2. $E^{\circ}_{cell}=0.34-(-0.76)=1.10\,\text{V}$.
  3. Reaction: $\text{Zn(s)}+\text{Cu}^{2+}\rightarrow\text{Zn}^{2+}+\text{Cu(s)}$, $n=2$.
  4. Reaction quotient $Q=\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]}=\frac{1.0}{0.10}=10$.
  5. At $25^{\circ}\text{C}$, $\frac{RT}{F}=0.0257\,\text{V}$, so $$E_{cell}=1.10-\frac{0.0257}{2}\ln 10=1.10-0.0296=1.07\,\text{V}.$$

Thus, the cell EMF is $1.07\,\text{V}$, confirming the spontaneous nature of the reaction.

Standard electrode potentials and the electrochemical series

Standard Electrode Potential (E°) – The potential of a half‑cell measured under standard conditions (1 M, 1 atm, 25 °C) when connected to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE, set at 0 V). Unit: $\text{V}$.

Electrochemical Series – List of elements/ions arranged in decreasing order of $E^\circ$ values. It predicts spontaneity, direction of redox reactions and relative reactivity.

Key Relations

  • $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$
  • $\Delta G = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$  ($n$ = moles of electrons, $F = 96\,485\,\text{C mol}^{-1}$)
  • $K = 10^{\frac{nE^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.0592}}$  (at 25 °C)

Derivation of $\Delta G$ relation

From the definition of electrical work $W = -nFE$, and the thermodynamic identity $\Delta G = W$ for a reversible cell, we obtain $\Delta G = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$.

SI Units

  • Electrode potential – volt (V)
  • Free energy – joule (J)
  • Faraday constant – coulomb per mole (C mol⁻¹)

Example

Find $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ and $\Delta G$ for the reaction:
$\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$
Given $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.34\,\text{V}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\,\text{V}$.

Solution:

Half‑cell$E^\circ$ (V)
Cu$^{2+}$ + 2e$^-$ → Cu+0.34
Zn → Zn$^{2+}$ + 2e$^-$ (oxidation)+0.76

Since Zn is oxidised, its $E^\circ$ is taken as $+0.76\,\text{V}$ (reverse of reduction value).
$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\,\text{V}$.

$\Delta G = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}} = -(2)(96\,485)(1.10) = -2.12\times10^{5}\,\text{J mol}^{-1}$.

The positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ and negative $\Delta G$ confirm the reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions.

Nernst equation and calculation of cell potential

Definition: The Nernst equation relates the cell potential $E$ of an electrochemical cell to the activities (or concentrations) of the reacting species.

General form

$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$

At $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ (298 K) it is written in base‑10 logarithm as

$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{0.05916\text{ V}}{n}\log_{10} Q$

Symbols and SI units

  • $E$, $E^{\circ}$ – cell potential, volts (V)
  • $R$ – universal gas constant, $8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$
  • $T$ – temperature, kelvin (K)
  • $n$ – number of electrons transferred in the overall reaction (dimensionless)
  • $F$ – Faraday constant, $96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$
  • $Q$ – reaction quotient (dimensionless)

Derivation (brief)

From thermodynamics $\Delta G = -nFE$ and $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ} + RT\ln Q$.

Equating and using $\Delta G^{\circ} = -nFE^{\circ}$ gives $E = E^{\circ} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$.

Example

Calculate $E$ at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ for the cell:

$$\text{Cu(s)}\;|\;\text{Cu}^{2+}(0.01\,\text{M})\;||\;\text{Fe}^{3+}(0.10\,\text{M}),\;\text{Fe}^{2+}(0.01\,\text{M})\;|\;\text{Fe(s)}$$

Given $E^{\circ}(\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}) = +0.771\,\text{V}$ and $E^{\circ}(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}) = +0.337\,\text{V}$.

Overall reaction (2 e⁻ transferred):
$2\text{Fe}^{3+} + \text{Cu} \rightarrow 2\text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{Cu}^{2+}$

$E^{\circ}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\circ}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\circ}_{\text{anode}} = 0.771 - 0.337 = 0.434\,\text{V}$

Reaction quotient $Q = \frac{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]^{2}[\text{Cu}^{2+}]}{[\text{Fe}^{3+}]^{2}} = \frac{(0.01)^{2}(0.01)}{(0.10)^{2}} = 1\times10^{-4}$

Using the Nernst equation (n = 2):
$E = 0.434 - \frac{0.05916}{2}\log_{10}(1\times10^{-4})$
$\log_{10}(1\times10^{-4}) = -4$
$E = 0.434 - \frac{0.05916}{2}(-4) = 0.434 + 0.1183 = 0.552\,\text{V}$

Key points

  • At standard conditions ($Q=1$) $E = E^{\circ}$.
  • Increasing reactant activity (\in denominator of $Q$) makes $E$ more positive.
  • Temperature dependence enters through the $\frac{RT}{nF}$ term.

Electrolytic cells and Faraday’s laws of electrolysis

Electrolytic Cell: An electrolytic cell converts electrical energy into chemical change. It consists of two electrodes immersed in an electrolyte and is driven by an external DC source.

Faraday’s First Law: The mass ($m$) of a substance liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed.

$$m = \frac{Q\,M}{nF}$$

where $M$ = molar mass (g·mol$^{-1}$), $n$ = valency change, $F = 96\,485\,\text{C·mol}^{-1}$ (Faraday constant).

Faraday’s Second Law: The masses of different substances liberated by the same quantity of electricity are proportional \to their equivalent weights ($E = \frac{M}{n}$).

$$\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{E_1}{E_2}$$

SI Units

  • Charge, $Q$: coulomb (C)
  • Current, $I$: ampere (A)
  • Time, $t$: second (s)
  • Molar mass, $M$: gram per mole (g·mol$^{-1}$)

Key Relations

QuantityExpressionUnit
Charge$Q = I t$C
Faraday constant$F = N_A e = 96\,485$C·mol$^{-1}$

Solved Example

In the electrolysis of $\text{CuSO}_4$, 2.00 A current is passed for 3.00 h. Find the mass of copper deposited at the cathode.

Given: $I = 2.00\,\text{A}$, $t = 3.00\,\text{h}=3.00\times3600\,\text{s}=10800\,\text{s}$, $M_{Cu}=63.55\,\text{g·mol}^{-1}$, $n=2$.

First, $Q = I t = 2.00 \times 10800 = 2.16\times10^{4}\,\text{C}$.

Then, $m = \frac{Q M}{nF}=\frac{2.16\times10^{4}\times63.55}{2\times96\,485}=7.1\,\text{g}$ (\approx).

Thus, $7.1\,\text{g}$ of Cu is deposited.

Conductance and conductivity of electrolytes

Conductance (G) and Conductivity (\(\kappa\)) of Electrolytes

  • Conductance (G): Ability of an electrolyte solution to conduct electric current. SI unit: siemens (S).
  • Conductivity (\(\kappa\)): Conductance of a solution having unit cross‑sectional area and unit length. SI unit: siemens per metre (S·m‑1).

Key Relations

  • For a cell of length l (m) and cross‑sectional area A (m2): $G = \frac{\kappa A}{l}$
  • Hence $\kappa = \frac{G l}{A}$
  • Molar conductivity $\Lambda_{m}$: $\Lambda_{m}=\frac{\kappa \times 1000}{c}$ where c is molar concentration (mol·m‑3).
  • Equivalent conductivity $\lambda$: $\lambda = \frac{\kappa \times 1000}{N_{eq}}$ where $N_{eq}$ is normality (eq·m‑3).

Symbols & Units

SymbolQuantitySI Unit
$G$ConductanceS
$\kappa$ConductivityS·m‑1
$\Lambda_{m}$Molar conductivityS·cm2·mol‑1
$\lambda$Equivalent conductivityS·cm2·eq‑1

Solved Example

Electrolyte solution fills a cell of length $l=\,1.0\,\text{cm}$ and cross‑section $A=\,0.5\,\text{cm}^2$. Measured conductance $G=\,2.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{S}$. Find (a) conductivity $\kappa$, (b) molar conductivity if concentration $c=\,0.01\,\text{mol·L}^{-1}$.

Solution:

(a) $\kappa = \frac{G l}{A}=\frac{2.0\times10^{-3}\times1.0}{0.5}=4.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{S·cm}^{-1}$.

(b) Convert $c$ to mol·cm‑3: $0.01\,\text{mol·L}^{-1}=1.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{mol·cm}^{-3}$. Then $\Lambda_{m}=\frac{\kappa\times1000}{c}=\frac{4.0\times10^{-3}\times1000}{0.01}=400\,\text{S·cm}^2\text{·mol}^{-1}$.

Thus the solution has $\kappa=4.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{S·cm}^{-1}$ and $\Lambda_{m}=400\,\text{S·cm}^2\text{·mol}^{-1}$.

Corrosion and its prevention

Corrosion is the undesired electrochemical conversion of a metal into its more stable oxide, hydroxide or salt in the presence of an electrolyte.

Key points

  • Occurs via simultaneous anodic (metal $→$ metal ion + $e^{-}$) and cathodic (e.g., $O_{2}+2H_{2}O+4e^{-}\rightarrow4OH^{-}$) reactions.
  • Uniform, pitting and galvanic are the common forms.

Corrosion rate formula

$CR = \frac{K\,I}{n\,A\,D}$

where $I$ = corrosion current (A), $n$ = number of electrons transferred, $A$ = exposed area (cm$^{2}$), $D$ = metal density (g·cm$^{-3}$), $K$ = constant $0.00327$ (gives $CR$ in mm·yr$^{-1}$).

SI Units

  • Corrosion current $I$: ampere (A)
  • Density $D$: kilogram per cubic metre (kg·m$^{-3}$) – convert from g·cm$^{-3}$ when needed.
  • Corrosion rate $CR$: millimetre per year (mm·yr$^{-1}$)

Prevention methods

  • Protective coating: paints, galvanisation.
  • Cathodic protection:
    • Sacrificial anode (e.g., Zn for Fe).
    • Impressed current using an external DC source.
  • Alloying: adding Cr, Ni to form passive film.
  • Inhibitors: chemicals that slow anodic or cathodic reactions.

Typical anodic and cathodic reactions

ElectrodeReaction
Anode (Fe)$Fe\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+2e^{-}$
Cathode (O$_{2}$)$O_{2}+2H_{2}O+4e^{-}\rightarrow4OH^{-}$

Solved example

Given: $I = 2.5\times10^{-3}$ A, $A = 10\,cm^{2}$, $n = 2$, $D = 7.87\,g\,cm^{-3}$, $K = 0.00327$. Find $CR$.

Solution:

$CR = \frac{K I}{n A D}=\frac{0.00327\times2.5\times10^{-3}}{2\times10\times7.87}$

$=\frac{8.175\times10^{-6}}{157.4}=5.2\times10^{-8}\,mm\,yr^{-1}$

The corrosion rate is extremely low, indicating effective protection under the given conditions.

Applications of electrochemistry

Applications of Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry finds extensive use in industry and daily life. Key applications include:

  • Electroplating: Deposition of a thin metal layer on a substrate to improve appearance, corrosion resistance or wear‑ability.
  • Electro‑refining: Purification of metals (e.g., Cu, Ag) by making the impure metal the anode and the pure metal the cathode.
  • Batteries & fuel cells: Galvanic cells convert chemical energy to electrical energy; common types are dry cells, lead‑acid, and Li‑ion cells.
  • Corrosion prevention: Cathodic protection (using a sacrificial anode) and coating by electro‑plating inhibit metal degradation.
  • Industrial production: Large‑scale generation of Cl₂, Na, H₂, and NaOH by electrolysis of brine.

Key Formulas

QuantityExpressionSI Unit
Charge$Q = I t$C (coulomb)
Mass deposited (Faraday’s law)$m = \frac{Q M}{n F}$g
Standard cell potential$E^{\circ}_{\text{cell}} = E^{\circ}_{\text{cathode}} - E^{\circ}_{\text{anode}}$V
Nernst equation (25°C)$E = E^{\circ} - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q$V

Solved Example

During electro‑refining of copper, a current of $I = 2.5\,\text{A}$ is passed for $t = 30\,\text{min}$. Find the mass of copper deposited at the cathode. ($M_{Cu}=63.55\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$, $n=2$, $F=96485\,\text{C mol}^{-1}$)

Solution:

  1. Calculate charge: $Q = I t = 2.5 \times (30 \times 60) = 2.5 \times 1800 = 4500\,\text{C}$.
  2. Apply Faraday’s law: $m = \frac{Q M}{n F} = \frac{4500 \times 63.55}{2 \times 96485} \approx 1.48\,\text{g}$.

Thus, $1.48\,\text{g}$ of pure copper is obtained.
