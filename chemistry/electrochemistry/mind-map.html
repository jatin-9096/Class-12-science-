1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Terminology

Electrochemistry deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. The central entities are electrochemical cells, which are devices that either generate electrical energy from spontaneous redox reactions (galvanic or voltaic cells) or drive non‑spontaneous reactions using an external source of electricity (electrolytic cells). A typical cell consists of two half‑cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. The electrode where oxidation occurs is called the anode, while the electrode where reduction occurs is the cathode. The potential difference between the two electrodes is termed the cell potential or electromotive force (EMF), symbolised by $E_{\text{cell}}$. The sign convention adopted by IUPAC states that $E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$.

The quantitative description of a redox process is provided by the standard electrode potential, $E^\circ$, measured under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 25 °C). These potentials are tabulated against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which is arbitrarily assigned a potential of 0.00 V. The Nernst equation links the cell potential at any arbitrary condition to the standard potential: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$ where $R$ is the universal gas constant, $T$ the absolute temperature, $n$ the number of moles of electrons transferred, $F$ Faraday’s constant, and $Q$ the reaction quotient. This equation is the cornerstone for predicting the direction of spontaneity and calculating equilibrium constants for redox reactions.

Chemistry · Fundamental Laws
Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

Michael Faraday formulated two empirical laws that quantify the relationship between the amount of electricity passed through an electrolyte and the amount of chemical change produced.

First Law: The mass ($m$) of a substance liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity ($Q$) passed through the cell: $$m = Z \, Q$$ where $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance. Since $Q = I t$ (current $I$ multiplied by time $t$), the law can also be expressed as $m = Z I t$.

Second Law: For a given quantity of electricity, the masses of different substances liberated are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E$). The equivalent weight is defined as the molar mass divided by the number of electrons exchanged ($n$): $$E = \frac{M}{n}$$ Consequently, if the same charge $Q$ is passed through two different electrolytes, the ratio of the masses deposited is: $$\frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{E_1}{E_2} = \frac{M_1 n_2}{M_2 n_1}$$ These laws underpin industrial processes such as electroplating, metal refining, and the production of chlorine and sodium hydroxide by the chlor‑alkali process. They also provide a practical route to determine Faraday’s constant ($F = 96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$) experimentally.

Chemistry · Intuitive Understanding
Cell Potential, Spontaneity and the Gibbs Free Energy

The thermodynamic link between electrical work and chemical change is expressed through the relationship: $$\Delta G = -n F E_{\text{cell}}$$ where $\Delta G$ is the Gibbs free energy change for the overall redox reaction. A negative $\Delta G$ indicates a spontaneous process, which corresponds to a positive $E_{\text{cell}}$ for galvanic cells. Conversely, a negative cell potential implies a non‑spontaneous reaction that must be driven by an external voltage source, as in electrolytic cells.

The sign convention for $E_{\text{cell}}$ is crucial. In a galvanic cell, electrons flow from the anode (higher oxidation potential) to the cathode (higher reduction potential). The cell diagram is written as: $$\text{Anode} \mid \text{Anode solution} \parallel \text{Cathode solution} \mid \text{Cathode}$$ For example, the Daniell cell is represented as: $$\text{Zn(s)} \mid \text{Zn}^{2+}(1\ \text{M}) \parallel \text{Cu}^{2+}(1\ \text{M}) \mid \text{Cu(s)}$$ The standard EMF for this cell is $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} - E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = 0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.76\ \text{V}) = 1.10\ \text{V}$. By applying the Nernst equation, the actual EMF under non‑standard concentrations can be evaluated, providing insight into how concentration gradients influence the driving force of the cell.

Chemistry · Exceptions and Special Cases
Non‑Standard Conditions, Kinetic Barriers and Over‑Potential

While the Nernst equation accurately predicts equilibrium potentials, real electrochemical systems often deviate due to kinetic factors. The term over‑potential ($\eta$) denotes the extra voltage required to overcome activation energy barriers for electron transfer at an electrode surface. Over‑potential is especially pronounced for reactions involving gas evolution (e.g., hydrogen evolution on a platinum electrode) or for poorly conducting electrodes.

The overall cell potential in practice becomes: $$E_{\text{obs}} = E_{\text{thermo}} - \eta_{\text{anode}} - \eta_{\text{cathode}}$$ where $E_{\text{thermo}}$ is the thermodynamic EMF obtained from the Nernst equation. Over‑potential can be reduced by employing catalysts, increasing temperature, or using electrodes with higher surface area.

Another exception arises in concentration cells, where both half‑cells contain the same redox couple but at different concentrations. The cell potential is solely a function of the concentration ratio: $$E_{\text{cell}} = \frac{RT}{nF}\ln\frac{c_{\text{high}}}{c_{\text{low}}}$$ Here, no external standard electrode is needed, yet the cell still produces a measurable voltage. Such cells illustrate the profound impact of activity coefficients and ionic strength on electrochemical measurements.

Chemistry · Worked Example
Calculating EMF of a Mixed‑Solution Cell

Problem: Determine the cell potential at 298 K for the following cell: $$\text{Fe(s)} \mid \text{Fe}^{2+}(0.010\ \text{M}) \parallel \text{Cu}^{2+}(0.50\ \text{M}) \mid \text{Cu(s)}$$ Given standard potentials: $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} = -0.44\ \text{V}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.34\ \text{V}$.

Solution: First, identify the anode and cathode. Since $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} > E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}}$, copper undergoes reduction (cathode) and iron undergoes oxidation (anode). The standard EMF is: $$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = 0.34\ \text{V} - (-0.44\ \text{V}) = 0.78\ \text{V}$$ The reaction involves $n = 2$ electrons. Apply the Nernst equation: $$E = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$$ The overall cell reaction is: $$\text{Fe(s)} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{Cu(s)}$$ Hence, the reaction quotient $Q = \frac{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]} = \frac{0.010}{0.50} = 0.020$. Substituting $R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $T = 298\ \text{K}$, $F = 96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$: $$\frac{RT}{nF} = \frac{8.314 \times 298}{2 \times 96485} = 0.01285\ \text{V}$$ Therefore, $$E = 0.78\ \text{V} - 0.01285\ \text{V} \times \ln(0.020)$$ $$\ln(0.020) = -3.912$$ $$E = 0.78\ \text{V} - 0.01285\ \text{V} \times (-3.912)$$ $$E = 0.78\ \text{V} + 0.0503\ \text{V} = 0.830\ \text{V}$$

Interpretation: The cell potential under the given non‑standard concentrations is $0.83\ \text{V}$, slightly higher than the standard value because the concentration of $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ (cathode) is larger than that of $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ (anode), driving the reaction more strongly towards product formation.

Formulas and Derivations

2. Master Equation Bank

Derivation Details

In the CBSE Class 12 curriculum, the thermodynamic section is built upon a few fundamental postulates that connect macroscopic observables with microscopic states. The first postulate states that the entropy of an isolated system is maximized at equilibrium. The second postulate introduces the concept of internal energy, U, as a state function that depends only on the present state of the system and not on the path taken. These two postulates allow us to derive the differential form of internal energy, which is the cornerstone for all subsequent thermodynamic potentials. We begin with the first law of thermodynamics, which in differential form reads $$dU = \delta Q + \delta W$$ where $\delta Q$ is the infinitesimal heat added to the system and $\delta W$ is the infinitesimal work done on the system. For a simple compressible system, the work term is $-P\,dV$, where $P$ is the pressure and $V$ is the volume. Substituting this into the first law gives $$dU = \delta Q - P\,dV.$$ At this juncture, we invoke the definition of entropy, $S$, through the Clausius inequality, which for a reversible process becomes $$\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS$$ where $T$ is the absolute temperature. Combining the two equations for a reversible process yields the fundamental thermodynamic identity $$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV.$$ This identity is the starting point for deriving all other thermodynamic potentials. It tells us that the internal energy is a function of the natural variables $S$ and $V$, i.e. $U=U(S,V)$, and the partial derivatives of $U$ with respect to these variables give the temperature and pressure: $$\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial S}\right)_V = T \quad \text{and} \quad \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_S = -P.$$ These relations are often referred to as the Maxwell relations for internal energy, and they provide a powerful tool for connecting measurable quantities with state functions.

From the fundamental identity, we can construct other thermodynamic potentials by performing Legendre transforms that change the natural variables of the function. The Helmholtz free energy, $A$, is defined as $$A = U - T\,S.$$ Taking the differential of $A$ and substituting $dU$ from the fundamental identity gives $$dA = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT = (T\,dS - P\,dV) - T\,dS - S\,dT = -P\,dV - S\,dT.$$ Thus, the natural variables of $A$ are $T$ and $V$, and the partial derivatives are $$\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}\right)_V = -S \quad \text{and} \quad \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial V}\right)_T = -P.$$ The Gibbs free energy, $G$, is defined as $$G = U + P\,V - T\,S.$$ Differentiating $G$ yields $$dG = dU + P\,dV + V\,dP - T\,dS - S\,dT = (T\,dS - P\,dV) + P\,dV + V\,dP - T\,dS - S\,dT = V\,dP - S\,dT.$$ Therefore, the natural variables of $G$ are $T$ and $P$, and the partial derivatives are $$\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_P = -S \quad \text{and} \quad \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_T = V.$$ These expressions are central to the analysis of chemical equilibria, as the equilibrium condition for a reaction at constant $T$ and $P$ is $\Delta G = 0$.

Using the fact that mixed second derivatives of a state function are equal, we can derive the Maxwell relations. Starting from the Helmholtz free energy differential $dA = -S\,dT - P\,dV$, we write the mixed partial derivatives: $$\left(\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial V\,\partial T}\right) = \left(\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial T\,\partial V}\right).$$ Substituting the first derivatives gives $$\left(\frac{\partial (-S)}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial (-P)}{\partial T}\right)_V,$$ which simplifies to $$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.$$ This is one of the four Maxwell relations. Similarly, from $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$, we obtain $$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P.$$ The other two Maxwell relations are derived from the internal energy differential $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$ and the enthalpy differential $dH = T\,dS + V\,dP$. These relations are invaluable for deriving thermodynamic properties that are difficult to measure directly, such as the temperature dependence of pressure or the pressure dependence of entropy.

One of the most frequently encountered derivations in the CBSE syllabus is the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, which describes the slope of a phase boundary in a $P$–$T$ diagram. Consider a reversible phase transition between two phases, say liquid and vapor, at equilibrium. The condition for equilibrium is that the chemical potentials of the two phases are equal: $\mu_1 = \mu_2$. Differentiating this equality with respect to temperature at constant pressure gives $$\frac{d\mu_1}{dT} = \frac{d\mu_2}{dT}.$$ Using the Gibbs–Duhem relation $d\mu = -S_m\,dT + V_m\,dP$ where $S_m$ and $V_m$ are the molar entropy and volume, we obtain $$-S_{m1}\,dT + V_{m1}\,dP = -S_{m2}\,dT + V_{m2}\,dP.$$ Rearranging terms yields the Clausius–Clapeyron equation $$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{S_{m2} - S_{m1}}{V_{m2} - V_{m1}} = \frac{\Delta S_m}{\Delta V_m}.$$ Since the latent heat of transition, $L$, is related to the entropy change by $L = T\,\Delta S_m$, the equation can also be written as $$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta V_m}.$$ For the liquid–vapor transition, $\Delta V_m$ is dominated by the vapor volume, so the equation simplifies to the familiar form $$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T\,V_{\text{vap}}}.$$ This derivation is a textbook example of how thermodynamic potentials and Maxwell relations combine to yield experimentally useful relations.

In the context of chemical kinetics, the van’t Hoff equation is derived from the temperature dependence of the equilibrium constant, $K$. Starting from the definition of the Gibbs free energy change for a reaction, $$\Delta G = -RT\,\ln K,$$ we differentiate with respect to temperature at constant pressure: $$\frac{d(\Delta G)}{dT} = -R\,\ln K - \frac{RT}{K}\,\frac{dK}{dT}.$$ Using the relation $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\,\Delta S$, we have $$\frac{d(\Delta G)}{dT} = -\Delta S - \Delta H/T.$$ Equating the two expressions for $d(\Delta G)/dT$ and simplifying gives the van’t Hoff equation $$\frac{d\ln K}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H}{RT^2}.$$ Integrating between two temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ yields $$\ln\left(\frac{K_2}{K_1}\right) = -\frac{\Delta H}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right).$$ This relation is routinely used to estimate the temperature dependence of equilibrium constants and to determine enthalpy changes from experimental $K$ values.
