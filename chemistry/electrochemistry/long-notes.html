Electrochemical cells and cell EMF

Electrochemical Cell – A system where a spontaneous redox reaction produces electrical energy.

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell – Converts chemical energy → electrical energy; EMF $E_{\text{cell}} > 0$.

Electrolytic Cell – Uses external source to drive non‑spontaneous reaction; $E_{\text{cell}} < 0$.

Cell Notation – Example: $\text{Zn}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(a_{\text{Zn}^{2+}})||\text{Cu}^{2+}(a_{\text{Cu}^{2+}})|\text{Cu}$.

  • EMF (Cell Potential): $E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{cathode}} - E_{\text{anode}}$ (V).
  • Relation with Gibbs free energy: $\Delta G = -nF E_{\text{cell}}$.
  • Nernst Equation (general): $E = E^{\circ} - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$.
  • At $25^{\circ}\text{C}$: $E = E^{\circ} - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q$ (V).
  • Equilibrium constant: $\log K = \frac{nE^{\circ}}{0.0592}$.
  • For gases $Q = \frac{P_{\text{products}}^{\nu}}{P_{\text{reactants}}^{\nu}}$ (atm).
  • Temperature effect: $E$ decreases with increase in $T$ if $\Delta G > 0$.
Half‑reaction$E^{\circ}$ (V)
Zn$^{2+}$ + 2e$^{-}$ → Zn$-0.76$
Cu$^{2+}$ + 2e$^{-}$ → Cu$+0.34$

Solved Example

Find $E_{\text{cell}}$ for $\text{Zn}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(0.010\,\text{M})||\text{Cu}^{2+}(1.0\,\text{M})|\text{Cu}$ at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$.

Standard EMF: $E^{\circ}_{\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\,\text{V}$.

Reaction quotient $Q = \frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]} = \frac{0.010}{1.0}=0.010$.

Using Nernst equation: $E = 1.10 - \frac{0.0592}{2}\log(0.010) = 1.10 - 0.0296(-2) = 1.10 + 0.0592 = 1.1592\,\text{V} \approx 1.16\,\text{V}$.

SI Units: EMF – volt (V), concentration – mol·L$^{-1}$, pressure – atm, $F = 96\,485\,\text{C mol}^{-1}$.

Standard electrode potential and Nernst equation

Standard Electrode Potential (E°) – The emf of a cell when all its components are in their standard states (1 M, 1 atm, 25 °C). Unit: $\\text{V}$.

Nernst Equation – Relates cell potential at any condition to its standard potential:

$$E = E^{\\circ} - \\frac{RT}{nF}\\ln Q$$

  • $R = 8.314\\,\\text{J mol}^{-1}\\text{K}^{-1}$
  • $T$ – temperature \in $\\text{K}$
  • $n$ – number of moles of electrons transferred
  • $F = 96485\\,\\text{C mol}^{-1}$
  • $Q$ – reaction quotient

At $25\\,^{\\circ}\\text{C}$, $\\frac{RT}{F}=0.0257\\,\\text{V}$, and the equation is often written as:

$$E = E^{\\circ} - \\frac{0.0592}{n}\\log_{10} Q$$

SymbolMeaningUnit
$E$Cell potential$\\text{V}$
$E^{\\circ}$Standard cell potential$\\text{V}$
$Q$Reaction quotient–
$n$Electrons transferred–

Example: For the half‑reaction $\\ce{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- -> Cu}$, $E^{\\circ}=+0.34\\,\\text{V}$. Calculate $E$ when $[\\ce{Cu^{2+}}]=0.010\\,\\text{M}$ at $25\\,^{\\circ}\\text{C}$.

Here $n=2$, $Q=\\frac{1}{[\\ce{Cu^{2+}}]}=\\frac{1}{0.010}=100$. Using the base‑10 form:

$$E = 0.34 - \\frac{0.0592}{2}\\log_{10} 100 = 0.34 - 0.0296\\times 2 = 0.34 - 0.0592 = 0.2808\\,\\text{V}$$

Thus the electrode potential drops \to $0.28\\,\\text{V}$ under the given conditions.

Galvanic and electrolytic cells – cell notation

Definitions

  • Galvanic (voltaic) cell: Spontaneous redox reaction that produces electrical energy.
  • Electrolytic cell: Non‑spontaneous redox reaction driven by external emf.

Cell notation

General form: $\\text{anode (oxidation)}\\;|\\;\\text{oxidized species (aq)}\\;||\\;\\text{reduced species (aq)}\\;|\\;\\text{cathode (reduction)}$

ComponentSymbol
Anode (oxidation)$\\text{M}|\\text{M}^{n+}$
Cathode (reduction)$\\text{M}^{n+}|\\text{M}$
Salt bridge$||$

Cell potential

  • Standard cell emf: $E^{\\circ}_{\\text{cell}} = E^{\\circ}_{\\text{cathode}} - E^{\\circ}_{\\text{anode}}$ (unit: $\\text{V}$).
  • Nernst equation (25 °C): $E = E^{\\circ} - \\frac{0.0592}{n}\\log Q$, where $n$ = electrons transferred, $Q$ = reaction quotient.

Solved example

Find $E_{\\text{cell}}$ for the cell $\\text{Zn}|\\text{Zn}^{2+}(0.01\\,\\text{M})||\\text{Cu}^{2+}(1\\,\\text{M})|\\text{Cu}$.

  • $E^{\\circ}_{\\text{Cu}^{2+}/\\text{Cu}} = +0.34\\,\\text{V}$, $E^{\\circ}_{\\text{Zn}^{2+}/\\text{Zn}} = -0.76\\,\\text{V}$.
  • $E^{\\circ}_{\\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\\,\\text{V}$.
  • $n = 2$, $Q = \\frac{[\\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\\text{Cu}^{2+}]} = \\frac{0.01}{1}=0.01$.
  • $E = 1.10 - \\frac{0.0592}{2}\\log(0.01) = 1.10 - 0.0296(-2) = 1.10 + 0.0592 = 1.1592\\,\\text{V} \\approx 1.16\\,\\text{V}$.

Thus the galvanic cell delivers about $1.16\\,\\text{V}$ under the given conditions.

Conductance of electrolytes, molar conductivity and Kohlrausch’s law

Conductance (G) and Conductivity (\kappa)

  • G = \frac{1}{R} \;(\text{S})
  • \kappa = \frac{G\,l}{A} \;(\text{S·cm}^{-1}) where l = distance between electrodes and A = cross‑sectional area.

Molar Conductivity (\Lambda_m) and Equivalent Conductivity (\Lambda_{eq})

  • \Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa}{c} \;(\text{S·cm}^2\text{·mol}^{-1}) where c = molarity (mol·cm^{-3}).
  • \Lambda_{eq} = \frac{\kappa\,1000}{N} \;(\text{S·cm}^2\text{·eq}^{-1}) where N = normality.

Kohlrausch’s Law (Strong Electrolytes)

  • \Lambda_m = \Lambda_m^{0} - K\sqrt{c}
  • \Lambda_m^{0}=\lambda_{+}^{0}+\lambda_{-}^{0} (sum of limiting ionic conductivities).

Units

  • \kappa: S·cm^{-1}
  • \Lambda_m: S·cm^{2}\text{·mol}^{-1}
  • \Lambda_{eq}: S·cm^{2}\text{·eq}^{-1}

Example

Determine the molar conductivity of 0.010 M NaCl solution. Cell constant $K=0.5\,\text{cm}^{-1}$, measured resistance $R=200\,\Omega$.

StepCalculation
1. Conductance$G=\frac{1}{R}=\frac{1}{200}=0.005\,\text{S}$
2. Conductivity$\kappa=G\times K=0.005\times0.5=0.0025\,\text{S·cm}^{-1}$
3. Molar conductivity$\Lambda_m=\frac{\kappa}{c}=\frac{0.0025}{0.010}=0.25\,\text{S·cm}^{2}\text{·mol}^{-1}$

Thus, $\Lambda_m=0.25\,\text{S·cm}^{2}\text{·mol}^{-1}$ for the given NaCl solution.

Faraday’s laws of electrolysis

Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

These laws relate the amount of substance liberated at an electrode to the quantity of electricity passed through the electrolyte.

  • First law: The mass ($m$) of a substance deposited or liberated is directly proportional to the charge ($Q$) passed. $m \\propto Q$.
  • Second law: For the same quantity of electricity, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E$). $\\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\\frac{E_1}{E_2}$.

From the definition of charge $Q = I t$ (where $I$ is current in A and $t$ in s), the quantitative form of the first law is

$$m = \\frac{Q \\; M}{F \\; n}=\\frac{I t \\; M}{F \\; n}$$

where $M$ = molar mass (kg·mol$^{-1}$), $n$ = valency, $F = 96\\,485\\,\\text{C mol}^{-1}$ (Faraday constant).

QuantityFormulaSI Unit
Charge$Q = I t$C (coulomb)
Molar mass$M$kg·mol$^{-1}$
Faraday constant$F = 96\\,485\\,\\text{C mol}^{-1}$C·mol$^{-1}$
Equivalent weight$E = \\frac{M}{n}$kg·eq$^{-1}$

Example: In the electrolysis of molten $\\mathrm{NaCl}$, a current of $2.0\\,\\text{A}$ is passed for $30\\,\\text{min}$. Find the mass of Na deposited at the cathode.

Solution:

$I = 2.0\\,\\text{A},\\; t = 30\\,\\text{min}=1800\\,\\text{s}$
$Q = I t = 2.0 \\times 1800 = 3600\\,\\text{C}$
For Na, $M = 23\\,\\text{g mol}^{-1}=0.023\\,\\text{kg mol}^{-1},\\; n = 1$
$m = \\frac{Q M}{F n}=\\frac{3600 \\times 0.023}{96\\,485}=8.6\\times10^{-4}\\,\\text{kg}=0.86\\,\\text{g}$.

Thus $0.86\\,\\text{g}$ of Na is deposited.

Electrochemical series and its applications

Electrochemical Series – An ordered list of half‑cells arranged according to their standard electrode potentials $E^\circ$ (unit: V). The more positive $E^\circ$, the stronger the oxidising agent; the more negative, the stronger the reducing agent. Potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) which is defined as $0\,\text{V}$.

Standard Electrode Potential

  • Defined for the half‑reaction $\text{Red}+n e^-\rightarrow\text{Ox}$ under standard conditions (1 M, 1 atm, $25^\circ\text{C}$).
  • $E^\circ = -\frac{\Delta G^\circ}{nF}$ where $\Delta G^\circ$ is the standard Gibbs free energy (J mol$^{-1}$), $n$ is the number of electrons transferred and $F = 96\,485\,\text{C\,mol}^{-1}$.

Key Formulas

ExpressionMeaning
$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$Cell EMF under standard conditions
$\Delta G^\circ = -nF E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$Relation between free energy and cell EMF
$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q$Nernst equation at $25^\circ\text{C}$ ( $Q$ = reaction quotient )
$K = 10^{\frac{nE^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.0592}}$Equilibrium constant from standard EMF

Applications

  • Predicting spontaneity: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$ ⇒ spontaneous, $<0$ ⇒ non‑spontaneous.
  • Corrosion protection: a metal with lower $E^\circ$ (e.g., Zn, $-0.76\,\text{V}$) can be used as a sacrificial anode for iron.
  • Design of galvanic cells – selection of electrode pairs to obtain desired voltage.
  • Electrolytic processes – external voltage $E_{\text{ext}}$ must exceed $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ to drive a non‑spontaneous reaction.
  • Estimating equilibrium constants using $K = 10^{\frac{nE^\circ_{\text{cell}}}{0.0592}}$.

Solved Example

Find $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ for the reaction $\text{Zn}(s)+\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)\rightarrow\text{Zn}^{2+}(aq)+\text{Cu}(s)$. Given $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}}=+0.34\,\text{V}$ and $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}}=-0.76\,\text{V}$.

Solution: Cu$^{2+}$/Cu acts as the cathode, Zn$^{2+}$/Zn as the anode.
$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\,\text{V}$. Since $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} > 0$, the reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions.

Corrosion and its prevention

Corrosion: Spontaneous electro‑chemical deterioration of a metal in its environment, resulting in formation of a more stable compound (usually oxide, hydroxide or sulfide).

Electro‑chemical basis

  • Anodic reaction (oxidation): $\\text{M} \\rightarrow \\text{M}^{n+} + n e^{-}$
  • Cathodic reaction (reduction): $\\text{O}_2 + 2 H_2O + 4 e^{-} \\rightarrow 4 OH^{-}$ (in neutral/alkaline media)

Corrosion rate formula

ParameterExpression
Corrosion rate (CR)$\\displaystyle \\text{CR}=\\frac{I_{corr}}{n F A}$
SI unit$\\text{kg m}^{-2}\\text{s}^{-1}$ (or $\\mu\\text{m yr}^{-1}$)

Prevention techniques

  • Coating: Paint, galvanisation, enamel – isolates metal from corrosive agents.
  • Cathodic protection:
    • Sacrificial anode (e.g., Zn, Mg) – anodic dissolution of the more active metal.
    • Impressed current – external DC source forces metal to act as cathode.
  • Alloying: Adding Cr, Ni, Mo to form passive film (stainless steel).
  • Inhibitors: Substances that adsorb on metal surface, e.g., $\\text{NaNO}_2$, $\\text{Na}_2\\text{S}_2\\text{O}_3$.

Solved example

Given: Corrosion current $I_{corr}=2.5\\times10^{-3}$ A for a steel coupon of area $A=0.01\\,\\text{m}^2$. For iron, $n=2$.

Calculate $\\text{CR}$.

Solution:

$\\displaystyle \\text{CR}=\\frac{I_{corr}}{n F A}=\\frac{2.5\\times10^{-3}}{2\\times96\\,\\,485\\times0.01}=1.30\\times10^{-6}\\,\\text{kg m}^{-2}\\text{s}^{-1}$ which corresponds to $\\approx0.04\\,\\mu\\text{m yr}^{-1}$.

Applications of electrochemistry (electroplating, batteries, fuel cells)

Applications of Electrochemistry

  • Electroplating – deposition of a metal on a substrate using an external source of electricity. Useful for corrosion resistance, decorative finish, wear resistance.
  • Batteries – devices that convert chemical energy into electrical energy. Classified as primary (non‑rechargeable) and secondary (rechargeable). Typical examples: Zn–Cu (primary), Lead‑acid, Ni‑Cd, Li‑ion (secondary).
  • Fuel Cells – electrochemical cells that convert the chemical energy of a fuel (commonly H₂) and an oxidant (O₂) directly into electricity with water as the only by‑product.

Key Formulas

ConceptFormulaSI Unit

Battery capacity$C = I t$Ah
Energy delivered$W = V I t = V C$J

Solved Example

Calculate the mass of Cu deposited when a current of $2\\,\\text{A}$ is passed for $30\\,\\text{min}$ through a CuSO₄ solution. ($M_{Cu}=63.5\\,\\text{g mol}^{-1}$, $n=2$.)

Solution:

$t = 30\\,\\text{min}=1800\\,\\text{s}$

Using Faraday’s law, $$m = \\frac{M I t}{n F}=\\frac{63.5\\times 2\\times 1800}{2\\times 96485}=\\frac{228600}{192970}\\approx 1.18\\,\\text{g}$$

Thus $1.18\\,\\text{g}$ of Cu is plated on the cathode.
