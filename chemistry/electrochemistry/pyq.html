Practice Question 1 (5 marks)

Define the rate of a chemical reaction and derive the rate law for a second‑order reaction. Include the expression for the rate constant.

Marking Scheme

  • 2 marks – Definition of reaction rate (e.g., change in concentration per unit time).
  • 2 marks – Derivation of rate law for a second‑order reaction, showing steps leading to $$r = k[A]^2$$ or $$r = k[A][B]$$.
  • 1 mark – Explanation of the rate constant $k$ and its units.

Practice Question 2 (5 marks)

Explain the concept of activation energy and describe how it influences the reaction rate. Provide an example of how temperature affects the rate using the Arrhenius equation.

Marking Scheme

  • 2 marks – Definition of activation energy ($E_a$).
  • 1 mark – Discussion of its effect on the rate (higher $E_a$ → lower rate).
  • 1 mark – Inclusion of the Arrhenius equation $$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$.
  • 1 mark – Example showing temperature dependence (e.g., doubling temperature increases rate).

Case Study 1 – Corrosion of Iron in Urban Environments

Passage: A municipal water supply in a coastal city contains dissolved oxygen and chloride ions at concentrations of about $8 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ and $5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$ respectively. Iron pipelines used for water distribution are observed to develop reddish‑brown rust patches after six months of service. The overall corrosion reaction is often represented as: $$\text{Fe(s)} + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O(l)} \rightarrow \text{Fe(OH)}_2(s)$$ In the presence of chloride ions, the protective $\text{Fe(OH)}_2$ layer is destabilised, leading to the formation of soluble complexes such as $\text{FeCl}_2$. The corrosion rate, $r$, can be expressed by Faraday’s law: $$r = \frac{M \, i}{nF}$$ where $M$ is the molar mass of iron ($55.85\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$), $i$ is the corrosion current density, $n$ is the number of electrons transferred (2 for Fe → Fe$^{2+}$), and $F$ is Faraday’s constant ($96500\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$). Laboratory measurements give a corrosion current density of $i = 2.5\ \mu\text{A cm}^{-2}$ for the pipeline steel under the given water chemistry.

  1. Calculate the corrosion rate of iron in $\text{mm yr}^{-1}$ using the given current density. (Take density of iron as $7.87\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$.)
  2. Write the half‑cell reactions occurring at the anodic and cathodic sites in the presence of dissolved oxygen.
  3. Explain how chloride ions accelerate the corrosion process, referring to the concept of pitting corrosion.
  4. Suggest two practical measures that can be adopted by the municipal corporation to reduce the corrosion of iron pipelines.

Solution

(A) Calculation of corrosion rate

First convert the current density to amperes per square centimetre:

$$i = 2.5\ \mu\text{A cm}^{-2}=2.5\times10^{-6}\ \text{A cm}^{-2}$$

Using Faraday’s law:

$$r_{\text{mass}}=\frac{M\,i}{nF}= \frac{55.85\times2.5\times10^{-6}}{2\times96500}\ \text{g cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$$ $$r_{\text{mass}}=7.24\times10^{-10}\ \text{g cm}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}$$

Convert mass loss to thickness loss using the density ($\rho =7.87\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$):

$$r_{\text{thick}}=\frac{r_{\text{mass}}}{\rho}= \frac{7.24\times10^{-10}}{7.87}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}=9.20\times10^{-11}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}$$

Convert to $\text{mm yr}^{-1}$:

$$1\ \text{cm}=10\ \text{mm},\qquad 1\ \text{yr}=3.1536\times10^{7}\ \text{s}$$ $$r_{\text{mm yr}^{-1}}=9.20\times10^{-11}\times10\times3.1536\times10^{7}=0.029\ \text{mm yr}^{-1}$$

Thus the corrosion rate is approximately $0.03\ \text{mm yr}^{-1}$.

(B) Half‑cell reactions

Anodic (oxidation) reaction:

$$\text{Fe(s)} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$$

Cathodic (reduction) reaction in neutral/alkaline water with dissolved oxygen:

$$\text{O}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-(aq)$$

Overall reaction (by multiplying the anodic reaction by 2 and adding to the cathodic reaction) yields the formation of $\text{Fe(OH)}_2$ as shown in the passage.

(C) Role of chloride ions – pitting corrosion

  • Chloride ions are aggressive anions that adsorb onto the metal surface and disrupt the protective $\text{Fe(OH)}_2$ film.
  • They penetrate the passive layer at microscopic defects, creating localised anodic sites where Fe → Fe$^{2+}$ proceeds rapidly.
  • The resulting metal dissolution produces a small pit; the geometry of the pit causes the electric field to concentrate, further accelerating metal loss – a self‑propagating process known as pitting.
  • Complex formation such as $\text{FeCl}_2$ keeps Fe$^{2+}$ in solution, preventing re‑precipitation of a protective layer.

(D) Practical mitigation measures

  1. Cathodic protection: Apply a sacrificial anode (e.g., zinc or magnesium) to the pipeline so that the external metal corrodes preferentially, protecting the iron.
  2. Water treatment: Reduce dissolved oxygen by deaeration and limit chloride concentration through ion‑exchange softening or reverse‑osmosis, thereby decreasing the driving force for both uniform and pitting corrosion.
  3. Additional measures (optional): Use protective coatings (epoxy, bitumen) and select corrosion‑resistant alloys for critical sections.

Case Study 2 – Electroplating of Silver on Glassware for Antimicrobial Applications

Passage: A pharmaceutical company wishes to produce glass vials with a thin, uniform silver coating to exploit silver’s antimicrobial properties. The coating is to be deposited by electrolytic plating using a silver nitrate solution ($\text{AgNO}_3$) of concentration $0.01\ \text{mol L}^{-1}$, containing $0.1\ \text{M}$ potassium nitrate ($\text{KNO}_3$) as supporting electrolyte. The plating bath is maintained at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and the glass vials are first rendered conductive by a thin layer of tin oxide. The plating cell uses a pure silver anode and the vials act as cathodes. A constant current of $0.5\ \text{A}$ is passed for $180\ \text{s}$ for each vial. The overall cathodic reaction is: $$\text{Ag}^+(aq) + e^- \rightarrow \text{Ag(s)}$$ The efficiency of the process is reported to be $85\%$ due to side reactions (hydrogen evolution). The company requires a coating thickness of $0.5\ \mu\text{m}$ on the inner surface of a vial having a cylindrical shape of radius $1.2\ \text{cm}$ and height $10\ \text{cm}$.

  1. Determine the mass of silver deposited on one vial during the plating operation.
  2. Calculate the theoretical coating thickness obtained (in $\mu\text{m}$) and comment on whether the required $0.5\ \mu\text{m}$ is achieved.
  3. Write the half‑cell reactions occurring at the anode and cathode, and explain why the silver anode is preferred over an inert anode.
  4. Discuss two environmental or safety considerations associated with the use of silver plating baths and propose mitigation strategies.

Solution

(A) Mass of silver deposited

First calculate the total charge passed:

$$Q = I t = 0.5\ \text{A} \times 180\ \text{s}=90\ \text{C}$$

Effective charge contributing to silver deposition (85 % efficiency):

$$Q_{\text{eff}} = 0.85 \times 90 = 76.5\ \text{C}$$

Using Faraday’s law, the amount (in moles) of Ag deposited is:

$$n_{\text{Ag}} = \frac{Q_{\text{eff}}}{nF}= \frac{76.5}{1 \times 96500}=7.93\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$$

Mass of silver:

$$m_{\text{Ag}} = n_{\text{Ag}} \times M_{\text{Ag}} = 7.93\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol} \times 107.87\ \text{g mol}^{-1}=0.0856\ \text{g}$$

(B) Theoretical coating thickness

Surface area of the inner cylindrical wall (ignoring the bottom and top for simplicity):

$$A = 2\pi r h = 2\pi (1.2\ \text{cm})(10\ \text{cm}) = 75.4\ \text{cm}^2$$

Convert area to $\text{m}^2$ for consistency:

$$A = 75.4\ \text{cm}^2 = 7.54\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$$

Density of silver $\rho_{\text{Ag}} = 10.49\ \text{g cm}^{-3}=1.049\times10^{4}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

Volume of silver deposited:

$$V = \frac{m}{\rho}= \frac{0.0856\ \text{g}}{10.49\ \text{g cm}^{-3}} = 8.16\times10^{-3}\ \text{cm}^3 = 8.16\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}^3$$

Thickness $t$ obtained by dividing volume by area:

$$t = \frac{V}{A}= \frac{8.16\times10^{-9}}{7.54\times10^{-3}} = 1.08\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}=1.08\ \mu\text{m}$$

Thus the actual coating thickness is about $1.1\ \mu\text{m}$, which exceeds the required $0.5\ \mu\text{m}$. The process therefore comfortably meets the specification.
