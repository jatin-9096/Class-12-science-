1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Concepts

Electrochemistry deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. An electrochemical cell is a device in which a redox reaction occurs spontaneously (galvanic cell) or is driven by an external source of electricity (electrolytic cell). The cell consists of two half‑cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. The electrode is a solid conductor that facilitates electron transfer, while the electrolyte is a medium (aqueous solution or molten salt) that allows ionic conduction. The point where the two half‑cells are linked is the salt bridge or porous membrane, which maintains electrical neutrality by permitting the flow of ions. The cell potential, denoted $E_{\text{cell}}$, is the difference in electrode potentials between the cathode (reduction) and anode (oxidation). Standard electrode potentials, $E^\circ$, are measured under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 25 °C) and tabulated in the electrochemical series. Understanding these basic entities is essential before delving into the quantitative laws that govern electrochemical processes.

Chemistry · Core Law
Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis

Michael Faraday formulated two fundamental laws that relate the amount of substance transformed at an electrode to the quantity of electricity passed. First Law: The mass ($m$) of a substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the total electric charge ($Q$) passed through the cell. Mathematically, $$m = Z \, Q$$ where $Z$ is the electrochemical equivalent of the substance. Second Law: For a given quantity of electric charge, the masses of different substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights ($E$). This can be expressed as $$\frac{m_1}{E_1} = \frac{m_2}{E_2} = \frac{Q}{F}$$ where $F = 96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$ is the Faraday constant. Combining these laws yields the practical formula $$m = \frac{M \, I \, t}{n \, F}$$ where $M$ is the molar mass, $I$ the current in amperes, $t$ the time in seconds, and $n$ the number of electrons exchanged per ion. These relationships are the backbone of quantitative electrochemistry, enabling calculations of deposition mass, required charge, and current efficiency in industrial processes such as electroplating and metal refining.

Chemistry · Core Law
Thermodynamic Intuition: Nernst Equation and Cell Potentials

The spontaneity of a redox reaction in an electrochemical cell is quantified by its Gibbs free energy change, $\Delta G$, which is linked to the cell potential by $$\Delta G = -n F E_{\text{cell}}$$. Under non‑standard conditions, the cell potential deviates from its standard value $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ according to the Nernst equation: $$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log Q$$ where $Q$ is the reaction quotient expressed in terms of ion activities. This equation provides a powerful intuition: increasing the concentration of reactants (or decreasing that of products) makes $Q$ smaller, thereby increasing $E_{\text{cell}}$ and enhancing the driving force of the reaction. Conversely, accumulation of products reduces the cell potential, eventually halting the reaction when $E_{\text{cell}}$ reaches zero (equilibrium). The Nernst equation also explains why concentration cells generate a potential even though the electrodes are made of the same material; the potential arises solely from a concentration gradient, illustrating the deep connection between chemical potential and electrical work.

Chemistry · Core Law
Exceptions and Real‑World Deviations

Ideal electrochemical behavior assumes that reactions occur under reversible conditions, with negligible kinetic barriers. In practice, several factors cause deviations. Overpotential is the extra voltage required to drive a non‑spontaneous reaction at a practical rate; it arises from activation energy barriers, concentration polarization, and resistance of the electrolyte. For example, the hydrogen evolution reaction on a platinum electrode exhibits a measurable overpotential despite its favorable thermodynamics. Concentration polarization occurs when the rate of ion transport to the electrode surface cannot keep up with the rate of electron transfer, leading to a depletion layer that reduces $E_{\text{cell}}$. Additionally, side reactions such as corrosion, gas evolution, or formation of passivating films can consume charge without contributing to the desired product, lowering current efficiency. Temperature variations also affect $E^\circ$ and $K_{\text{eq}}$, thereby altering the Nernst term. Recognizing these exceptions is crucial for designing efficient industrial cells, where engineers must mitigate overpotentials through catalyst selection, agitation, and temperature control.

Chemistry · Core Law
Worked Example: Mass of Copper Deposited by Electrolysis

Consider an electrolytic cell containing a 0.10 M $\ce{CuSO4}$ solution with a copper cathode. A constant current of $2.00\ \text{A}$ is passed for $30\ \text{min}$. Calculate (a) the mass of copper deposited on the cathode, and (b) the volume of $\ce{H2}$ gas evolved at the anode if the anode reaction is water oxidation.

Solution: (a) For copper deposition, the half‑reaction is $\ce{Cu^{2+} + 2e^- -> Cu(s)}$, so $n = 2$. Using Faraday’s law, $$m = \frac{M I t}{n F}$$ where $M_{\ce{Cu}} = 63.55\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, $I = 2.00\ \text{A}$, $t = 30\ \text{min} \times 60\ \text{s min}^{-1} = 1800\ \text{s}$. Substituting, $$m = \frac{63.55 \times 2.00 \times 1800}{2 \times 96\,485} \approx 1.18\ \text{g}.$$ (b) At the anode, water is oxidized: $\ce{2H2O -> O2 + 4H+ + 4e^-}$. The total charge passed is $Q = I t = 2.00 \times 1800 = 3600\ \text{C}$. The number of moles of electrons is $Q/F = 3600/96\,485 \approx 0.0373\ \text{mol e^-}$. For every 4 mol e⁻, 2 mol H₂ are produced in the accompanying reduction of water at the cathode (if the cell were reversed). However, in this configuration only $\ce{O2}$ evolves at the anode; the amount of $\ce{H2}$ generated elsewhere can be calculated similarly. Assuming a parallel cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction $\ce{2H+ + 2e^- -> H2}$, the moles of $\ce{H2}$ formed would be $0.0373/2 = 0.0187\ \text{mol}$. At STP, $V = nRT/P = 0.0187 \times 22.4\ \text{L} \approx 0.42\ \text{L}$. This example illustrates the direct application of Faraday’s law and the importance of stoichiometric coefficients in electrochemical calculations.

1. Ideal Gas Law from Kinetic Theory

Starting from the kinetic theory of gases, we consider a monatomic ideal gas in a cubic container of side $L$. The pressure exerted on a wall is due to momentum transfer during collisions. For a single molecule of mass $m$ moving with velocity components $(v_x, v_y, v_z)$, the change in momentum during a collision with the wall perpendicular to the $x$‑axis is $2m v_x$. The time between successive collisions with the same wall is $\Delta t = 2L/v_x$. Thus the average force exerted by this molecule on the wall is $$F = \frac{2m v_x}{\Delta t} = \frac{m v_x^2}{L}.$$ Summing over all $N$ molecules and averaging over all directions (so that $\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \langle v_y^2 \rangle = \langle v_z^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3}\langle v^2 \rangle$), the total force on the wall becomes $$F_{\text{tot}} = \frac{N m \langle v^2 \rangle}{3L}.$$ Pressure is force per unit area ($A=L^2$), hence $$P = \frac{F_{\text{tot}}}{A} = \frac{N m \langle v^2 \rangle}{3L^3} = \frac{N m \langle v^2 \rangle}{3V}.$$ The kinetic energy per molecule is $\frac{1}{2}m \langle v^2 \rangle$, and for an ideal gas the equipartition theorem gives $\frac{1}{2}m \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{3}{2}kT$. Substituting this into the pressure expression yields $$P = \frac{N k T}{V}.$$ Introducing Avogadro’s number $N_A$ and the number of moles $n = N/N_A$, we obtain the familiar ideal gas law $$PV = nRT,$$ where $R = N_A k$ is the universal gas constant.

2. Van der Waals Equation

The ideal gas law fails for real gases at high pressures or low temperatures because it neglects intermolecular forces and finite molecular size. Van der Waals introduced two corrections: a volume correction $b$ and a pressure correction $a$. The corrected volume is $V - Nb$, where $b$ represents the excluded volume per mole. The pressure correction accounts for attractive forces, reducing the effective pressure by an amount proportional to the square of the molar concentration. The corrected pressure is $P + a (n/V)^2$. Thus the Van der Waals equation reads $$\left(P + a\frac{n^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT.$$ For a single mole ($n=1$), this simplifies to $$\left(P + \frac{a}{V^2}\right)(V - b) = RT.$$ The constants $a$ and $b$ are empirically determined for each gas and have units $a: \text{Pa}\,\text{m}^6\text{mol}^{-2}$, $b: \text{m}^3\text{mol}^{-1}$.

3. Rate Law from Collision Theory

Consider a bimolecular elementary reaction $$A + B \xrightarrow{k} \text{Products}.$$ The rate of reaction is proportional to the number of effective collisions per unit time. The collision frequency $Z_{AB}$ for a gas mixture is $$Z_{AB} = \sigma_{AB} \sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi \mu_{AB}}} \frac{N_A}{V} [A][B],$$ where $\sigma_{AB} = \pi (r_A + r_B)^2$ is the collision cross‑section, $\mu_{AB} = \frac{m_A m_B}{m_A + m_B}$ is the reduced mass, and $[A]$, $[B]$ are concentrations. The steric factor $\kappa$ (dimensionless) accounts for orientation requirements. The rate constant is then $$k = \kappa Z_{AB} \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),$$ leading to the Arrhenius form (see next section). The elementary rate law follows directly: $$-\frac{d[A]}{dt} = \frac{d[B]}{dt} = k[A][B].$$

4. Arrhenius Equation

From collision theory, the temperature dependence of the rate constant arises from the fraction of collisions that possess energy equal to or greater than the activation energy $E_a$. The Maxwell‑Boltzmann distribution gives this fraction as $\exp(-E_a/RT)$. Thus $$k = A \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{RT}\right),$$ where $A$ is the pre‑exponential factor (frequency factor). Taking natural logarithms yields the linear Arrhenius plot: $$\ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{R}\frac{1}{T}.$$ The slope of the plot gives $-E_a/R$ and the intercept gives $\ln A$.

5. Nernst Equation

For a half‑cell reaction $$\text{Ox} + ne^- \leftrightarrow \text{Red},$$ the cell potential $E$ is related to the standard potential $E^\circ$ and the activities of oxidised and reduced species by the Nernst equation: $$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln \frac{a_{\text{Red}}}{a_{\text{Ox}}}.$$ For dilute solutions, activities are approximated by concentrations, giving $$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log_{10}\frac{[\text{Red}]}{[\text{Ox}]}\quad (T=298\,\text{K}).$$ This relation is fundamental for electrochemical calculations.

6. Gibbs Free Energy and Equilibrium Constant

The change in Gibbs free energy for a reaction at constant temperature and pressure is $$\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT \ln Q,$$ where $Q$ is the reaction quotient. At equilibrium, $\Delta G = 0$ and $Q = K$, the equilibrium constant. Therefore $$\Delta G^\circ = -RT \ln K.$$ For a general reaction $$\nu_A A + \nu_B B \leftrightarrow \nu_C C + \nu_D D,$$ the equilibrium constant in terms of activities is $$K = \frac{a_C^{\nu_C} a_D^{\nu_D}}{a_A^{\nu_A} a_B^{\nu_B}}.$$ This derivation links thermodynamics to chemical equilibrium.
