Carbohydrates – classification, structure and isomerism

Definition: Carbohydrates are organic compounds with the general formula $C_n(H_2O)_n$ (or $C_nH_{2n}O_n$) containing carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed 1:2:1 ratio. They serve as primary energy sources and structural components in living organisms.

Classification:

  • Monosaccharides – simplest units (e.g., glucose, fructose). Formula $C_6H_{12}O_6$.
  • Disaccharides – two monosaccharide units linked by a glycosidic bond (e.g., sucrose, maltose).
  • Polysaccharides – long chains of monosaccharide residues (e.g., starch, cellulose, glycogen).

Structural Forms:

  • Open‑chain (acyclic) – linear aldehyde (aldose) or ketone (ketose) form.
  • Cyclic – hemiacetal/hemiacetal formation gives furanose (5‑membered) or pyranose (6‑membered) rings.

Isomerism:

  • Structural isomerism – chain, positional and functional‑group isomers.
  • Stereoisomerism –
    • Enantiomers: non‑superimposable mirror images (D‑ and L‑forms). Determined by configuration at the highest numbered chiral carbon.
    • Di­astereomers: stereoisomers not related as mirror images.
    • Anomers: cyclic forms differing at the anomeric carbon ($\alpha$ or $\beta$).

Key Formulae:

  • Molar mass of a carbohydrate = $12n + 2n + 16n = 180\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$ for $C_6H_{12}O_6$.
  • Mass‑% of carbon = $\frac{12n}{180}\times100\% = 40\%$ (for glucose).

SI Units: Molecular weight – g·mol$^{-1}$; concentration – mol·L$^{-1}$.

Solved Example: Calculate moles of glucose in $180\,\text{g}$ sample.

Given molar mass $M_{\text{glucose}} = 180\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$,\[ n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{180\,\text{g}}{180\,\text{g mol}^{-1}} = 1\,\text{mol} \]Thus $180\,\text{g}$ of glucose corresponds to $1\,\text{mol}$ (or $6.02\times10^{23}$ molecules).

Monosaccharides – important examples and reactions

Monosaccharides – simplest carbohydrates containing a single carbonyl group and $C_nH_{2n}O_n$ ( $n = 3$ to $7$ ). They are classified as aldoses (aldehyde group) or ketoses (ketone group).

Important examples

Common NameFormulaType
Glyceraldehyde$C_3H_6O_3$Aldose
Dihydroxyacetone$C_3H_6O_3$Ketose
Glucose (D‑glucose)$C_6H_{12}O_6$Aldose
Fructose$C_6H_{12}O_6$Ketose
Ribose$C_5H_{10}O_5$Aldose

Key reactions

  • Oxidation (to aldonic/uronic acids): $R-CHO + [O] \rightarrow R-COOH$ (aldose) or $R-CO-CH_2OH + [O] \rightarrow R-COOH$ (ketose).
  • Reduction (to alditols): $R-CHO + 2[H] \rightarrow R-CH_2OH$ giving e.g., glucose \rightarrow sorbitol.
  • Formation of glycosidic bond: $R-OH + HO-R' \rightarrow R-O-R' + H_2O$ (condensation).
  • Isomerisation (Lobry de Bruyn–Alberda van Ekenstein): Aldose \leftrightarrow Ketose via enediol intermediate.

Solved example

Calculate moles of glucose in $180\ \text{g}$ sample. Molar mass of glucose $M = 180\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

$$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{180\ \text{g}}{180\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 1\ \text{mol}$$

Thus $1\ \text{mol}$ of glucose contains $6.02\times10^{23}$ molecules.

Disaccharides and polysaccharides – structure and functions

Disaccharides – A disaccharide is a carbohydrate formed by a glycosidic linkage between two monosaccharide units. The linkage is denoted as $\alpha$ or $\beta$ depending on the anomeric carbon configuration and the carbon numbers involved (e.g., $\alpha$‑1,4, $\beta$‑1,2). Common examples are sucrose, lactose and maltose.

  • Sucrose: $\alpha$‑D‑glucose $\leftrightarrow$ $\beta$‑D‑fructose via $\alpha$‑1,2 bond; non‑reducing.
  • Lactose: $\beta$‑D‑galactose $\leftrightarrow$ $\alpha$‑D‑glucose via $\beta$‑1,4 bond; reducing.
  • Maltose: $\alpha$‑D‑glucose $\leftrightarrow$ $\alpha$‑D‑glucose via $\alpha$‑1,4 bond; reducing.

Polysaccharides – Long chains of monosaccharide units linked by glycosidic bonds. They are classified as homopolysaccharides (e.g., starch, glycogen, cellulose) or heteropolysaccharides (e.g., chitin). Key parameters:

  • Degree of Polymerisation (DP): $\text{DP}=\frac{M_n}{M_0}$, where $M_n$ is the number‑average molar mass (g·mol$^{-1}$) and $M_0$ is the monomer molar mass (g·mol$^{-1}$).
  • Average Molar Mass: $M_n=\frac{\sum n_i M_i}{\sum n_i}$.
PolysaccharideMonomerLinkageFunction
Starch (amylose)\alpha$‑D‑glucose$\alpha$‑1,4Energy reserve \in plants
Glycogen\alpha$‑D‑glucose$\alpha$‑1,4 \text{(branches $\alpha$‑1,6)}Energy reserve in animals
Cellulose\beta$‑D‑glucose$\beta$‑1,4Structural support \in plants

Solved Example

Calculate the degree of polymerisation of amylose if its number‑average molar mass $M_n = 1.2\times10^5\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$ and the monomer molar mass $M_0 = 180\,\text{g mol}^{-1}$.

Solution:

  1. Write the DP formula: $\text{DP}=\frac{M_n}{M_0}$.
  2. Substitute the values: $\text{DP}=\frac{1.2\times10^5}{180}$.
  3. Perform the division: $\text{DP}=\frac{1.2\times10^5}{1.8\times10^2}=\frac{1.2}{1.8}\times10^{3}=0.667\times10^{3}\approx 667$.

Thus, the amylose chain contains roughly $667$ glucose units. The result is dimensionless, as DP is a count of monomer units.

Proteins – amino acids, peptide bond and levels of organization

Proteins – Amino Acids, Peptide Bond & Levels of Organization

  • Amino acid: An organic molecule containing $\\alpha$‑carbon bonded to $\\text{–COOH}$, $\\text{–NH}_2$, a hydrogen and a variable $R$‑group.
  • General formula: $\\text{NH}_2\\text{–CH}(R)\\text{–COOH}$ (molar mass in $\\text{g\cdot mol}^{-1}$).
  • Peptide bond: Covalent $\\text{C–N}$ bond formed by condensation (dehydration) between $\\text{–COOH}$ of one amino acid and $\\text{–NH}_2$ of another, releasing $\\text{H}_2\\text{O}$.
ReactionProducts
$\\text{Amino acid}_1 + \\text{Amino acid}_2$$\\text{Dipeptide} + \\text{H}_2\\text{O}$

Levels of protein organization

  1. Primary – sequence of amino acids.
  2. Secondary – $\\alpha$‑helix, $\\beta$‑sheet (hydrogen bonding).
  3. Tertiary – 3‑D folding (disulphide bridges, hydrophobic interactions).
  4. Quaternary – association of multiple polypeptide chains.

Example: A protein has a molar mass of $5.0\\times10^3\\,\\text{g\cdot mol}^{-1}$ and is composed solely of glycine ($M_{\\text{Gly}} = 75\\,\\text{g\cdot mol}^{-1}$). Find the number of peptide bonds.

Solution:

  1. Number of residues $n = \\frac{M_{\\text{protein}}}{M_{\\text{Gly}}}=\\frac{5.0\\times10^3}{75}=66.7\\approx 67$.
  2. Peptide bonds $= n-1 = 66$.

Thus the protein contains $66$ peptide bonds.

Nucleic acids – DNA and RNA structure, nucleotides and genetic information flow

Definition: Nucleic acids are high‑molecular‑weight polymers of nucleotides that store and transmit genetic information.

Basic unit – Nucleotide

  • Three components: a nitrogenous base (purine = A,G; pyrimidine = C,T,U), a five‑carbon sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), and one or more phosphate groups.
  • General formula: $\text{Base–Sugar–(PO}_4\text{)}_n$.

DNA vs RNA

  • DNA: double‑helix, antiparallel strands, deoxyribose, bases A,T,G,C.
  • RNA: single‑stranded, ribose, bases A,U,G,C.

Base‑pairing rules (Chargaff’s rule)

  • $[A]=[T]$, $[G]=[C]$ in DNA.
  • In RNA, $[A]=[U]$, $[G]=[C]$.

Average molar mass of a nucleotide

  • DNA: $330\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$
  • RNA: $340\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$

Genetic information flow (Central Dogma)

  • DNA $\xrightarrow{\text{transcription}}$ RNA $\xrightarrow{\text{translation}}$ Protein.

Example: A DNA fragment weighs $10\ \mu\text{g}$ and contains 30 % A, 30 % T, 20 % G, 20 % C. Find the number of nucleotides.

Vitamins – classification, functions and deficiency disorders

Vitamins – Definition: Organic compounds required in minute amounts for normal metabolism, cannot be synthesized in adequate quantity by the human body.

Classification (by solubility):

Fat‑solubleWater‑soluble
Vitamin $A$ ($C_{40}H_{56}O$), Vitamin $D$ ($C_{27}H_{44}O$), Vitamin $E$ ($C_{29}H_{50}O_{2}$), Vitamin $K$ ($C_{31}H_{46}O_{2}$)Vitamin $C$ ($C_6H_8O_6$), B‑complex (B$_1$, B$_2$, B$_3$, B$_5$, B$_6$, B$_7$, B$_9$, B$_12$)

Absorption & Storage: Fat‑soluble vitamins are incorporated into micelles, absorbed with dietary lipids and transported as chylomicrons; they are stored in liver and adipose tissue. Water‑soluble vitamins are absorbed by active transport in the proximal small intestine and excess is eliminated in urine.

Key Functions:

  • Vitamin $A$: Vision, epithelial integrity, immune modulation.
  • Vitamin $D$: Calcium‑phosphate homeostasis, bone mineralisation.
  • Vitamin $E$: Antioxidant, protects membrane lipids.
  • Vitamin $K$: $\gamma$‑carboxylation of clotting factors.
  • Vitamin $C$: Co‑factor for hydroxylases, enhances iron absorption, antioxidant.
  • B‑complex: Co‑enzymes in carbohydrate, protein and lipid metabolism.

Deficiency Disorders (selected):

  • Vitamin $A$ – Night blindness, xerophthalmia.
  • Vitamin $D$ – Rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults).
  • Vitamin $C$ – Scurvy: gum bleeding, impaired collagen synthesis.
  • Vitamin $B_1$ – Beriberi: peripheral neuropathy, cardiac failure.
  • Vitamin $B_9$ – Megaloblastic anaemia, neural‑tube defects.

Toxicity: Because fat‑soluble vitamins accumulate, excess intake may cause hypervitaminosis (e.g., $A$, $D$). Water‑soluble vitamins are generally excreted, so toxicity is rare, though very high doses of vitamin $C$ may cause gastrointestinal upset.

Solved Example:

Daily Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) of Vitamin $C$ = $60\ \text{mg}$. One orange provides $50\ \text{mg}$. How many oranges are needed to meet RDA?

Solution: Required number $= \dfrac{60\ \text{mg}}{50\ \text{mg/orange}} = 1.2$ oranges. Hence, at least $2$ oranges are required to satisfy the RDA.

Lipids – classification, structure and biological importance

Definition: Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic biomolecules composed mainly of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, which are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents.

Classification

TypeExamples
Simple lipidsFats & oils (triacylglycerols), waxes
Compound lipidsPhospholipids, glycolipids, sphingolipids
Derived lipidsSteroids, terpenes, fat‑soluble vitamins

General structure of a triglyceride

Glycerol ($C_3H_8O_3$) esterified with three fatty acids gives the formula $C_{3}H_{5}(OCOR)_{3}$, where $R$ denotes the hydrocarbon chain (e.g., $C_{15}H_{31}$ for palmitic acid).

Biological importance

  • Long‑term energy reserve: $1\;g$ of fat yields $\approx9\;kcal$, about twice that of carbohydrates.
  • Major component of cell membranes – phospholipids form bilayers.
  • Precursors of hormones (steroids) and signaling molecules (eicosanoids).
  • Thermal insulation and mechanical protection.

Solved example

Calculate the molar mass of a triglyceride formed from glycerol and three molecules of palmitic acid ($C_{16}H_{32}O_{2}$).

  1. Write molecular formula: $C_{3}H_{5}(OCOC_{15}H_{31})_{3}$ → overall $C_{55}H_{98}O_{6}$.
  2. Molar mass = $55\times12.01 + 98\times1.008 + 6\times16.00$ g\,mol$^{-1}$ = $660.55 + 98.78 + 96.00 = 855.33\;g\;mol^{-1}$.

Thus the triglyceride has a molar mass of $8.55\times10^{2}\;g\;mol^{-1}$.
