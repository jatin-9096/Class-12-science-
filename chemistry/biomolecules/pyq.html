Case Study 1 – Electroplating of Automotive Parts

Passage: A leading automobile manufacturer in India has decided to improve the corrosion resistance of its brake‑disc components by electroplating a thin layer of chromium on the surface. The disc is made of cast iron (Fe) with a density of $7.86\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$ and a rectangular shape of dimensions $12\ \text{cm} \times 10\ \text{cm} \times 0.5\ \text{cm}$. The required thickness of the chromium coating is $2\ \mu\text{m}$. The plating bath contains $K_2Cr_2O_7$ as the source of $Cr^{6+}$ ions and the overall reduction reaction at the cathode can be represented as: $$\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}+14\text{H}^+ + 12e^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+}+7\text{H}_2\text{O}$$ The deposited chromium is obtained by the subsequent reaction: $$\text{Cr}^{3+}+3e^- \rightarrow \text{Cr(s)}$$ The current efficiency for chromium deposition is $85\%$ and the plating operation is carried out at a constant current of $25\ \text{A}$ for $3\ \text{h}$. The standard reduction potential for the $Cr^{3+}/Cr$ couple is $-0.74\ \text{V}$. The electrolyte temperature is maintained at $55^{\circ}\text{C}$, and the solution is stirred continuously to avoid concentration polarization.

  1. Calculate the mass of chromium that must be deposited to achieve the required coating thickness.
  2. Determine the theoretical time required to deposit this mass of chromium at the given current, assuming $100\%$ current efficiency.
  3. Considering the actual current efficiency of $85\%$, compute the actual time needed for the plating operation. Compare it with the given plating duration of $3\ \text{h}$ and comment on the feasibility.
  4. Calculate the energy consumption (in kWh) for the plating process, using the actual current and the operating voltage of $12\ \text{V}$ (including over‑potentials).
  5. Discuss two advantages and two disadvantages of using chromium electroplating for automotive brake discs.

Solution

  1. Mass of chromium required: The volume of the brake disc is: $$V = 12\ \text{cm} \times 10\ \text{cm} \times 0.5\ \text{cm}=60\ \text{cm}^3.$$ The surface area to be coated (both faces) is: $$A = 2 \times (12\ \text{cm} \times 10\ \text{cm}) = 240\ \text{cm}^2.$$ Required coating thickness $t = 2\ \mu\text{m}=2\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm}$. Hence the volume of chromium needed is: $$V_{\text{Cr}} = A \times t = 240\ \text{cm}^2 \times 2\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm}=0.048\ \text{cm}^3.$$ Density of chromium $\rho_{\text{Cr}} = 7.19\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$. $$m_{\text{Cr}} = \rho_{\text{Cr}} \times V_{\text{Cr}} = 7.19\ \text{g cm}^{-3}\times0.048\ \text{cm}^3 \approx 0.345\ \text{g}.$$
  2. Theoretical time (100 % efficiency): Molar mass of Cr, $M_{\text{Cr}} = 52.00\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$. Number of moles required: $$n_{\text{Cr}} = \frac{0.345\ \text{g}}{52.00\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 6.63\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}.$$ From the half‑reaction $\text{Cr}^{3+}+3e^- \rightarrow \text{Cr}$, $3$ Faradays are needed per mole of Cr. Total charge $Q$ needed: $$Q = n_{\text{Cr}} \times 3 \times F = 6.63\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\times3\times96485\ \text{C mol}^{-1} \approx 1.92\times10^{3}\ \text{C}.$$ With a constant current $I = 25\ \text{A}$, the theoretical time $t_{\text{th}}$ is: $$t_{\text{th}} = \frac{Q}{I}= \frac{1.92\times10^{3}\ \text{C}}{25\ \text{A}} = 76.8\ \text{s} \approx 1.28\ \text{min}.$$
  3. Actual time with $85\%$ efficiency: Effective current for deposition $I_{\text{eff}} = 0.85 \times 25\ \text{A}=21.25\ \text{A}.$ $$t_{\text{act}} = \frac{Q}{I_{\text{eff}}}= \frac{1.92\times10^{3}\ \text{C}}{21.25\ \text{A}} = 90.4\ \text{s} \approx 1.51\ \text{min}.$$ The planned plating duration is $3\ \text{h}=180\ \text{min}$, which is far larger than the calculated $1.5\ \text{min}$. The excess time is intentional to ensure uniform coverage, to compensate for edge effects, solution agitation, and possible variations in current density across the complex geometry of the disc. Therefore, the proposed duration is more than sufficient; in practice, the process is stopped once the desired thickness is confirmed by a non‑destructive thickness gauge.
  4. Energy consumption: Power $P = V \times I = 12\ \text{V} \times 25\ \text{A}=300\ \text{W}.$ Energy for the actual plating time ($3\ \text{h}=10\,800\ \text{s}$): $$E = P \times t = 300\ \text{W} \times 10\,800\ \text{s}=3.24\times10^{6}\ \text{J}.$$ Converting to kilowatt‑hours: $$E_{\text{kWh}} = \frac{3.24\times10^{6}\ \text{J}}{3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J kWh}^{-1}} \approx 0.90\ \text{kWh}.$$ Thus, each brake‑disc plating consumes roughly $0.9\ \text{kWh}$ of electrical energy.
  5. Advantages of chromium electroplating:
    • Corrosion resistance: Chromium forms a passive $Cr_2O_3$ layer that protects the underlying iron from atmospheric moisture and road salts.
    • Hardness and wear resistance: Cr coating (hardness $ \approx 900\ \text{HV}$) significantly reduces wear rate, extending the service life of brake discs under high‑temperature friction.
    Disadvantages:
    • Environmental and health hazards: Hexavalent chromium ($Cr^{6+}$) is carcinogenic; waste treatment and disposal require stringent regulations.
    • Cost and equipment complexity: Maintaining precise bath composition, temperature, and current density demands sophisticated control systems, raising capital and operating costs.
