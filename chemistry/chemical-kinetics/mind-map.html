Rate of reaction and its expression

Rate of Reaction – change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

Mathematically, $r = -\frac{1}{\nu_i}\frac{d[C_i]}{dt} = \frac{1}{\nu_j}\frac{d[C_j]}{dt}$ where $\nu$ are stoichiometric coefficients.

SI unit: $mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}$.

Rate Law

  • For an elementary step: $r = k\prod_i [A_i]^{\nu_i}$.
  • Overall order $n = \sum_i \nu_i$.
  • Zero, first and second order special cases are given below.
OrderRate LawIntegrated Form
Zero$r = k$$[A] = [A]_0 - kt$
First$r = k[A]$$\ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0} = -kt$
Second$r = k[A]^2$$\frac{1}{[A]} = \frac{1}{[A]_0} + kt$

Example: The decomposition of $N_2O_5$ follows first‑order kinetics. At $298\,K$, $[N_2O_5]_0 = 0.10\,M$ and after $30\,min$ the concentration becomes $0.040\,M$. Find $k$ and the half‑life.

For a first‑order reaction, $\ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0} = -kt$.

\[k = -\frac{1}{t}\ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}= -\frac{1}{1800\,s}\ln\frac{0.040}{0.10}= \frac{0.9163}{1800}=5.1\times10^{-4}\,s^{-1}\]

Half‑life $t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k}=\frac{0.693}{5.1\times10^{-4}}\approx1.36\times10^{3}\,s\approx22.7\,min$.

Order and molecularity of reaction

Order of Reaction – The overall order is the sum of the powers of concentration of each reactant in the rate law. For a reaction $aA + bB \rightarrow products$, rate law $r = k[A]^m[B]^n$; overall order $= m+n$ (dimensionless).

Molecularity – The number of molecules that collide in an elementary step. Unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular. Molecularity is always an integer and equals the reaction order for an elementary step.

  • Rate constant ($k$) – Proportionality constant in rate law. SI unit: $\mathrm{mol^{1-n}\,L^{n-1}\,s^{-1}}$ where $n$ is overall order.
  • Units of rate – $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.

Derivation of integrated rate law (first order)

StepExpression
1$\displaystyle \frac{d[A]}{dt} = -k[A]$
2$\displaystyle \frac{d[A]}{[A]} = -k\,dt$
3$\displaystyle \ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0} = -kt$
4$\displaystyle [A] = [A]_0 e^{-kt}$

Example

For the reaction $2A \rightarrow B$, the experimentally determined rate law is $r = k[A]^2$. Determine the half‑life ($t_{1/2}$) and calculate $k$ if $t_{1/2}= 30\ \text{s}$.

For a second‑order reaction, $t_{1/2}= \frac{1}{k[A]_0}$. Rearranging, $k = \frac{1}{t_{1/2}[A]_0}$. Assuming $[A]_0 = 0.020\ \text{mol\,L^{-1}}$,

$k = \frac{1}{30 \times 0.020}=1.67\ \text{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.

Thus the reaction is second order, molecularity is bimolecular, and the rate constant is $1.67\ \text{L\,mol^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$.

Rate law and determination of order of reaction

Definition: The rate law expresses the instantaneous rate of a reaction as a product of the rate constant $k$ and the concentrations of reactants raised to their respective orders.

General form:

Expression
$rate = k [A]^{m}[B]^{n}$
  • Rate unit: $mol\,L^{-1}s^{-1}$
  • Rate constant unit: $[k] = \text{(mol L}^{-1}\text{)}^{1-(m+n)} s^{-1}$
  • Overall order: $m+n$ (sum of individual orders)

Determination of order:

  • Method of initial rates – keep one reactant concentration constant and vary the other; compare rates.
  • Half‑life method – for first‑order, $t_{1/2}$ is constant; for second‑order, $t_{1/2}$ varies with $1/[A]_0$.

Example (initial‑rate method):

Experiment$[A]_0$ (M)$[B]_0$ (M)Initial rate (M s$^{-1}$)
10.100.102.0\times10^{-5}
20.200.108.0\times10^{-5}
30.200.201.6\times10^{-4}

From experiments 1→2, $[A]$ doubles, rate \times4 ⇒ $2^{m}=4$ ⇒ $m=2$. From 2→3, $[B]$ doubles, rate \times2 ⇒ $2^{n}=2$ ⇒ $n=1$. Hence rate law: $rate = k [A]^{2}[B]^{1}$. Using experiment 1, $k = \frac{2.0\times10^{-5}}{(0.10)^{2}(0.10)} = 2.0\,s^{-1}$.

Integrated rate equations and half‑life

Definition: Chemical kinetics studies the speed of a chemical reaction and the factors influencing it.

Rate law: For a single reactant $A$, $r = -\\frac{d[A]}{dt}=k[A]^n$, where $n$ is the order and $k$ (SI unit $\\text{s}^{-1}$ for $n=1$, $\\text{M}^{-1}\\text{s}^{-1}$ for $n=2$, etc.) is the rate constant.

Integrated rate equations

  • Zero order ($n=0$): $[A]=[A]_0-kt$; half‑life $t_{1/2}=\\frac{[A]_0}{2k}$.
  • First order ($n=1$): $\\ln\\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}=-kt$ or $\\log_{10}\\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}=-\\frac{k}{2.303}t$; half‑life $t_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln2}{k}=\\frac{0.693}{k}$ (independent of $[A]_0$).
  • Second order ($n=2$): $\\frac{1}{[A]}=\\frac{1}{[A]_0}+kt$; half‑life $t_{1/2}=\\frac{1}{k[A]_0}$.
OrderRate lawIntegrated formHalf‑life
0$r=k$$[A]=[A]_0-kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{[A]_0}{2k}$
1$r=k[A]$$\\ln\\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}=-kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{0.693}{k}$
2$r=k[A]^2$$\\frac{1}{[A]}=\\frac{1}{[A]_0}+kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{1}{k[A]_0}$

Example (first‑order): For $A\rightarrow$ products, $k=1.5\\times10^{-3}\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$. Find the time when $[A]=0.25[A]_0$.

Using $\\ln\\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}=-kt$:

$\\ln0.25=-1.5\\times10^{-3}\\,t$ \; $\\Rightarrow\\; t=\\frac{\\ln4}{1.5\\times10^{-3}}\\approx\\frac{1.386}{1.5\\times10^{-3}}\\approx9.24\\times10^{2}\\,\\text{s}$ (≈15.4 min).

Temperature dependence of rate constant – Arrhenius equation

Definition: The temperature dependence of the rate constant is expressed by the Arrhenius equation, which relates the rate constant $k$ to absolute temperature $T$.

Arrhenius equation (exponential form)

$$k = A\\,\\exp\\left(-\\frac{E_a}{RT}\\right)$$

  • $k$ – rate constant (SI unit: s$^{-1}$ for first order, m$^{3}$·mol$^{-1}$·s$^{-1}$ for second order, etc.)
  • $A$ – pre‑exponential (frequency) factor, same unit as $k$
  • $E_a$ – activation energy (J·mol$^{-1}$)
  • $R$ – universal gas constant $8.314\,\text{J·mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$
  • $T$ – absolute temperature (K)

Linear form (for graphical determination)

Taking natural logarithm,

$$\\ln k = \\ln A - \\frac{E_a}{R}\\,\\frac{1}{T}$$

A plot of $\\ln k$ versus $1/T$ yields a straight line with slope $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\\ln A$.

QuantitySymbolSI Unit
Rate constant$k$depends on order (s$^{-1}$, m$^{3}$·mol$^{-1}$·s$^{-1}$, …)
Activation energy$E_a$J·mol$^{-1}$
Gas constant$R$J·mol$^{-1}$·K$^{-1}$
Temperature$T$K

Example: For a first‑order reaction, $k_1=0.015\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $T_1=300\,\text{K}$ and $k_2=0.045\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $T_2=320\,\text{K}$. Calculate $E_a$.

Using

$$E_a = R\\,\\frac{\\ln(k_2/k_1)}{(1/T_1)-(1/T_2)}$$

$$\\ln(k_2/k_1)=\\ln 3=1.0986$$

$$\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}=\frac{1}{300}-\frac{1}{320}=0.000208\,\text{K}^{-1}$$

$$E_a = 8.314\times\frac{1.0986}{0.000208}=4.38\times10^{4}\,\text{J·mol}^{-1}\approx 44\,\text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$$

Thus the activation energy is about $44\,\text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$, and the pre‑exponential factor can be obtained from the intercept of the $\\ln k$ vs $1/T$ plot.

Collision theory and factors affecting reaction rate

Collision Theory states that a reaction occurs only when reactant particles collide with energy ≥ $E_a$ and proper orientation.

Key terms

  • Effective collision – a collision that leads to reaction.
  • Orientation factor ($\rho$) – fraction of collisions having correct orientation (0 < $\rho$ \le 1).
  • Collision frequency ($Z$) – number of collisions per unit volume per unit time.

Rate expression

$$rate = Z \rho e^{-E_a/RT}$$

Combining $Z\rho$ into a single constant $A$ gives the Arrhenius equation:

$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$

Collision frequency for bimolecular gas reactions

ParameterExpression
$Z_{AB}$$N_A \sigma_{AB} \sqrt{\dfrac{8RT}{\pi \mu_{AB}}}\,[A][B]$
$\sigma_{AB}$$\pi d_{AB}^2$ (collision cross‑section)
$\mu_{AB}$$\dfrac{m_A m_B}{m_A+m_B}$ (reduced mass)

Units

  • $k$: s$^{-1}$ (first order) or M$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ (second order)
  • $E_a$: J mol$^{-1}$
  • $A$: same as $k$
  • $Z$: mol L$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$

Example

Given $A = 2.5\times10^{12}$ s$^{-1}$, $E_a = 75$ kJ mol$^{-1}$, find $k$ at $T = 300$ K.

Solution:

  1. Convert $E_a$ \to J mol$^{-1}$: $E_a = 75\times10^{3}$ J mol$^{-1}$.
  2. Compute exponent: $-\dfrac{E_a}{RT}= -\dfrac{75\times10^{3}}{8.314\times300}= -30.1$.
  3. $k = 2.5\times10^{12}\, e^{-30.1}= 2.5\times10^{12}\times9.2\times10^{-14}= 2.3\times10^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$.

Thus $k \approx 0.23$ s$^{-1}$ at 300 K.

Catalysis – homogeneous and heterogeneous

Definition: A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed, providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy $E_a$.

Types of Catalysis

  • Homogeneous catalysis: Catalyst and reactants are in the same phase (usually liquid). Example: acid‑catalyzed esterification.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis: Catalyst is in a different phase (solid) from the reactants (gas or liquid). Reactions occur on the catalyst surface.

Key Features

  • Rate law for a catalyzed reaction: $r = k_{\text{cat}}[C]^m[\text{reactants}]^n$ where $[C]$ is catalyst concentration.
  • For heterogeneous catalysis, turnover frequency (TOF) $= \frac{\text{moles of product formed}}{\text{moles of active sites}\times \text{time}}$ (unit: s$^{-1}$).
  • Surface area effect: $k_{\text{cat}} \propto \text{surface area} \propto \frac{1}{\text{particle size}}$.

Derivation of Rate Enhancement

From Arrhenius equation $k = A\\exp\\left(-\\frac{E_a}{RT}\\right)$. With a catalyst, $E_a$ reduces to $E_a'$, giving $ \frac{k_{\text{cat}}}{k_{\text{uncat}}}= \exp\\left(\frac{E_a-E_a'}{RT}\right) $.

ParameterSymbolSI Unit
Activation energy$E_a$J·mol$^{-1}$
Rate constant$k$s$^{-1}$ (or appropriate order)
Turnover frequencyTOFs$^{-1}$

Solved Example

At $298\ \text{K}$, the uncatalyzed reaction has $E_a = 80\ \text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$ and $k_{\text{uncat}} = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. A solid catalyst lowers $E_a$ to $50\ \text{kJ·mol}^{-1}$. Find $k_{\text{cat}}$.

Solution: $ \frac{k_{\text{cat}}}{k_{\text{uncat}}}= \exp\\left(\frac{80-50}{R\cdot298}\right)$, $R=8.314\ \text{J·mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$. Compute $\Delta E =30\ \text{kJ}=3.0\times10^{4}\ \text{J}$. Exponent $= \frac{3.0\times10^{4}}{8.314\times298}=12.1$. Hence $k_{\text{cat}} = 2.0\times10^{-5}\times e^{12.1}\approx 2.0\times10^{-5}\times1.8\times10^{5}=3.6\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

Reaction mechanisms and complex reactions

Definition: A reaction mechanism is a step–wise description of elementary processes that together give the observed overall reaction.

Key concepts

  • Elementary step – single molecular event; its rate law is directly written from molecularity.
  • Molecularity – number of reactant molecules colliding in an elementary step (unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular).
  • Rate determining step (RDS) – slowest step; overall rate ≈ rate of RDS.
  • Steady–state approximation – concentration of reactive intermediate remains constant (d[I]/dt≈0).
  • Pre–equilibrium – fast reversible step followed by slow step; intermediate expressed via equilibrium constant $K = k_1/k_{-1}$.

General rate law

For an overall reaction of order $n$, $rate = k\,[A]^{\alpha}[B]^{\beta}\dots$ where $\alpha+\beta+\dots=n$.

Units of $k$ : $[k]=\mathrm{M^{1-n}\,s^{-1}}$ (SI: mol·L$^{-1}$·s$^{-1}$ for first order).

StepExpression
Fast equilibrium$K = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} = \frac{[I]}{[A][B]}$
Slow step$rate = k_2[I]$
Overall$rate = k_2K[A][B]$

Solved example

Mechanism: $NO_2+NO_2 \rightleftharpoons N_2O_4$ (fast, $k_1$, $k_{-1}$) ; $N_2O_4 \rightarrow 2NO+O_2$ (slow, $k_2$). Given $k_1=2.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}$, $k_{-1}=5.0\times10^2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $k_2=1.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $[NO_2]=0.01\ \mathrm{M}$.

Equilibrium constant $K = k_1/k_{-1}=4.0\ \mathrm{M^{-1}}$. Rate $=k_2K[NO_2]^2 = 1.0\times10^{-2}\times4.0\times(0.01)^2 = 4.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{M\,s^{-1}}$.
