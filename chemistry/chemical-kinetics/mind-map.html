1. Conceptual Framework

Chemistry · Core Law
Fundamental Definitions and Scope

Electrochemistry, a pivotal branch of physical chemistry, deals with the interconversion of chemical energy and electrical energy. The central entities are redox (reduction‑oxidation) reactions, wherein electrons are transferred from a reducing agent (oxidised) to an oxidising agent (reduced). An electrochemical cell harnesses this electron flow to generate a measurable electromotive force (EMF). Two broad categories of cells are recognised: galvanic (voltaic) cells, which operate spontaneously to deliver electrical work, and electrolytic cells, which require an external source of electricity to drive non‑spontaneous reactions. The standard electrode potential, $E^\circ$, is a thermodynamic quantity that reflects the tendency of a half‑reaction to occur under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 25 °C). The sign and magnitude of $E^\circ$ provide a direct measure of the driving force for electron transfer. Understanding these definitions is essential before delving into quantitative relationships such as the Nernst equation and the calculation of cell potentials.

Chemistry · Core Law
Thermodynamic Foundations: Gibbs Free Energy and Cell Potential

The link between thermodynamics and electrochemistry is encapsulated in the equation $\\Delta G = -nFE_{\text{cell}}$, where $\\Delta G$ is the change in Gibbs free energy, $n$ denotes the number of moles of electrons transferred, $F$ is the Faraday constant (approximately $96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$), and $E_{\text{cell}}$ is the cell potential under the given conditions. A negative $\\Delta G$ corresponds to a positive $E_{\text{cell}}$, indicating a spontaneous reaction as observed in galvanic cells. Conversely, a negative $E_{\text{cell}}$ signifies a non‑spontaneous process, characteristic of electrolytic cells where external voltage must be applied. The standard Gibbs free energy change, $\\Delta G^\circ$, relates to the standard cell potential $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ via $\\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}$. This relationship allows chemists to predict the feasibility of redox processes and to calculate equilibrium constants through $\\Delta G^\circ = -RT\\ln K$, linking electrochemical data to chemical equilibria.

Chemistry · Core Law
The Nernst Equation: Quantitative Prediction of Cell Potentials

While $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ provides the potential under standard conditions, real‑world scenarios often involve non‑standard concentrations, pressures, or temperatures. The Nernst equation bridges this gap: $$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \\frac{RT}{nF}\\ln Q,$$ where $R$ is the universal gas constant (8.314 J mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$), $T$ is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and $Q$ is the reaction quotient reflecting the instantaneous activities of reactants and products. At 25 °C (298 K), the equation simplifies to $$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \\frac{0.0592\ \text{V}}{n}\\log Q.$$ This form is extensively used in laboratory calculations. The Nernst equation not only predicts how cell potential varies with concentration but also underpins the operation of pH meters, ion‑selective electrodes, and corrosion rate estimations. Mastery of this equation enables students to analyse complex redox systems, such as mixed‑potential corrosion or biological redox chains.

Chemistry · Core Law
Electrode Types, Conventions, and the Cell Diagram

Electrodes are the physical sites where oxidation and reduction occur. The anode is the electrode at which oxidation takes place, releasing electrons into the external circuit; the cathode is where reduction occurs, accepting electrons. In a galvanic cell, the anode is negative, while the cathode is positive; the polarity reverses in an electrolytic cell. The cell diagram conventionally lists the components from left to right: anode compartment | anode solution (including any salt bridge) || cathode solution | cathode compartment. For example, the Daniell cell is represented as $$\\text{Zn(s)}|\\text{Zn}^{2+}(1\ \text{M})||\\text{Cu}^{2+}(1\ \text{M})|\\text{Cu(s)}.$$ The double vertical line denotes the salt bridge or porous membrane that maintains electrical neutrality. Understanding the diagrammatic representation aids in visualising ion migration, electron flow, and the overall cell operation. Moreover, the choice of electrode material (e.g., inert platinum versus reactive metal) influences overpotential and kinetic factors, which are crucial when interpreting real‑world cell performance.

Chemistry · Core Law
Worked Example: Calculating the EMF of a Cu–Fe Cell at Non‑Standard Conditions

Consider a galvanic cell comprising a copper electrode immersed in a $0.10\ \text{M}$ $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ solution and an iron electrode in a $0.50\ \text{M}$ $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ solution at $30^\circ\text{C}$. The half‑reactions and their standard potentials are: $$\\text{Cu}^{2+}+2e^- \\rightarrow \\text{Cu(s)}\quad E^\circ = +0.34\ \text{V},$$ $$\\text{Fe}^{2+}+2e^- \\rightarrow \\text{Fe(s)}\quad E^\circ = -0.44\ \text{V}.$$ The cell operates spontaneously with copper acting as the cathode (higher $E^\circ$) and iron as the anode. The standard cell potential is $$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = 0.34 - (-0.44) = 0.78\ \text{V}.$$ At $30^\circ\text{C}$ (303 K), the Nernst equation becomes $$E_{\text{cell}} = 0.78 - \\frac{RT}{nF}\\ln\\frac{[\\text{Cu}^{2+}]}{[\\text{Fe}^{2+}]}.$$ Substituting $R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $F = 96\,485\ \text{C mol}^{-1}$, $n = 2$, and the concentrations yields $$E_{\text{cell}} = 0.78 - \\frac{(8.314)(303)}{(2)(96\,485)}\\ln\\frac{0.10}{0.50}.$$ Calculating the term: $$\\frac{RT}{nF} = \\frac{(8.314)(303)}{(2)(96\,485)} \\approx 0.0131\ \text{V},$$ and $$\\ln\\frac{0.10}{0.50} = \\ln(0.2) \\approx -1.609.$$ Thus $$E_{\text{cell}} = 0.78 - (0.0131)(-1.609) = 0.78 + 0.0211 = 0.801\ \text{V}.$$ The cell EMF at $30^\circ\text{C}$ and the given concentrations is therefore $0.801\ \text{V}$. This example illustrates the practical use of the Nernst equation, the importance of temperature correction, and the influence of ion activities on cell potential.

1. First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is a statement of the conservation of energy for a closed system. It can be written in integral form as

$$\Delta U = Q - W,$$

where $\Delta U$ is the change in internal energy, $Q$ is the heat supplied to the system, and $W$ is the work done by the system on its surroundings. For a differential change, we write

$$dU = \delta Q - \delta W.$$

In a quasi‑static (reversible) process, the work done by the system during an infinitesimal volume change $dV$ is given by

$$\delta W_{\text{rev}} = P\,dV,$$

where $P$ is the pressure of the system. Substituting this into the differential form of the first law, we obtain

$$dU = \delta Q_{\text{rev}} - P\,dV.$$

For a reversible process, the heat transfer is related to the change in entropy $S$ by the second law:

$$\delta Q_{\text{rev}} = T\,dS,$$

where $T$ is the absolute temperature. Substituting this into the previous equation gives the fundamental thermodynamic identity:

$$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV.$$

This identity is the cornerstone for deriving all other thermodynamic potentials and relations. It shows that the internal energy is a function of two independent variables, typically taken as entropy and volume, i.e., $U = U(S,V)$.

2. Entropy and the Second Law

The second law of thermodynamics states that for any spontaneous process in an isolated system, the total entropy increases. For a reversible process, the change in entropy of the system is equal to the heat exchanged divided by the temperature:

$$dS = \frac{\delta Q_{\text{rev}}}{T}.$$

Combining this with the first law, we can express the differential of internal energy solely in terms of entropy and volume:

$$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV.$$

For an irreversible process, the inequality holds:

$$dS > \frac{\delta Q}{T}.$$

In practice, we often consider the entropy change of a system and its surroundings to evaluate the spontaneity of a process.

3. Fundamental Thermodynamic Potentials

By performing Legendre transforms on the internal energy, we obtain four thermodynamic potentials that are convenient for different sets of natural variables.

3.1 Enthalpy $H$

Enthalpy is defined as

$$H = U + PV.$$

Taking the differential:

$$dH = dU + P\,dV + V\,dP.$$

Substituting the identity for $dU$ gives

$$dH = (T\,dS - P\,dV) + P\,dV + V\,dP = T\,dS + V\,dP.$$

Thus, the natural variables for enthalpy are entropy and pressure: $H = H(S,P)$.

3.2 Helmholtz Free Energy $A$

Helmholtz free energy is defined as

$$A = U - TS.$$

Its differential is

$$dA = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT.$$

Substituting $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$ yields

$$dA = (T\,dS - P\,dV) - T\,dS - S\,dT = -P\,dV - S\,dT.$$

Hence, the natural variables for $A$ are temperature and volume: $A = A(T,V)$.

3.3 Gibbs Free Energy $G$

Gibbs free energy is defined as

$$G = H - TS = U + PV - TS.$$

Taking the differential:

$$dG = dH - T\,dS - S\,dT.$$

Using $dH = T\,dS + V\,dP$ we get

$$dG = (T\,dS + V\,dP) - T\,dS - S\,dT = V\,dP - S\,dT.$$

Thus, the natural variables for Gibbs free energy are temperature and pressure: $G = G(T,P)$.

3.4 Chemical Potential $\mu$

For a system with multiple components, the internal energy differential becomes

$$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \sum_i \mu_i\,dn_i,$$

where $n_i$ is the number of moles of component $i$ and $\mu_i$ is its chemical potential. This expression is fundamental for deriving the Gibbs–Duhem relation and for understanding phase equilibria.

4. Maxwell Relations

Maxwell relations are obtained by equating mixed second derivatives of the thermodynamic potentials. For example, from the differential of Helmholtz free energy $dA = -P\,dV - S\,dT$, we have

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.$$

Similarly, from $dG = V\,dP - S\,dT$ we obtain

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P.$$
