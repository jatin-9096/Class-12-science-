Introduction to chemical kinetics

Chemical Kinetics – Introduction

Chemical kinetics studies the speed at which reactants are converted to products and the factors influencing this speed.

Key Definitions

  • Rate of reaction = change in concentration per unit time, $r = -\\frac{1}{\\nu_i}\\frac{d[A_i]}{dt}$.
  • Rate law expresses $r$ in terms of concentrations of reactants: $r = k\\, [A]^m [B]^n$.
  • Order of reaction = sum of exponents $m+n$; determines the dependence on concentration.
  • Rate constant ($k$) – proportionality factor; its SI unit varies: $\\text{s}^{-1}$ for first order, $\\text{M}^{-1}\\text{s}^{-1}$ for second order.
  • Half‑life ($t_{1/2}$) – time for concentration to fall to half its initial value.

Important Integrated Forms

OrderIntegrated Rate LawHalf‑life
Zero$[A] = [A]_0 - kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{[A]_0}{2k}$
First$\\ln\\frac{[A]_0}{[A]} = kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{k}$
Second$\\frac{1}{[A]} - \\frac{1}{[A]_0}=kt$$t_{1/2}=\\frac{1}{k[A]_0}$

Sample Problem (First‑order decomposition)

For the reaction $N_2O_5 \rightarrow 2NO_2 + \\frac{1}{2}O_2$, $[N_2O_5]$ falls from $0.10\\,\\text{M}$ to $0.025\\,\\text{M}$ in $30\\,\\text{min}$. Find $k$ and $t_{1/2}$.

Solution:

Using the first‑order law $\\ln\\frac{[A]_0}{[A]} = kt$:

$k = \\frac{1}{30}\\,\\ln\\frac{0.10}{0.025}=\\frac{1}{30}\\,\\ln 4 = \\frac{\\ln 4}{30}\\,\\text{min}^{-1}=0.0462\\,\\text{min}^{-1}$.

Half‑life $t_{1/2}=\\frac{\\ln 2}{k}=\\frac{0.693}{0.0462}=15\\,\\text{min}$.

Thus $k=0.046\\,\\text{min}^{-1}$ and $t_{1/2}=15\\,\\text{min}$.

Rate of reaction and rate law

Rate of Reaction – Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

Mathematically, $r = -\\frac{1}{\\nu_i}\\frac{d[\\,i\\,]}{dt}$ where $\\nu_i$ is the stoichiometric coefficient.

Rate Law – Empirical expression relating $r$ to concentrations of reactants.

ExpressionMeaning
$r = k[\\,A\\,]^m[\\,B\\,]^n$Rate = rate constant $k$ times concentration powers
  • Order of reaction: $m+n$ (overall), $m$ (w.r.t A), $n$ (w.r.t B).
  • Units of $k$ depend on overall order: for overall order $p$, $[k]=\\text{mol}^{1-p}\\,\\text{L}^{p-1}\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$.

Derivation (method of initial rates) – From experiments keeping other concentrations constant, compare rates to obtain exponents.

Example

For $2A \\rightarrow B$, initial rates are measured:

Experiment$[A]_0$ (mol L$^{-1}$)Initial rate (mol L$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$)
10.102.0\\times10^{-5}
20.208.0\\times10^{-5}

Rate doubles when $[A]$ is doubled → $m=1$. Hence rate law $r = k[\\,A\\,]$.

Using experiment 1: $k = \\frac{2.0\\times10^{-5}}{0.10}=2.0\\times10^{-4}\\,\\text{s}^{-1}$.

Thus, $r = 2.0\\times10^{-4}[\\,A\\,]$ (SI unit $\\text{mol L}^{-1}\\text{s}^{-1}$).

Order and molecularity of reaction

Definition: The order of a reaction is the exponent to which the concentration of a reactant appears in the rate law; it is determined experimentally, not from the stoichiometric equation.

General rate law for a single‑step reaction is
$\text{rate}= -\frac{d[A]}{dt}=k[A]^n$, where $n$ is the overall order and $k$ is the rate constant.

SI unit of $k$ : $\text{M}^{1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ (M = mol L$^{-1}$).

  • Zero order ($n=0$): $\text{rate}=k$; integrated form $[A]=[A]_0-kt$.
  • First order ($n=1$): $\text{rate}=k[A]$; integrated form $\ln\frac{[A]}{[A]_0}=-kt$ or $t=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}$.
  • Second order ($n=2$): $\text{rate}=k[A]^2$; integrated form $\frac{1}{[A]}-\frac{1}{[A]_0}=kt$.

Molecularity refers to the number of reactant molecules that collide in an elementary step. It is always an integer (unimolecular, bimolecular, termolecular) and equals the reaction order only for elementary reactions.

Reaction orderMolecularity (elementary step)
$0$Not applicable (rate independent of concentration)
$1$Unimolecular
$2$Bimolecular
$3$Termolecular (rare)

Solved example (first‑order)

For $A\rightarrow\text{products}$, $[A]_0=0.10\,\text{M}$, $k=2.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Find the time $t$ when $[A]=0.025\,\text{M}$.

Using $t=\frac{1}{k}\ln\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}$:
$ t = \frac{1}{2.0\times10^{-3}}\ln\frac{0.10}{0.025}$
$ = 5.0\times10^{2}\,\text{s}\times\ln4$
$ \approx 5.0\times10^{2}\times1.386 = 6.93\times10^{2}\,\text{s}$
$\boxed{t\approx 6.9\times10^{2}\,\text{s}}$ (≈ 11.5 min).

Determination of order of reaction

Definition: The order of reaction with respect to a reactant is the exponent to which its concentration term is raised in the rate law. The overall order is the sum of individual orders.

General rate law

$rate = k [A]^m [B]^n$

Key relations

  • Overall order $= m+n$.
  • From two experiments (changing only $[A]$): $\frac{rate_1}{rate_2}=\left(\frac{[A]_1}{[A]_2}\right)^m$.
  • Hence $m=\frac{\log(rate_1/rate_2)}{\log([A]_1/[A]_2)}$.
  • Units of $k$: $\text{mol}^{1-n}\,\text{L}^{n-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ where $n$ is the overall order.

Methods to determine order

  • Initial‑rates method: Use the above ratio for each reactant.
  • Integrated‑rate method: Plot $\ln[ A ]$ vs $t$ (first order) or $1/[ A ]$ vs $t$ (second order) and check linearity.
  • Half‑life method: For first order $t_{1/2}$ is constant; for second order $t_{1/2}\propto 1/[A]_0$.

SI units

  • Rate: $\text{mol\,L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$.
  • Concentration: $\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$.
  • Rate constant $k$: depends on overall order (see above).

Solved example (determine order for $2A\rightarrow products$)

Experiment$[A]$ (M)Initial rate (M s$^{-1}$)
10.102.0\times10^{-5}
20.208.0\times10^{-5}

Using the ratio method:

$\frac{rate_1}{rate_2}=\frac{2.0\times10^{-5}}{8.0\times10^{-5}}=0.25$

$\frac{[A]_1}{[A]_2}=\frac{0.10}{0.20}=0.5$

Thus $m=\frac{\log0.25}{\log0.5}=2$, indicating a second‑order reaction in $A$ (overall order $=2$).

Calculate $k$ from experiment 1:

$k=\frac{rate}{[A]^2}=\frac{2.0\times10^{-5}}{(0.10)^2}=2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$

Hence the rate law is $rate = 2.0\times10^{-3}\,[A]^2$ with $k$ units $\text{M}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$.

Integrated rate equations and half‑life

Integrated Rate Equations

  • Zero‑order reaction ($A\to products$): Rate $= -\frac{d[A]}{dt}=k$. Integration gives $[A]=[A]_0-kt$.
  • First‑order reaction: $-\frac{d[A]}{dt}=k[A]$. Integration gives $\ln[A]=\ln[A]_0-kt$ or $[A]=[A]_0e^{-kt}$.
  • Second‑order reaction (both reactants same): $-\frac{d[A]}{dt}=k[A]^2$. Integration gives $\frac{1}{[A]}=\frac{1}{[A]_0}+kt$.

Half‑life ($t_{1/2}$)

  • Zero order: $t_{1/2}=\frac{[A]_0}{2k}$ (units s, $k$ in $\text{mol\,L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$).
  • First order: $t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k}=\frac{0.693}{k}$ (units s, $k$ in $\text{s}^{-1}$).
  • Second order: $t_{1/2}=\frac{1}{k[A]_0}$ (units s·L·mol$^{-1}$, $k$ in $\text{L\,mol}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$).
OrderIntegrated formHalf‑life expression
Zero$[A]=[A]_0-kt$$t_{1/2}=\frac{[A]_0}{2k}$
First$[A]=[A]_0e^{-kt}$$t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k}$
Second$\frac{1}{[A]}=\frac{1}{[A]_0}+kt$$t_{1/2}=\frac{1}{k[A]_0}$

Example

A first‑order decomposition has $k=2.5\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$. Find $t_{1/2}$ and the concentration after 10 min if $[A]_0=0.80\,\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$.

Solution:

  • $t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{2.5\times10^{-3}}=2.77\times10^{2}\,\text{s}=4.62\,\text{min}$.
  • Using $[A]=[A]_0e^{-kt}$, $t=10\,\text{min}=600\,\text{s}$, $[A]=0.80\,e^{-2.5\times10^{-3}\times600}=0.80\,e^{-1.5}=0.80\times0.223=0.179\,\text{mol\,L}^{-1}$.

Key points: half‑life of a first‑order reaction is independent of $[A]_0$, whereas for zero‑ and second‑order it varies with initial concentration.

Temperature dependence of rate constant (Arrhenius equation)

Arrhenius Equation – The temperature dependence of the rate constant $k$ is given by $k = A\\,e^{-E_a/(RT)}$ where $k$ is the rate constant, $A$ the frequency factor, $E_a$ the activation energy, $R$ the gas constant (8.314 J mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$) and $T$ the absolute temperature (K).

Linear form – Taking natural logarithm, $$\ln k = \ln A - \\frac{E_a}{R}\\,\\frac{1}{T}$$. A plot of $\ln k$ versus $1/T$ yields a straight line with slope $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$.

Units

VariableSymbolUnitMeaning
Rate constant$k$depends on order (e.g., s$^{-1}$ for first order)Speed of reaction
Frequency factor$A$s$^{-1}$ (first order)Pre‑exponential factor
Activation energy$E_a$kJ mol$^{-1}$Energy barrier
Gas constant$R$J mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$Universal constant
Temperature$T$KAbsolute temperature

Solved example

Given: $k_1 = 1.5\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $T_1 = 300\,\text{K}$ and $k_2 = 6.0\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1}$ at $T_2 = 320\,\text{K}$. Find $E_a$.

  • Use $$\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1}= -\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$
  • $\ln(k_2/k_1)=\ln(6.0/1.5)=\ln4=1.386$
  • $\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}=\frac{1}{320}-\frac{1}{300}= -2.083\times10^{-4}\,\text{K}^{-1}$
  • Rearrange: $E_a = -R\,\frac{\ln(k_2/k_1)}{(1/T_2-1/T_1)}$
  • Substitute: $E_a = -8.314\times\frac{1.386}{-2.083\times10^{-4}} \approx 5.53\times10^{4}\,\text{J mol}^{-1}=55.3\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$

Thus the activation energy is $55.3\,\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ and the Arrhenius plot would have slope $-E_a/R$.

Catalysis and reaction mechanism

Definition of Catalysis: A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed, providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy $E_a$.

Types of Catalysts

TypePhaseExample
HomogeneousSame phase as reactantsAcid‑catalyzed esterification
HeterogeneousDifferent phasePt surface in hydrogenation
EnzymeBiological macromoleculeAmylase

Reaction Mechanism

  • Consists of a series of elementary steps.
  • Each step follows its own rate law; the slowest step is the rate‑determining step (RDS).
  • Overall rate law is derived from the RDS and any pre‑equilibria.

Key Derivation (Two‑step Catalytic Pathway)

Consider: $A + C \rightleftharpoons AC \xrightarrow{k_2} P + C$

Assuming rapid pre‑equilibrium for the first step, $K = \frac{k_1}{k_{-1}} = \frac{[AC]}{[A][C]}$.

Overall observed rate constant:

$k_{\text{obs}} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_{-1}+k_2}$

Units: $k_{\text{obs}}$ – $s^{-1}$ (first order), $E_a$ – $kJ\,mol^{-1}$.

Effect on Activation Energy

Arrhenius equation: $k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$; a catalyst lowers $E_a$, increasing $k$ at a given $T$.

Solved Example

Uncatalyzed reaction: $k = 2.0\times10^{-3}\,s^{-1}$. Catalytic cycle parameters: $k_1 = 5.0\times10^{2}\,M^{-1}s^{-1}$, $k_{-1}=1.0\times10^{2}\,s^{-1}$, $k_2=1.0\times10^{3}\,s^{-1}$.

Calculate $k_{\text{obs}}$:

$k_{\text{obs}} = \frac{5.0\times10^{2}\times1.0\times10^{3}}{1.0\times10^{2}+1.0\times10^{3}} = \frac{5.0\times10^{5}}{1.1\times10^{3}} \approx 4.55\times10^{2}\,s^{-1}$.

Half‑life for first‑order decay:

$t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{k_{\text{obs}}} \approx \frac{0.693}{4.55\times10^{2}} \approx 1.5\times10^{-3}\,s$.

This illustrates how a catalyst dramatically accelerates the reaction by providing a fast pathway with a much larger $k_{\text{obs}}$ and a correspondingly short half‑life.

Collision theory and factors affecting rate

Collision Theory states that a reaction occurs when reactant molecules collide with (i) energy ≥ activation energy ($E_a$) and (ii) proper orientation.

Key Equation

ExpressionMeaning
$k = Z_{AB}\,p\,e^{-E_a/RT}$Rate constant in terms of collision frequency ($Z_{AB}$), steric factor ($p$) and Arrhenius factor.

Collision Frequency ($Z_{AB}$)

$Z_{AB}=N_A\,N_B\,\sigma_{AB}\sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi \mu}}$ where $N_i$ are number concentrations (mol L$^{-1}$), $\sigma_{AB}$ is collision cross‑section (m$^{2}$) and $\mu$ reduced mass (kg).

  • Concentration: $Z_{AB}\propto [A][B]$ – doubling a reactant doubles the rate for elementary bimolecular steps.
  • Temperature: Appears in $Z_{AB}$ (∝$\sqrt{T}$) and in $e^{-E_a/RT}$ – a 10 °C rise roughly doubles $k$ (rule of thumb).
  • Orientation (steric factor $p$): $0
  • Catalyst: Provides an alternative pathway with lower $E_a$, increasing $e^{-E_a/RT}$.
  • Pressure (gases): Increases $[A]$ and $[B]$, thus $Z_{AB}$.

SI Units

  • $k$ – $s^{-1}$ (first order), $M^{-1}s^{-1}$ (second order).
  • $E_a$ – $kJ\,mol^{-1}$.

Example

For a reaction with $E_a=75\;kJ\,mol^{-1}$ at $T=300\;K$, calculate the \frac{t}ion $f$ of molecules having energy ≥ $E_a$.

$f = e^{-E_a/RT}=e^{-\frac{75\times10^{3}}{8.314\times300}}=e^{-30.1}\approx9.2\times10^{-14}$.

Thus only an extremely small fraction collides effectively; a catalyst or temperature rise is essential.
